# **Structural Divergence and Labor Market Dualism: A Comprehensive Analysis of ISM Manufacturing and Services Employment Indices (2025-2026)**

## **Executive Summary: The Split Verdict Economy**

The economic landscape of the United States at the transition from 2025 to 2026 presents a complex tableau of divergence, characterized by a distinct decoupling between the manufacturing and services sectors. This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level analysis of the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) Employment Indices for January 2026 and December 2025, synthesizing data to answer the critical question of whether the U.S. economy is witnessing a synchronized contraction or a sector-specific structural adjustment.

The analysis unequivocally points to a **split labor market**. The manufacturing sector, despite a headline recovery in output, remains entrenched in a historic employment contraction, marking its 28th consecutive month of shedding labor.1 In stark contrast, the services sector has demonstrated resilience, returning to expansion in December 2025 despite significant headwinds in specific technology and logistics verticals.3

This "jobless recovery" in manufacturing, juxtaposed with a "strategic stabilization" in services, suggests that the U.S. economy is not in a recessionary freefall but rather undergoing a profound structural rotation. Manufacturers are leveraging automation and attrition to manage costs amidst tariff uncertainty, while service providers are recalibrating workforce levels to align with resilient, albeit moderating, consumer demand.

**1\. Methodological Framework and Economic Significance**

To accurately interpret the divergence between manufacturing and services employment, it is essential to first establish the methodological underpinnings of the ISM Report on Business. These indices are not merely raw counts of jobs added or lost; they are sophisticated diffusion indices that measure the *breadth* of economic change, providing a leading indicator for broader labor market trends.

### **1.1 The Mechanics of the Diffusion Index**

The ISM Employment Index is calculated based on the percentage of respondents reporting expanding, contracting, or stagnant employment levels compared to the previous month. A reading above 50 percent indicates that the variable is generally expanding; a reading below 50 percent indicates generally declining activity. A reading of 50 percent indicates no change.5

It is critical to note that the index measures the direction and diffusion of change, not the magnitude. A reading of 48.1% does not imply a 1.9% reduction in total payrolls, but rather that a greater proportion of firms are reducing headcount than increasing it. This distinction is vital when analyzing the January 2026 manufacturing data, where a significant surge in output did not translate into broad-based hiring.7

### **1.2 Seasonal Adjustment and Statistical Noise**

The data analyzed in this report reflects the most recent annual adjustments to seasonal factors released by the ISM.7 Seasonal adjustments are designed to smooth out repetitive intra-year variations, such as post-holiday layoffs in retail or summer hiring spikes in construction, allowing for a clearer view of the underlying economic trend.

For January 2026, the seasonal factors played a significant role. The raw data often shows a drop in activity following the holiday season, but the seasonally adjusted figures reveal whether that drop was better or worse than historical norms. The "head-scratching" nature of the January 2026 Manufacturing report, which saw a leap into expansion territory despite negative sentiment, underscores the importance of these adjustments in interpreting the signal amidst the noise.9

### **1.3 The Predictive Power of the Employment Index**

Historically, the ISM Employment indices have served as reliable leading indicators for the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Non-Farm Payroll reports.10 A persistent contraction in the ISM Manufacturing Employment Index often presages weakness in the goods-producing component of federal jobs data. However, the relationship is not always linear. In the current cycle, the "hoarding" of labor in services and the "shedding" of labor in manufacturing have created a disconnect that challenges traditional predictive models.

**2\. Manufacturing Employment: The "Jobless" Expansion**

The manufacturing sector in January 2026 presents a paradox: output is surging, yet employment continues to shrink. This divergence is the defining characteristic of the current industrial cycle.

### **2.1 January 2026: A Headline Recovery with Hollow Employment**

In a development that surprised many analysts, the **ISM Manufacturing PMI** for January 2026 rose to **52.6%**, moving into expansion territory for the first time in 12 months.5 This surge was driven by a sharp rebound in **New Orders**, which jumped to **57.1%** 5, and **Production**, which rose to **55.9%**.7

However, this buoyancy did not extend to the labor market. The **Manufacturing Employment Index** for January 2026 registered **48.1%**.5 While this represents a notable improvement of 3.3 percentage points from the December 2025 reading of 44.8%, it remains firmly in contraction territory.

#### **2.1.1 The 28-Month Contraction Streak**

The persistent weakness in manufacturing employment cannot be overstated. As of January 2026, the sector has experienced **28 consecutive months of contraction**.1 Furthermore, employment has contracted in **36 of the last 37 months**.2

This is not a cyclical dip; it is a structural entrenchment. The data suggests that manufacturers have fundamentally altered their approach to labor, prioritizing efficiency gains and attrition over headcount expansion. The fact that output (Production Index 55.9%) can expand significantly while employment (48.1%) contracts indicates a surge in labor productivity—producing more goods with fewer workers.

### **2.2 December 2025: The Depth of the Trough**

To fully appreciate the January figures, one must look at the baseline established in late 2025\. In December 2025, the Manufacturing Employment Index stood at **44.9%** (seasonally adjusted) 12, a reading indicative of significant labor shedding.

The December report painted a bleak picture. While the overall economy continued to expand, the manufacturing sector was contracting for the tenth consecutive month.13 The employment sub-index was weighed down by a cautious approach from management, with 63 percent of panelists indicating that "managing headcounts" was the norm rather than hiring.14

### **2.3 Qualitative Analysis: The "Hope" and the Fear**

The raw numbers are illuminated by the commentary from supply management executives. The sentiment in the sector is dominated by a mix of fatigue, tariff anxiety, and cautious optimism that has yet to materialize into action.

#### **2.3.1 "Hope Has Been the Word of the Year"**

A respondent in the Transportation Equipment industry poignantly noted, "'Hope' has been the word of the year... Unfortunately, all the hope in the world has not materialized into order activity in 2025 or the first half of 2026".15 This statement encapsulates the psychological toll of the prolonged downturn. Executives have been anticipating a turnaround that keeps getting pushed to "the second half of 2026," leading to a paralysis in hiring decisions. "It's hard to set strategy on hope," the respondent added.15

#### **2.3.2 The Tariff Overhang**

A major impediment to hiring is the uncertainty surrounding trade policy. Susan Spence, Chair of the ISM Manufacturing Business Survey Committee, noted that the sustainability of the January bounce "will depend heavily on how companies have structured their supply chains" and their exposure to "tariff uncertainty".9

Respondents expressed significant concern regarding potential new tariffs, particularly on European Union goods.15 One executive in the Machinery sector stated, "We will not be able to recover the increase tariffs in our current quotations".15 This margin compression forces companies to cut costs elsewhere, with labor often being the most flexible variable.

#### **2.3.3 Attrition as a Strategy**

The prevailing strategy for reducing labor costs is attrition rather than mass layoffs. Panelists indicated that for every comment regarding hiring, there were two comments regarding reducing headcounts.16 By leaving vacant positions unfilled, firms can lower their fixed costs without incurring the severance expenses or morale damage associated with active layoffs. This "passive contraction" explains why the employment index remains in the high 40s rather than plummeting to the 30s.

### **2.4 Sector-Specific Performance**

The monolithic "manufacturing" label hides significant variance among specific industries.

* **Growth Leaders:** Only two of the eighteen manufacturing industries reported employment growth in January 2026:  
  * **Transportation Equipment**  
  * **Computer & Electronic Products**.2  
  * *Analysis:* These sectors are benefiting from secular tailwinds. Computer & Electronic Products are driven by the AI investment cycle and data center expansion.17 Transportation Equipment, despite the "hope" commentary, is seeing some stability likely due to aerospace demand.  
* **Lagging Sectors:** Eleven industries reported employment declines, including:  
  * **Textile Mills**  
  * **Wood Products**  
  * **Petroleum & Coal Products**  
  * **Apparel, Leather & Allied Products**.2  
  * *Analysis:* These industries are highly sensitive to raw material prices and discretionary consumer spending. The contraction in wood products, for example, correlates with the broader challenges in the housing market mentioned in historical comparisons.19

**3\. Services Employment: The Resilience Engine**

While manufacturing struggles to convert orders into jobs, the services sector remains the primary engine of employment stability in the U.S. economy. The data from December 2025 confirms that the "Service Economy" is alive and expanding, albeit with new strategic nuances.

### **3.1 December 2025: A Return to Expansion**

In **December 2025**, the **ISM Services Employment Index** registered **52.0%**.3 This reading marked a decisive return to expansion following a contractionary dip to 48.9% in November 2025\.20

* **Magnitude of Change:** The index rose by **3.1 percentage points**, a statistically significant move that underscores the sector's resilience.  
* **Breadth:** The employment expansion was supported by broad strength in the Services PMI, which rose to 54.4%.3  
* **Drivers:** Respondents frequently mentioned "activity increases related to end-of-year activities" and preparation for 2026 as key drivers for hiring.21

### **3.2 Forecasting January 2026**

Although the official ISM Services Report for January 2026 was not released at the time of this analysis (scheduled for February 4, 2026\) 22, available leading indicators point to continued stability.

* **Consensus Expectations:** Market forecasts suggest the Services PMI will remain in expansion, with employment likely stabilizing near the 52.0% level.22  
* **Job Market Signals:** The January 2026 jobs report noted "steady, modest job growth" with unemployment dipping to 4.4%.23 This macro-level data strongly implies that the Services Employment Index did not collapse in January, maintaining its divergence from manufacturing.

### **3.3 The "Low-Hire" Environment and High-Profile Layoffs**

A paradox exists within the services data: the index is expanding (52.0%), yet headlines are dominated by massive layoffs in major service-oriented conglomerates.

#### **3.3.1 The Tech and Logistics Purge**

January 2026 saw significant layoff announcements that seem to contradict the expansionary ISM data:

* **UPS:** Announced **30,000 job cuts** in January 2026\.24  
* **Amazon:** Announced **16,000 corporate role eliminations**.26  
* **Citigroup:** Continuing a restructuring plan to cut 20,000 roles.25

#### **3.3.2 Reconciling the Data**

How can the sector expand while losing 46,000+ jobs from two companies alone? This highlights the mechanics of the diffusion index and the sheer size of the service economy.

1. **Diffusion vs. Magnitude:** The ISM index counts *how many* firms are hiring vs. firing. While Amazon (one firm) is cutting massively, thousands of smaller healthcare providers, restaurants, and consultancies may be hiring one or two employees each. The diffusion index captures this broad-based stability.  
2. **Sector Rotation:** The layoffs are concentrated in **Information** (Tech) and **Transportation & Warehousing** (Logistics). The December data confirms this, listing these two sectors among those reporting employment decreases.4  
3. **Automation Pivot:** These layoffs are strategic, not strictly demand-driven. Companies like UPS are pivoting toward "higher-margin shipments" and automation, shedding operational roles to fund efficiency.25 This is a capital-for-labor swap similar to what is observed in manufacturing.

**4\. The Great Divergence: Analyzing the Split**

The central question of this research—whether employment contraction is synchronized—is answered definitively by the data. It is **split**. The manufacturing sector is undergoing a long-term contraction in labor intensity, while the services sector is maintaining a fragile but positive expansion.

### **4.1 Structural Decoupling**

The correlation between manufacturing and services employment has weakened.

* **Manufacturing (48.1%):** Driven by global trade dynamics, inventory cycles, and automation. The recovery in orders is being met with inventory drawdowns and productivity gains rather than new hires.  
* **Services (52.0%):** Driven by domestic consumption and demographic needs (healthcare, aging population). It is insulated from the tariff uncertainties that plague manufacturers.

### **4.2 The "Rolling Recession" Thesis**

The data supports the "rolling recession" hypothesis. The manufacturing sector has effectively been in a recession for over two years (28 months of employment contraction), while the services sector has largely avoided a downturn. This asynchronous cycle prevents the aggregate economy from tipping into a deep recession, as one engine fires while the other sputters.

**5\. Historical Benchmarking: Recession Risk Analysis**

To contextualize the severity of the current situation, it is instructive to benchmark the 2025-2026 data against the three major recessions of the 21st century.

### **5.1 The 2001 Dot-Com Recession**

* **Context:** A manufacturing-led downturn precipitated by a collapse in business investment.  
* **Comparison:** In 2001, manufacturing employment collapsed, failing to recover pre-recession levels for years.28 The current manufacturing reading of 48.1% is weak, but it is not the freefall seen in 2001\.  
* **Services Role:** In 2001, services weakened but provided a buffer. Today, we see a similar dynamic, but with the added complexity of a tech-sector contraction (the epicenter of 2001\) occurring within a broader expansion.

### **5.2 The 2008 Great Recession (Global Financial Crisis)**

* **Context:** Systemic financial collapse leading to broad-based demand destruction.  
* **Data:** During the GFC, the Services Employment Index plummeted to a record low of **37.8%** in November 2008\.21 Manufacturing employment dropped below 35%.  
* **Verdict:** The current situation bears **no resemblance** to 2008\.  
  * Current Services (52.0%) vs. 2008 Low (37.8%).  
  * Current Manufacturing (48.1%) vs. 2008 Low (\~30%).  
  * The financial plumbing of the economy remains functional, and demand (New Orders 57.1%) is expanding, which is impossible during a credit crisis.

### **5.3 The 2020 Pandemic Recession**

* **Context:** An artificial cessation of economic activity.  
* **Data:** Services Employment hit an all-time record low of **29.3%** in April 2020\.6 Manufacturing hit **27.8%**.2  
* **Verdict:** The current environment is characterized by *friction*—tariffs, labor shortages, skill mismatches—not *cessation*. The indices are oscillating around the neutral line, not collapsing to zero.

### **5.4 2026: A Unique "Friction" Economy**

The current period is unique. It is not a demand-side crisis (like 2008\) nor a supply-side shock (like 2020). It is a **friction economy**.

* **Friction 1:** Tariffs create pricing friction, stalling manufacturing hiring.  
* **Friction 2:** AI and automation create skills friction, leading to layoffs in tech/logistics while healthcare remains desperate for workers.  
* **Friction 3:** Interest rates create capital friction, forcing firms to prioritize efficiency over expansion.

**6\. Future Outlook: The Path Through 2026**

The ISM's **Semiannual Economic Forecast** for 2026 provides a roadmap for how supply management executives expect these trends to evolve.

### **6.1 Manufacturing Outlook**

* **Revenue Growth:** Manufacturers expect revenues to increase by **4.4%** in 2026\.29  
* **Capital Expenditures:** Expected to increase by **3.0%**.30  
* **Employment:** Expected to grow by a marginal **0.4 percentage points**.29  
* **Analysis:** The forecast confirms the "jobless recovery" thesis. Executives are planning for revenue growth (4.4%) and investing in capital (3.0%), but they are not planning to add labor (0.4%). This gap will be bridged by productivity improvements and automation.

### **6.2 Services Outlook**

* **Revenue Growth:** Service providers expect revenues to increase by **4.6%** in 2026\.30  
* **Capital Expenditures:** Expected to increase by **2.5%**.30  
* **Employment:** Expected to grow by **2.5%**.29  
* **Analysis:** The services sector is planning for balanced growth. Revenue and employment are expected to grow in tandem, indicating that the sector remains labor-intensive and will continue to be the primary source of job creation in the U.S. economy.

### **6.3 Conclusion: The Resilience of the Dual Economy**

The investigation into the ISM Employment Indices for early 2026 reveals a distinct split. The manufacturing sector is effectively managing a long-term contraction in its workforce, utilizing technology and attrition to weather policy uncertainty. The services sector, conversely, is expanding its workforce to meet demand, albeit with strategic restructuring in specific verticals.

For decision-makers, this implies a need for targeted strategies. The broad "recession" narrative is false; however, a "manufacturing recession" in employment is a verified reality. The U.S. economy in 2026 is not breaking; it is evolving, shifting its weight from the factory floor to the service desk, and increasingly, from the service desk to the automated algorithm. The "hope" mentioned by manufacturers may be deferred, but the resilience of the service economy provides a formidable floor against a wider economic collapse.
