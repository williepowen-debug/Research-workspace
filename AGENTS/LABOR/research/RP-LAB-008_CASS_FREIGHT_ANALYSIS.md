# **The 2026 Freight Market Equilibrium: Deciphering Demand Destruction and Structural Transformation**

The North American logistics sector as of January 2026 is navigating a watershed moment, characterized by a complex interplay between cyclical stabilization and a deep-seated structural reset. This research report investigates the prevailing market conditions by analyzing high-frequency indicators—specifically the Cass Freight Index and the American Trucking Associations (ATA) Truck Tonnage Index—alongside leading indicators in equipment manufacturing and corporate strategic shifts. The overarching question remains whether the industry is witnessing a sustainable recovery or merely "bumping along the bottom" after nearly four years of recessionary pressure.1 Through an exhaustive examination of data from late 2025 and the early weeks of 2026, the analysis confirms that while the absolute nadir of the volume contraction may have passed, the "efficiency era" has ushered in a permanent reduction in labor demand, validating the logistics layoff thesis through institutionalized automation and network reconfiguration.3

## **Analysis of the Cass Freight Index: Shipment and Expenditure Dynamics**

The Cass Freight Index serves as an essential benchmark for the health of the North American shipping economy, aggregating billions of dollars in freight transactions processed through its extensive client base.5 The performance of the shipments and expenditures components in December 2025 and January 2026 provides the most granular view of current demand destruction and the resultant pressure on carrier yields.

### **December 2025 Shipment Volumes and Year-over-Year Trends**

In December 2025, the shipments component of the Cass Freight Index fell to a reading of 0.932, representing a significant 7.2% month-over-month decline from November 2025\.6 On a seasonally adjusted (SA) basis, the decline was a more tempered 3.2%, yet this still marked a new cycle low for the index.6

When examining the year-over-year (YoY) metrics, the depth of the demand destruction becomes apparent. The December 2025 reading of 0.932, compared to the same period in 2024, reflects a 7.5% YoY contraction.6 This figure is particularly striking when contrasted with the full-year 2025 average decline of 6.1%, suggesting that the downturn accelerated in the final month of the year despite seasonal expectations.6

| Cass Freight Index \- Shipments | December 2025 | November 2025 | Month-over-Month % | Year-over-Year % |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Index Value (Base 1990=1.00) | 0.932 | 1.004 | \-7.2% (Actual) / \-3.2% (SA) | \-7.5% |

The primary catalyst for this precipitous drop in December was the impact of three major winter storms that paralyzed the highway network across the Midwest during the first two weeks of the month.6 However, weather was not the sole factor. Analysis of holiday consumer spending data reveals that retail inventories underwent significant destocking in late 2025\.8 Freight shipments across all modes trailed spending trends, indicating that retailers were fulfilling orders from existing stock rather than initiating new transportation movements—a classic signal of demand destruction at the logistics level.6

### **Expenditure Trends and the Divergence of Inferred Rates**

The expenditures component of the Cass Freight Index, which tracks the total dollar amount spent on freight services, displayed a different trajectory. In December 2025, the expenditures index fell to 3.101, a 1.9% decrease from the previous month.5 On a seasonally adjusted basis, however, the expenditures index actually rose by 0.2%.6

| Cass Freight Index \- Expenditures | December 2025 | November 2025 | Month-over-Month % | Year-over-Year % |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Index Value (Base 1990=1.00) | 3.101 | 3.163 | \-1.9% (Actual) / \+0.2% (SA) | \-0.6% |

The year-over-year change in expenditures was a marginal 0.6% decline.6 This creates a notable divergence: while shipment volumes dropped by 7.5% YoY, total spending remained nearly flat. This disparity highlights the "inferred rate" phenomenon. With shipments down considerably more than overall spending, the cost per shipment has effectively increased.6 This increase is attributed to a combination of rising fuel surcharges, shifts in the modal mix (with more freight moving via Truckload and less via LTL), and the cumulative effect of carrier regulations pushing operational costs higher.9

### **January 2026 Outlook and Weather Normalization**

Preliminary data and analyst commentary for January 2026 suggest a market in a state of flux. The weather-driven backlog from December 2025 created a temporary surge in spot market activity during the first half of January 2026, as fleets worked to clear delayed shipments.6 Warmer weather in early January allowed for this clearing, supporting volumes that were slightly above the normal seasonal trend.6

Despite this short-term volatility, the structural trend remains soft. Without the weather-related pent-up demand, the shipments component was projected to be down roughly 5% YoY in January.6 The Cass Inferred Freight Rates, which had turned positive in December for the first time in two years, accelerated to a 4.3% YoY increase in early 2026, primarily due to capacity constraints and the "retrenchment" of private fleets back to for-hire services in specific lanes.9

## **ATA Truck Tonnage Index: Contract Stability Amidst Broader Weakness**

The American Trucking Associations (ATA) For-Hire Truck Tonnage Index provides a complementary view of the market, focusing predominantly on contract freight as opposed to the more volatile spot-heavy components of other indexes.11 The index performance at the close of 2025 and into early 2026 suggests a level of stabilization that has yet to be reflected in broader shipment counts.

### **Tonnage Readings and Quarterly Performance**

The ATA reported that its advanced seasonally adjusted For-Hire Truck Tonnage Index rose 0.4% in December 2025, following a 0.2% increase in November.13 The December reading of 112.9 reflects a 0.9% increase compared to December 2024, representing a rare year-over-year gain in a year characterized by sequential weakness.13

| ATA Truck Tonnage Index (SA) | December 2025 | November 2025 | October 2025 | Full Year 2025 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Index (Base 2015=100) | 112.9 | 112.4 | 111.9 | 112.4 (Avg) |
| Sequential Change | \+0.4% | \+0.2% | \-2.1% | \+0.1% YoY |

For the entirety of 2025, tonnage rose just 0.1% over the 2024 average.13 This flat result underscores a market that has found a floor but lacks the momentum for a meaningful recovery. The fourth quarter of 2025 was particularly challenging, with the index average falling 1.8% from the third quarter—the largest sequential quarterly decline since early 2023\.13

### **The Resilience of Contract Freight**

The disparity between the Cass Shipment Index (down 7.5% YoY) and the ATA Tonnage Index (up 0.9% YoY) reveals a significant trend: contract freight is holding up far better than spot market volumes.12 ATA Chief Economist Bob Costello observed that the index, which is dominated by contract movements, demonstrates that large carriers with established customer relationships have maintained higher levels of activity than the broader market would suggest.12

However, this stability is fragile. Tonnage levels remain at historic lows, having been suppressed by soft manufacturing and construction activity throughout 2025\.13 The "inventory cycle" is gradually improving, meaning that the bloated stocks that served as a headwind in previous years are normalizing, but they have not yet reached a state of aggressive restocking.1

## **Class 8 Truck Orders: A Leading Indicator of Regulatory Arbitrage**

One of the most paradoxical developments in the current freight cycle is the recent "skyrocketing" of Class 8 truck orders. In December 2025, preliminary North American Class 8 net orders surged by 118% month-over-month to approximately 42,700 units.16 This figure marks the strongest monthly order intake since late 2022 and would typically signal a robust demand recovery; however, the drivers behind this spike are overwhelmingly regulatory and strategic rather than fundamental.16

### **Deciphering the December Surge**

Industry analysts from ACT Research and FTR Transportation Intelligence emphasize that December's order activity does not reflect an anticipation of higher freight volumes or carrier profitability.16 Instead, it is the result of several convergent factors:

1. **EPA 2027 NOx Pre-buy:** Stricter EPA emissions standards for model-year 2027 trucks are expected to significantly increase the acquisition cost and complexity of new equipment.16 Large fleets are engaging in an early "pre-buy" of 2026 models to secure equipment under existing technology standards.17  
2. **Aging Fleets:** The average age of a tractor on the road has reached 14.2 years.17 Carriers that deferred replacement during the brutal 2023-2024 downturn reached a breaking point where maintenance costs outweighed the capital expense of new trucks.16  
3. **Policy Clarity:** The resolution of uncertainty surrounding Section 232 tariffs and final EPA rulings in late 2025 provided fleets with the necessary visibility to finalize deferred orders.18

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Units Ordered | 42,700 | 19,547 | \+16% to \+21% | \~29,000 |
| Sequential Change | \+118% | \-47% (Nov vs Oct) | \-22% (Sep-Dec Cum.) | NA |

The magnitude of the December surge is viewed as "overstating the improvement" in the market.16 Cumulative orders for the 2026 order season (beginning in September) remain 22% below the previous year, highlighting that the spike was a localized "catch-up" event rather than the start of a sustained acceleration.8

### **OEM Production Discipline**

Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) such as Freightliner, Peterbilt, and Mack have maintained tight production discipline into early 2026, keeping build rates aligned with backlogs rather than speculative demand.21 Tractor inventories are finally approaching healthier alignment, but vocational segments—such as those serving construction and energy—remain elevated as project flow continues to lag due to high financing costs.20

Mack Truck was the only major manufacturer to report an increase in sales for 2025, a result of robust infrastructure spending and the relative resilience of vocational segments compared to the over-the-road freight market.19

## **The State of the Freight Recession: Recovery or Continued Stasis?**

The duration and severity of the current freight downturn have challenged historical precedents. Tracing its origins to April 2022, the recession has persisted for over 45 months.23 As the industry moves into 2026, the question of whether the cycle has inflected toward recovery is central to strategic planning.

### **Chronology of a Downcycle**

The downturn began as a post-pandemic normalization but evolved into a prolonged period of demand destruction.22 Profit margins evaporated as inflation and fuel costs remained elevated while freight rates collapsed. By late 2024, the market had shed over 10% of active carriers, including the historic insolvency of Yellow Corp..24

Analysts describe the current environment as "bumping along the bottom".1 While the market is no longer crashing as it did in 2022—when rates were dropping by 20% per month—it has yet to experience the "bull market" momentum of 2021\.1

### **Recovery Forecasts for 2026**

Expert consensus suggests that 2026 will be a year of gradual, "U-shaped" recovery rather than a sharp "V-shaped" bounce.1 Several key metrics support this outlook:

1. **Capacity Contraction:** The persistent sub-replacement levels of truck sales and accelerated carrier bankruptcies (a 41% spike in exits over the last two years) are finally flushing out excess capacity.1  
2. **Inventory Normalization:** Retail destocking is largely finished, and the industry is entering a "restocking" phase.1 This shift typically leads to more consistent freight volumes and regular lanes rather than sporadic spot market chaos.  
3. **Supply-Demand Intersection:** Forecasts indicate that by mid-2026, the cumulative effect of carrier exits will intersect with steady GDP growth, causing a "flip" in pricing power back to the carriers.1

Industry observers agree that the "Great Freight Recession" could be officially declared over by the second or third quarter of 2026, provided that geopolitical tensions and tariff-related cost pressures do not trigger another wave of demand destruction.1

## **Corporate Volume Commentary: UPS and the Amazon Strategic Shift**

The structural realignment of the logistics industry is most clearly articulated in the evolving relationship between United Parcel Service (UPS) and Amazon. Their multi-year strategic pivot provides a case study in the "efficiency era" and the broader validation of the logistics layoff thesis.

### **The "Amazon Glide-down" and UPS Transformation**

UPS is in the midst of the largest network reconfiguration in its history, a strategy anchored by the "Amazon Glide-down".25 In 2025, UPS successfully reduced its Amazon-related volume by approximately 1 million pieces per day.27 For 2026, the company plans to "glide down" another 1 million pieces per day.27

This reduction is a deliberate move to shed lower-margin business in favor of higher-yield segments like Small and Medium-sized Businesses (SMB), healthcare, and B2B.26 In the fourth quarter of 2025, UPS's U.S. Domestic average daily volume (ADV) declined by 10.8%, primarily due to this planned reduction.27 Despite the volume drop, revenue per piece increased by 8.3%, demonstrating the success of their revenue quality strategy.27

| UPS Domestic Metrics | Q4 2025 | Q4 2024 | YoY Change |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Revenue | $16.8 Billion | $17.4 Billion | \-3.2% |
| Average Daily Volume (ADV) | 18.2M (est) | 20.4M (est) | \-10.8% |
| Revenue Per Piece | $9.23 (est) | $8.52 (est) | \+8.3% |
| B2B Volume Share | 42.3% | 39.8% | \+250 bps |

### **Amazon's Logistics Self-Reliance**

Parallel to UPS's withdrawal, Amazon continues to internalize its logistics operations. By building out its own inbound and last-mile networks, Amazon has reduced its global cost to serve for two consecutive years while increasing delivery speeds.29 This internal optimization is a primary driver of the reduced demand for external carriers, illustrating a form of "logistics displacement" that is a core component of the demand destruction story.27

## **Validating the Logistics Layoff Thesis: Structural Displacement**

The central inquiry of this research concerns whether the current freight and trucking indicators validate the logistics layoff thesis. The data from 2025 and the early weeks of 2026 suggests that the workforce reductions across the industry are not merely cyclical but are structural, driven by a permanent shift toward automation and "lean" operational models.

### **The Scale of Workforce Reductions**

The U.S. labor market at the start of 2026 is witnessing one of the broadest waves of workforce reduction in recent history.3 Nearly 600,000 workers have been affected by layoffs across tech, logistics, and manufacturing in early 2026 alone.3

1. **UPS:** After cutting 48,000 positions in 2025, UPS announced plans to eliminate up to 30,000 additional jobs in 2026\.3 These cuts are concentrated in delivery, sorting, and warehouse roles as the company reconfigures its network and closes facilities.3  
2. **Amazon:** The company confirmed cuts of about 16,000 corporate roles in early 2026, bringing its total planned layoffs to nearly 30,000 worldwide.3  
3. **FedEx:** A logistics site in North Texas was shut down in early 2025, affecting 305 employees, with additional cuts continuing through 2026\.30  
4. **Broader Industry:** WARN notices filed in late 2025 and early 2026 reveal a steady drumbeat of facility closures and layoffs at firms like GXO Logistics, DHL, and Ryder.31

### **Automation as a Displacement Catalyst**

The "efficiency era" is characterized by the replacement of human labor with automated systems. UPS management noted that the cost per piece in automated buildings is 28% lower than in conventional hubs.27 By the end of 2026, the company expects 68% of its U.S. volume to be processed through automated facilities, up from 66.5% in 2025\.34

Similarly, third-party logistics (3PL) providers like C.H. Robinson are leveraging AI-driven strategies to increase productivity while reducing headcounts. Average employee headcounts at C.H. Robinson declined by 12.9% in the fourth quarter of 2025\.35 These reductions are framed as "evergreen productivity improvements" fueled by AI, suggesting that even if freight volumes recover, the corresponding labor demand will not return to pre-recession levels.35

## **The Spot Market and Spot-Contract Gap Analysis**

The relationship between spot and contract rates is a critical indicator of market inflection points. For over three years, truckload pricing remained inverted, with spot rates hovering well below contract rates—an unsustainable dynamic for many smaller motor carriers.38

### **December 2025 Spot Rate Surge**

In December 2025, spot truckload rates reached their highest monthly averages of the year, although gains in freight volume were limited.39 The national average spot van rate jumped 20 cents to $2.29 per mile, and the reefer rate rose 15 cents to $2.69 per mile.39

| DAT Spot Rates (Dec 2025\) | Rate per Mile | MoM Change | YoY Change |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Van | $2.29 | \+$0.20 | \+9.0% |
| Reefer | $2.69 | \+$0.15 | \+9.0% |
| Flatbed | $2.53 | \+$0.06 | \+6.0% |

The surge was driven by the "quirks of the holiday calendar" and severe weather rather than a sustained demand inflection.39 However, the spread between average spot and contract rates narrowed to its smallest gap since early 2022, underscoring a growing urgency among shippers to secure capacity in a tightening environment.39

### **January 2026 Market Intelligence**

By late January 2026, the holiday-driven surge began to normalize. FreightWaves SONAR reported that nationwide tender rejections stood at 11.5% in late January, as "Winter Storm Fern" briefly tightened capacity.42 While rates fell seasonally, they did so at a slower pace than in previous years, indicating that the baseline of available capacity has finally begun to erode.43

## **Regional Insights and Modal Performance**

The freight downturn has not affected all segments and regions equally. Differential performance in various modes provides a more nuanced understanding of the broader economy.

### **Vocational and LTL Resilience**

The Less-Than-Truckload (LTL) segment has shown greater resilience than the Truckload (TL) segment throughout 2025\. C.H. Robinson reported that its LTL business delivered year-over-year volume growth for eight consecutive quarters, outperforming the broader market.37 In the manufacturing sector, vocational Class 8 demand—units like dump trucks and concrete mixers—remained relatively supported by federal infrastructure spending, even as over-the-road tractor demand plummeted.19

### **Regional Disruption and Port Activity**

Global trade policies and tariff-related "front-running" in late 2025 caused significant dislocations in port activity. Ocean rates declined substantially toward the end of the year as excess vessel capacity entered the market, coinciding with a more pronounced decline in demand after the third-quarter peak.37 In early 2026, trans-Pacific container rates began dropping earlier than expected, suggesting retailer caution amidst ongoing trade war uncertainty.42

## **Cross-Referencing Macro Indicators: Summary Findings**

When the Cass Freight Index, ATA Tonnage Index, and corporate data are cross-referenced, a consistent narrative of structural transformation emerges.

### **The "Bumping Along the Bottom" Hypothesis**

The evidence supports the hypothesis that the freight market is "bumping along the bottom".1 The bottom of the volume cycle likely occurred in late 2023, but the financial bottom for carriers has taken significantly longer to materialize due to the massive influx of capacity during the pandemic years.1

1. **Demand:** Shipment volumes remain soft and are highly sensitive to external shocks like weather. The 7.5% YoY decline in Cass Shipments confirms that demand destruction is still present.6  
2. **Pricing:** Carrier yields are stabilizing. The divergence between expenditures and shipments indicates that rates are finally finding a floor as operational costs create a "hard stop" for further pricing concessions.1  
3. **Capacity:** The "purge" of excess capacity is accelerating. Sub-replacement truck sales and high carrier exit rates are finally rebalancing the supply-demand equation.1

### **Final Assessment of the Logistics Layoff Thesis**

The logistics layoff thesis is strongly validated by the data. The workforce reductions are not merely a reaction to a temporary slump but are the realization of long-term strategic investments in automation and network density.27 UPS and Amazon's pivot away from each other—and toward more automated, self-reliant models—has permanently altered the labor requirements of the industry.3 The "efficiency era" is now a structural reality, where the recovery of freight volumes will not be accompanied by a traditional recovery in logistics employment.

## **Strategic Conclusions for 2026**

The North American freight market enters 2026 at an inflection point. While it is no longer in free fall, it is not yet in a broad-based recovery. The industry is leaner, more disciplined, and increasingly automated.2

For stakeholders, the primary takeaways are:

* **For Carriers:** Financial and operating discipline remains paramount. The first half of 2026 will likely require the same "survival mode" mentality as 2025, with a more robust seller's market emerging by mid-year.1  
* **For Shippers:** The window of favorable truckload pricing is closing. As capacity continues to contract and regulatory costs for 2027 models begin to factor into contract negotiations, the risk of a rapid tightening cycle increases.1  
* **For Labor:** The structural shift toward automation is permanent. The job cuts announced in early 2026 represent a recalibration of the industry's labor-to-volume ratio that will persist even as the freight cycle turns positive.3

In summation, 2026 is a foundational year for the logistics industry—one characterized by the exhaustion of the old freight cycle and the definitive beginning of a new, more efficient, and labor-lean era.
