# **Structural Interdependence in the Private Credit Ecosystem: A Comprehensive Bank-BDC Exposure Mapping**

The contemporary landscape of corporate finance is undergoing a fundamental structural realignment, moving away from the traditional, bank-centric model toward a hybrid architecture where regulated financial institutions and private credit vehicles exist in a state of deep, multifaceted interdependence. This evolution is not merely a shift in market share but a systemic transformation of how liquidity is originated, structured, and risk-managed. At the core of this transition is the Business Development Company (BDC), which has evolved from a niche investment vehicle into a primary engine of middle-market lending, often acting as a high-velocity intermediary for bank capital. As of 2025 and moving into 2026, the relationship between banks and BDCs has matured into what analysts term the "intermediation chain," a structure where banks provide the foundational liquidity through massive credit facilities and strategic partnerships, while BDCs and private credit funds execute the final delivery of capital to the real economy.1

This structural mapping provides an exhaustive examination of the ties that bind the regulated banking sector to the private credit ecosystem, spanning global systemically important banks (GSIBs) and an increasingly assertive cohort of regional institutions. The analysis identifies the specific mechanisms of exposure—ranging from subscription-secured facilities and NAV-based loans to warehouse lines and equity-anchored joint ventures—while assessing the systemic implications of concentrated exposures and the regulatory catalysts driving this "renaissance" of bank involvement in non-bank finance.

## **The Macroeconomic Context and Regulatory Catalysts**

The proliferation of bank exposure to BDCs and private credit funds is inextricably linked to the tightening regulatory environment for traditional lenders. The implementation of the Basel III "Endgame" and the emergence of "Basel IV" standards have significantly increased the cost of maintaining riskier corporate loans on bank balance sheets.2 In response, global banks have sought to preserve their client relationships and M\&A advisory fees by pivoting to a capital-light model. This involves originating loans and then distributing the risk to private credit partners or providing the financing for those partners to hold the debt themselves.3

Starting in late 2024, new reporting requirements forced US banks with over $10 billion in assets to provide a granular breakdown of their exposure to non-depository financial institutions (NDFIs). This regulatory shift unveiled a $1.2 trillion market, with bank loans to NDFIs reaching approximately 10.4% of total bank loans by June 2025—nearly triple the percentage recorded a decade prior.4 The "Business Credit Intermediary" category, which captures loans to private credit funds and BDCs, stood at approximately $299.3 billion, while private equity fund finance, including subscription and NAV facilities, accounted for another $285.2 billion.4 These figures exclude approximately $340 billion in unutilized bank lending commitments, indicating a total potential liquidity support system nearing $1 trillion.4

## **Mapping Global Systemically Important Bank (GSIB) Strategies**

The largest US banks, the GSIBs, serve as the primary facilitators for the "mega-funds" managed by titans such as Blackstone, Apollo, Ares, and Blue Owl.5 Their involvement has evolved from simple lending to the formation of landmark direct lending programs that pair the banks’ massive origination networks with the funds’ deep capital pools.3

### **Citigroup and the "Originate-to-Share" Architecture**

Citigroup’s partnership with Apollo Global Management, announced in September 2024, represents a paradigm shift in GSIB-private credit collaboration. The $25 billion program targets North American corporate and sponsor-backed lending, with a focus on non-investment grade transactions and leveraged buyouts.7 This "originate-to-share" model allows Citi to utilize its high-touch corporate relationships and M\&A expertise without deploying significant balance sheet capital, thereby avoiding a heavy RWA charge.3 The program includes participation from Mubadala Investment Company and Apollo’s insurance subsidiary, Athene, highlighting the increasing role of insurance capital as the ultimate anchor for these bank-led originations.8

In practice, this partnership functions as a "private staple" financing mechanism. During the early 2025 sale of Boeing’s Jeppesen navigation unit, Citi and Apollo offered potential buyers a pre-arranged debt package valued between $3 billion and $3.5 billion, effectively streamlining the acquisition process in a volatile rate environment.11

### **Wells Fargo and the "Overland Advantage"**

Wells Fargo has pursued a different path, focusing on an "originate-and-retain" strategy through its joint venture with Centerbridge Partners. The creation of **Overland Advantage**, a $5 billion BDC, allows Wells Fargo to capture the high-yielding middle-market lending space that had largely migrated to private credit.3 Overland targets non-sponsored, family-owned, and founder-led businesses—a segment often neglected by the largest private equity-focused credit funds.13

By January 2026, Overland Advantage had demonstrated significant momentum, underwriting approximately $7 billion in custom financing solutions since its May 2024 launch.15 The partnership structure allows Wells Fargo to provide senior bank revolving credit while Overland Advantage provides senior secured term loans, creating a "one-stop-shop" for middle-market borrowers.14

### **JPMorgan Chase and the Multi-Billion Dollar Commitment**

JPMorgan Chase has balanced its role as a massive provider of fund finance with its own direct lending ambitions. In February 2025, the bank committed $50 billion of its capital to direct lending, supplemented by $15 billion from third-party co-lenders.3 Unlike Citi or Wells, JPMorgan maintains a hybrid model, retaining control over sourcing and relationship economics while selectively allocating tranches to external private credit investors.3

Simultaneously, JPMorgan remains the dominant administrative agent for some of the largest revolving credit facilities in the BDC space. This includes a $4.7 billion senior secured facility for FS KKR Capital Corp. (FSK) and major warehouse lines for the Blackstone Secured Lending Fund.17

### **Barclays and AGL Credit Management**

Barclays established an exclusive cooperation agreement with AGL Credit Management in April 2024, aimed at the $1.7 trillion private credit market.19 Backed by a $1 billion anchor commitment from the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA), the platform operates as an independent manager with exclusive access to Barclays’ leveraged finance deal flow.19 However, this partnership has faced challenges in raising non-ADIA capital, securing less than $70 million from other investors by early 2024, reflecting the market’s caution toward newer managers in the direct lending space.21

## **Regional Banks: The Fund Finance "Renaissance"**

A critical second-order insight from the 2023 banking crisis is that the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and First Republic created a massive void in the middle-market fund finance sector. This vacuum has been filled by a group of "super-regional" banks that have made private credit and fund finance a top strategic priority for 2025 and 2026\.22

### **Citizens Financial Group: Building the "Premier Private Bank"**

Citizens Financial Group (CFG) has emerged as the clear leader in this regional bank pivot. Following the strategic hiring of over 200 specialists from defunct institutions like SVB and First Republic in late 2023, Citizens has built a "renaissance" around its Private Bank division.22 By early 2026, the Private Bank reached $40 billion in Assets Under Management and was on track to double its bottom-line contribution within three years.22

Citizens has specialized in catering to middle-market GPs, offering customized subscription lines and treasury management solutions that mimic the "high-touch" service model of SVB.23 Its capital markets fee performance, which grew 46% year-over-year in 2024, is largely a result of its progress in becoming a leading lender to the private equity community.26

### **Webster Bank and the Marathon Asset Management JV**

Webster Bank significantly expanded its private credit capabilities in July 2024 by forming a joint venture with Marathon Asset Management.14 The joint venture focuses on directly sourced senior secured loans for sponsor-backed middle-market companies.28 By January 2026, the partnership had underwritten landmark deals, such as the senior secured facility for H.I.G. Capital’s acquisition of Shore Excursions Group.29

### **PNC Bank and TCW Steel City**

PNC Financial Services formalized a 15-year collaboration with the TCW Group to launch **TCW Steel City** in May 2024\.30 The platform, targeting $2.5 billion in investor equity in its first year, leverages PNC’s national commercial banking network to source senior secured cash-flow and asset-based loans.30 This partnership is notable for its focus on both sponsored and non-sponsored middle-market firms, further broadening the pool of potential borrowers.30

### **Axos and Huntington: Absorbing Fallen Talent**

Both Axos Bank and Huntington National Bank have built their fund finance programs by absorbing entire teams from fallen lenders. Huntington added a 10-person team of former Signature Bank staff to build its subscription line lending program, while Axos hired Trevor Freeman to lead a similar effort.23 Axos now offers warehouse lines and fund finance facilities scaling up to $350 million, utilizing technology-driven service models to compete with larger peers.33

## **Fund Finance Mechanisms: Subscription, NAV, and Warehouse Lines**

The exposure mapping between banks and BDCs is defined by three primary financial instruments, each with unique risk profiles and regulatory treatments.35

### **Subscription Credit Facilities (Sublines)**

Subscription lines remain the industry’s "workhorse," with an estimated global market size of $850 billion to $900 billion.35 These facilities are secured by the undrawn capital commitments of a fund’s investors.37 For banks, these are considered low-risk, highly structured products, as the ultimate source of repayment is the callable equity of high-net-worth individuals, institutional investors, and pension funds.37 BMO Financial Group and SMBC Nikko have consistently ranked among the most active lenders in this segment.39

### **Net Asset Value (NAV) Facilities**

NAV facilities represent the next frontier of structural integration, secured by the actual portfolio assets of a fund.41 The market for NAV financing grew to $100 billion by 2024 and is projected to reach $600 billion by 2030\.43 These facilities became critical liquidity valves in 2024 and 2025 as the slow M\&A environment prevented funds from exiting portfolio companies.45 Banks like **Customers Bank** and **Piermont Bank** have carved out specialized niches in NAV lending, often providing these facilities to funds late in their lifecycle.47

### **Warehouse Lines of Credit**

Warehouse lines are high-velocity revolving facilities that provide the initial funding for BDC loan originations before they are term-leveraged via CLOs or unsecured bond issuances.49 These are typically non-recourse facilities, collateralized by the specific pool of loans being warehoused.51 Large banks, including JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo, are the primary providers of these critical "pipes" in the credit system.18

## **Concentrated Exposure and Systemic Vulnerabilities**

The rapid scaling of bank involvement in the private credit market has not occurred without the accumulation of significant systemic risks. Third-order analysis reveals that the "flight to quality" among lenders has paradoxically increased the potential for correlated distress.

### **Name-Specific Concentration Risk**

Moody’s research indicates that many banks have concentrated exposures to a small, elite group of private credit managers.4 While this concentration allows for deeper underwriting familiarity, it creates a "single point of failure" risk; if one major credit manager faces a liquidity event or valuation scandal, the impact would ripple across the dozen or more banks providing its sublines, warehouse facilities, and NAV loans.4 This risk is especially acute for smaller banks that have accelerated their NDFI lending to compensate for the loss of traditional C\&I market share.4

### **Portfolio Overlap and "Club" Lending Distress**

Analysis of 162 BDCs suggests a high degree of portfolio overlap, particularly among funds with over $5 billion in assets, where the average overlap is 9.2%.60 This is a result of "clubbing," where multiple BDCs and private credit funds co-invest in $1 billion+ transactions that were previously serviced by the broadly syndicated loan market.60

For banks, this means that even if they are lending to diverse BDC families, their ultimate collateral may be highly correlated. For example, a bank lending to 20 different BDCs may find that all 20 BDCs hold a significant position in the Zendesk or Catalent term loans.60 In a systemic downturn, these "clubbed" loans would likely experience simultaneous distress, leading to correlated drawdowns on bank credit lines across multiple BDC counterparties.61

### **Opacity and Valuation Discrepancies**

Unlike the broadly syndicated loan market, which has daily price discovery, private credit instruments often rely on internally managed, mark-to-model valuations.59 Moody’s and other regulators have highlighted that "underwriting and collateral controls can fail even when loans are secured," as evidenced by the losses experienced by banks in the recent Tricolor bankruptcy.59 The inherent opacity of BDC portfolios makes it difficult for bank lenders to assess the true risk of their exposures until a credit event occurs.4

## **The Rise of Asset-Backed Finance (ABF) and Insurance Integration**

As we move into 2026, the next phase of the bank-BDC relationship is the expansion into Asset-Backed Finance (ABF).63 FSK and other major BDCs are increasingly focusing on ABF as banks exit non-core commercial and consumer lending businesses.64 This segment represents directly originated investments backed by pools of hard assets, providing a diversification benefit from traditional cash-flow lending.64

A critical component of this shift is the role of insurance companies. Firms like Athene (Apollo’s insurance arm) and Martello Re (Centerbridge’s partnership with MassMutual) are increasingly providing the long-term capital that anchors bank-private credit joint ventures.6 This integration allows banks to maintain their origination engines while leveraging the "yield-hungry" capital of life insurers, who are less constrained by the short-term capital requirements that bind regulated banks.66

## **Strategic Implications for Regional Lenders: The "Ancillary Service" Diet**

For regional banks, the decision to lend to a BDC is increasingly being viewed through the lens of a "fair share" ancillary service requirement. Tim Spence, CEO of Fifth Third Bancorp, has described a "risk-weighted asset diet" where the bank walks away from corporate lending relationships—including potentially to funds—if it cannot capture a significant share of the treasury, payments, and wealth management business.68

This trend indicates that bank credit to BDCs will become increasingly selective. BDCs that can offer their bank lenders high-margin ancillary business will continue to enjoy favorable terms, while those looking for simple balance sheet capacity may face higher rates or reduced commitment sizes.68

## **Conclusion: The New Financial Order of 2026**

The bank-BDC exposure mapping reveals a financial ecosystem that has moved beyond "frenemy" competition into a state of structural symbiosis. Banks have successfully navigated the "gauntlet" of high interest rates and regulatory pruning by becoming the infrastructure providers for the private credit market.70

For 2026, the trajectory indicates a further deepening of this integration. The " renaissance" of regional banking, led by institutions like Citizens and Webster, suggests that fund finance will remain a primary engine of loan growth, even as traditional C\&I lending remains flat.22 The primary risk remains the " intermediation chain" price channel; as banks pass through their funding costs, the most vulnerable middle-market borrowers may face unsustainable interest burdens, potentially testing the resilience of the very BDC portfolios that banks are now so heavily financing.1

Ultimately, the BDC structure—characterized by indefinite lifespans, regulatory transparency, and high dividend payouts—has proven to be the ideal vehicle for bank-to-market credit delivery.72 As 2026 unfolds, the successful financial institutions will be those that can master the art of "capital-light" origination and risk distribution while managing the concentrated exposures that are now a permanent feature of the modern balance sheet.3
