# **The Convergence of Traditional Banking and Private Credit: Mapping Exposure Channels, Structural Risks, and Systemic Interlinkages**

The global financial landscape is currently defined by a profound structural migration of credit intermediation from the regulated banking sector to the private credit and non-bank financial institution (NBFI) ecosystem. This expansion, which has seen private credit assets under management grow nearly tenfold over the past fifteen years to reach approximately $1.5 trillion to $2.1 trillion by late 2024, has not resulted in a complete decoupling of the two sectors.1 Instead, it has fostered a complex web of interdependencies where traditional banks serve as the essential providers of liquidity, leverage, and distribution for private credit managers. As of the second quarter of 2025, bank lending to private equity and credit funds has surged to nearly $497 billion, representing a significant 59% increase from late 2024 levels.3 This report analyzes the specific channels through which banks maintain exposure to Business Development Companies (BDCs) and private credit funds, quantifies these exposures where possible, and maps the recursive "leverage-on-leverage" risks that threaten financial stability in a high-interest-rate environment.

## **The Evolution of the Bank-Private Credit Nexus**

The relationship between traditional depository institutions and private credit managers has evolved from direct competition to a sophisticated hybrid model. Banks have increasingly recognized that they cannot compete with the flexibility and speed of private lenders in certain segments of the middle market, leading them to adopt strategies that prioritize fee-based revenue and capital efficiency.4 This hybrid strategy involves a combination of direct lending to funds (fund finance), programmatic partnerships, and the holding of securitized products like Collateralized Loan Obligations (CLOs). The systemic importance of this nexus is underscored by the fact that loans to NDFIs (Non-Depository Financial Institutions) have outpaced all other bank lending activities since 2016, with approximately $300 billion in loans outstanding specifically to private credit participants as of mid-2025.5

## **Fund Finance: The Essential Plumbing of Private Markets**

Fund finance represents the most direct and pervasive credit link between banks and private credit managers. These facilities are designed to optimize a fund's internal rate of return (IRR) by delaying capital calls and managing operational cash flow.6 The two primary instruments in this space—subscription lines and Net Asset Value (NAV) facilities—carry distinct risk characteristics and are utilized at different stages of a fund's lifecycle.

### **Subscription Credit Facilities and the Mechanics of LP Commitments**

Subscription credit facilities, often referred to as "sub-lines," are revolving credit facilities secured by the uncalled capital commitments of a fund’s Limited Partners (LPs).8 These facilities allow General Partners (GPs) to bridge the time required to call capital from LPs, providing a streamlined way to fund investments and manage the "J-curve" early in a fund's life.7 For banks, these are considered low-risk, high-quality exposures because they are underwritten based on the creditworthiness of a diversified pool of institutional investors, such as pension funds, endowments, and sovereign wealth funds.8

The collateral package for a sub-line is highly specialized, involving a pledge of the fund's contractual right to issue capital calls and receive proceeds. Lenders are protected by a "borrowing base" calculation, where the available credit is a function of the aggregate uncalled capital commitments of "eligible" investors, multiplied by specific advance rates.8 Advance rates typically range from 60% for unrated or smaller investors to as high as 90% for high-quality institutional LPs.10 Major providers in this space include Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group (MUFG), which manages over $30 billion in committed capital lines as of 2025, and Citizens Financial Group (CFG), which has emerged as a regional bellwether for fund finance activity.6

### **The Rise of NAV Lending and "Money-Out" Dynamics**

As funds mature and their capital commitments are exhausted, managers increasingly turn to NAV facilities. Unlike sub-lines, which are secured by uncalled capital, NAV loans are collateralized by the net asset value of the fund’s underlying portfolio investments.12 This market is forecasted to grow from $100 billion in 2022 to over $700 billion by 2030, driven by the need for liquidity in an environment where exit activity has slowed.13

NAV lending is categorized by its use of proceeds: "money-in" loans fund add-on acquisitions or support portfolio companies, while "money-out" loans fund distributions to LPs. While 86% of LPs support money-in facilities, there is significant opposition to money-out lending, with 62% of LPs viewing it as a risky way to manufacture Distributed to Paid-In capital (DPI) metrics.14 This tension has led to a 90% decrease in the use of money-out NAV loans in some segments of the market as of late 2023, though the overall demand for fund-level leverage remains high.15 For banks, NAV lending presents a more complex risk profile than sub-lines, as it requires sophisticated monitoring of illiquid, marked-to-model valuations and carries the systemic risk of cross-collateralization.12

## **Citizens Financial Group (CFG): A Regional Bellwether in Specialized Credit**

Citizens Financial Group (CFG), with approximately $222.7 billion in total assets as of late 2025, provides a critical case study of how mid-sized regional banks are integrating private credit services into their commercial banking segments.18 CFG has successfully navigated a challenging interest rate environment by pivoting toward specialized lending and capital markets advisory, positioning itself as a "true organic growth story" among regional banks.20

### **Commercial Banking Strategy and Subscription Line Exposure**

CFG’s commercial banking segment, which serves businesses with complex financial needs, has identified subscription line financing as a primary driver of its origination strength.11 The bank utilizes these facilities to support deal-related activity and asset-backed lending, which helps diversify its loan portfolio away from traditional corporate exposures.11 Transcripts from 2024 and 2025 highlight that CFG’s management views fund finance as a high-performing organization focus, integrated with its broader technology and people investments.21

### **Mapping the Citizens Financial Group Risk Model**

A central concern for the REGINALD watchlist is the recursive risk inherent in regional bank fund finance desks. The "CFG Risk Map" involves a scenario where LPs borrow to fund their capital calls, and the fund itself borrows via a sub-line provided by a bank like Citizens. This "leverage-on-leverage" creates a double-layered exposure to a single set of underlying commitments. If the LP defaults on its own loan, it cannot meet the fund's capital call, which in turn impairs the collateral of the bank's sub-line. While CFG maintains a strong allowance for credit losses (1.61%) and high general office reserves (10.6%), the lack of transparency in LP-level borrowing makes it difficult to assess the true aggregate leverage in the system.17

## **The Leverage-on-Leverage Matrix: Mapping Recursive Risk**

The most significant systemic risk in the private credit ecosystem is the layering of debt across multiple levels of the financial chain. This "leverage-on-leverage" structure creates hidden interconnections that can turn a localized credit event into a systemic liquidity shock.13

### **The Four Layers of Debt Layering**

1. **Portfolio Company Level:** Middle-market companies often carry high leverage, with debt-to-EBITDA ratios typically ranging from 4.6x to 5.7x.13  
2. **Fund/BDC Level:** Managers use revolving credit facilities, warehouse lines, or CLOs to enhance returns for their investors. Aggregated leverage for credit funds is often cited as modest, typically between 1.0x and 1.2x, while BDCs are capped by regulation at 2.0x.10  
3. **Fund Financing Level:** Banks provide sub-lines and NAV facilities to the fund entity itself, secured by capital commitments or the portfolio's aggregate value.8  
4. **LP Level:** Institutional investors may use leverage to manage their own private market allocations or to achieve a leverage effect on their investment portfolios.12

### **Systemic Implications and Contagion Channels**

The concern for regulators is that while these assets have moved off bank balance sheets, the connections to banks remain, merely changing form.17 This creates several critical vulnerabilities:

* **Cross-Collateralization:** NAV loans shift the risk from the individual portfolio company level to the fund level. An idiosyncratic failure at one large company could theoretically trigger a LTV covenant breach for the entire fund's debt facility.12  
* **Valuation Lag and Opacity:** Private loans are marked-to-model rather than marked-to-market. This "valuation lag" can mask the accumulation of risk, making the fund’s NAV appear stable even as the real economic value of underlying loans declines.17  
* **Correlation Risk:** As the market becomes more competitive, parties with equity exposure to private markets gain increasing exposure to debt on those same equities, creating a feedback loop that exacerbates pain during a downturn.14

Industry reports from Alpha Group and 17Capital suggest that these risks are manageable, noting that portfolio valuations would need to decline by more than 30%—significantly worse than the 6% decline seen during the 2008 financial crisis—to breach typical covenant levels.16 However, the SEC and other regulators remain focused on the "opaque nature" of these funds and the potential for market spillovers to highly interconnected segments like leveraged loans.16

## **Direct Partnerships and Warehouse Facilities**

To capture the growth of private credit without the regulatory capital burden of holding high-risk loans, global banks have formed programmatic partnerships and joint ventures with BDCs and private credit managers.1 These arrangements allow banks to refer clients to their private credit partners in exchange for fees and the ability to offer larger "cheque sizes" than their balance sheets might allow.4

## **CLO Holdings and the Risk of BDC Forced Selling**

Banks are the primary institutional holders of the senior-most tranches (AAA and AA) of Collateralized Loan Obligations (CLOs), which are often issued by BDCs and private credit funds to finance their portfolios.10 These holdings provide a stable yield and regulatory capital efficiency, but they link bank balance sheets to the underlying health of the private credit market.

### **Regulatory Leverage and the Forced Deleveraging Trigger**

BDCs are governed by the Investment Company Act of 1940, which limits their leverage to a 2.0x debt-to-equity ratio.10 If the fair value of a BDC's loan portfolio declines—due to rising defaults or a widening of credit spreads—the BDC may be forced to sell assets to maintain its regulatory compliance. Because the private credit market is illiquid, such "forced selling" can trigger a downward price spiral, impacting the valuation of CLO collateral and potentially causing mark-to-market losses for the banks holding the senior tranches.17

## **Risk Parameters and the 2025-2026 Cycle**

The transition of the private credit market into a higher-for-longer interest rate environment is exposing vulnerabilities that were less apparent during the decade of quantitative easing. The "silent pulse" of private credit risk is characterized by several emerging headwinds that will define the 2025-2026 cycle.17

### **The Maturity Wall and Refinancing Risk**

A significant portion of market-based corporate debt is due to mature in the coming years. Risky corporates, particularly those backed by private equity, must refinance at rates that are substantially higher than their original cost of capital.26 This "maturity wall" is expected to increase the stress on private credit portfolios, potentially leading to a spike in default rates from the current sub-1% levels.26 For banks, this means the quality of the "borrowing base" for sub-lines and NAV facilities could deteriorate, requiring more frequent and intensive credit monitoring.4

### **Regulatory Expansion and Transparency as a Premium**

Regulators are increasingly focused on bridging the "regulatory perimeter" between banks and non-banks. The Federal Reserve's Y14 reporting revisions are already forcing banks to provide more granular data on their NDFI interconnectedness.4 In a crisis, capital is expected to fly toward quality and transparency; managers and banks that provide more granular data on portfolio health will likely retain investor and lender confidence.17

### **Conclusion: Systemic Resilience vs. Hidden Fragility**

The convergence of traditional banking and private credit has created a more diversified but more complex financial system. While the probability of a total systemic collapse remains low, the probability of a painful correction is elevated.17 The stability of the system depends on the robustness of the collateral frameworks—the subscription agreements, side letters, and LP commitments—that underpin the $1.2 trillion in bank-to-NDFI lending.5 For the REGINALD watchlist, the mapping of Citizens Financial Group's regional exposure, the sizing of programmatic partnerships like Overland Advantage, and the monitoring of BDC leverage caps are essential for identifying the "silent" risks that could turn a localized correction into a systemic shock. The 2025-2026 window of fragility will test whether the "patient" capital of private credit and the "strong defense" of regional and national banks can withstand the pressures of a refinancing cycle and an opaque leverage environment.17
