# **Liquidity Stress in Private Equity Limited Partners: Structural Shifts, Distribution Droughts, and Credit Transmission Risks**

## **1\. Executive Summary: The Transition from Denominator Effect to Structural Liquidity Gridlock**

The private markets ecosystem is currently navigating its most profound liquidity test since the Global Financial Crisis (GFC). Following a decade of extraordinary capital accumulation fueled by zero-interest-rate policies (ZIRP), the asset class has entered a protracted period of distribution stagnation. As of early 2026, the primary stressor facing Limited Partners (LPs)—including major public pension plans and elite university endowments—has shifted from the relative valuation pressures of the "Denominator Effect" to the absolute cash flow constraints of a "Numerator Crisis."

The core finding of this report is that while systemic capital call defaults remain rare due to severe contractual penalties, liquidity stress is manifesting through alternative channels that pose distinct risks to the banking sector. Specifically, the transmission mechanism of stress has migrated from direct credit default risk to complex, correlated leverage risk. Financial institutions, notably Citizens Financial Group (CFG), have aggressively expanded into the "Fund Finance" void left by the 2023 failures of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) and First Republic Bank (FRB). This expansion includes not only traditional subscription lines but also Net Asset Value (NAV) lending and personal recourse financing for General Partners (GPs) and LPs.

This analysis identifies a bifurcation in LP behavior. Public pension funds, exemplified by CalPERS and the Teacher Retirement System of Texas (TRS), are employing governance engineering—such as the "Total Portfolio Approach"—and tactical pacing reductions to manage illiquidity without realizing losses. In stark contrast, university endowments, including the pioneers of the "Yale Model," are exhibiting signs of acute distress, initiating multi-billion dollar secondary sales to support operating budgets dependent on endowment distributions.

The following report provides an exhaustive examination of these dynamics, utilizing data from pension board minutes, secondary market pricing reports, and bank earnings transcripts to construct a comprehensive risk assessment of the PE/VC liquidity landscape in 2026\.

## ---

**2\. The Macro-Liquidity Environment: Anatomy of the Distribution Drought**

To understand the current behavior of LPs, one must first quantify the breakdown in the "self-funding" mechanics of private equity. Historically, institutional portfolios relied on distributions from mature vintages to fund the capital calls of new commitments. This cycle has been severely disrupted by a "Distribution Drought" that has persisted for over three years.

### **2.1 The Collapse of Distribution to Paid-In Capital (DPI)**

The most critical metric signaling LP stress is the collapse in Distribution to Paid-In Capital (DPI). While Internal Rate of Return (IRR) can be sustained through unrealized valuation marks, DPI represents actual cash returns—the oxygen of the LP ecosystem.

Data indicates that the current liquidity blockage is historic in magnitude. The cumulative DPI for funds from the 2019 through 2022 vintages currently sits at approximately **0.1x**. This figure is **81% lower** than the comparable averages for 2015–2018 vintages at the same stage of their lifecycle.1 This precipitous drop indicates that funds raised during the peak of the post-COVID boom have returned virtually no capital to investors, despite entering the timeframe where early realizations typically occur.

The persistence of this drought is further evidenced by the behavior of specific vintage cohorts. Only **9%** of funds from the 2021 vintage have returned *any* capital to LPs after three years of operations. By comparison, **25%** of the 2017 vintage had initiated distributions at the same three-year mark.2 This extension of the J-curve means LPs are remaining cash-negative for significantly longer durations than their asset-liability models initially projected.

### **2.2 The "Lock-In" Effect and Duration Mismatch**

The root cause of the DPI collapse is the refusal of GPs to sell assets at compressed valuations. In a higher interest rate environment, exit multiples have contracted. Rather than crystallizing these lower valuations—which would damage their track records and ability to raise future funds—GPs have extended holding periods.

The average holding period for buyout assets reached **6.4 years** in 2025\.3 This is significantly higher than the traditional 3-5 year hold model. Bain & Co. analysts have characterized this backlog as a "**5+ year problem**," suggesting that even if public markets reopen fully, the inventory of "zombie" portfolio companies is too large to clear quickly without depressing prices.3

For LPs, this creates a duration mismatch. Institutional investors operate on strict cash flow schedules. Pension funds must pay monthly benefits; endowments must fund annual university operating budgets. When the private equity portfolio ceases to generate cash, these LPs face a "Numerator Crisis"—the absolute value of their PE allocation is high, but the numerator (cash distributions) is near zero. This forces them to seek liquidity through other means, driving the stress signals observed in the secondary and debt markets.

**3\. LP Stress Signals: A Segmented Analysis of Institutional Response**

The reaction to the liquidity drought is not uniform across the LP universe. Our analysis reveals a distinct bifurcation in behavior between Public Pension Funds, who are utilizing their scale and governance flexibility to "bend but not break," and University Endowments, who appear to be "breaking" under the pressure of operating budget dependencies.

### **3.1 Public Pensions: Governance Engineering as a Liquidity Buffer**

Major public pension funds are largely avoiding the "forced seller" designation. Instead of liquidating assets at a discount in the secondary market, they are rewriting their internal rules of engagement to accommodate the illiquidity.

#### **3.1.1 CalPERS: The Strategic Pivot to the Total Portfolio Approach (TPA)**

The California Public Employees' Retirement System (CalPERS) provides the most prominent example of governance engineering. Facing an environment where private market allocations could breach traditional caps due to the denominator effect (in 2022\) or the numerator crisis (current), CalPERS has moved to abolish asset-class-specific constraints.

Effective July 1, 2026, CalPERS will fully implement the **Total Portfolio Approach (TPA)**.4 Under the previous Strategic Asset Allocation (SAA) model, the fund operated with rigid targets for each asset class (e.g., 13% Private Equity target). If the PE allocation grew to 15%—either because public markets fell (denominator effect) or because PE failed to distribute cash (numerator effect)—the Investment Committee would technically be required to halt new commitments or sell assets to rebalance.

The TPA model removes these rigid silos. Instead of managing to a specific percentage of PE, the fund will manage risk at the *total fund* level.6 This is a disguised liquidity management tool. By adopting TPA, CalPERS grants itself the flexibility to hold an overweight position in illiquid assets without triggering policy violations that would necessitate forced secondary sales.

This governance shift is accompanied by a counter-intuitive investment signal: CalPERS is not cutting allocations. The fund has indicated plans to *increase* investments in private markets, viewing the current vintage years as attractive entry points.5 This suggests that CalPERS views its infinite time horizon and contribution inflows as sufficient buffers against the near-term liquidity squeeze. They are effectively "doubling down" to solve the return gap, betting that the eventual recovery in distributions will validate the strategy.

#### **3.1.2 Teacher Retirement System of Texas (TRS): Tactical Slowdowns and Retail Anxiety**

The Teacher Retirement System of Texas (TRS) presents a more nuanced signal of stress, balancing aggressive long-term targets with near-term caution.

**Allocation Overhang:** Currently, TRS allocates **15.9%** of its portfolio to private equity against a target of **12%**.7 This significant overweight position is a direct result of the distribution drought—capital is being called, but older funds are not exiting.

**The "Slow Pacing" Signal:** Unlike CalPERS, which is leaning in, TRS leadership has explicitly acknowledged the need for restraint. CIO Jase Auby and the investment committee have stated they are "slowing the pace" of new commitments to manage this overweight position.8 This is a soft form of allocation cut—not a hard stop, but a reduction in the velocity of new capital deployment to allow the portfolio to naturally de-risk over time.

**The Retail Threat:** A unique and critical insight from TRS board minutes is their growing anxiety regarding **retail capital**.8 The board has expressed concern that as GPs struggle to raise capital from liquidity-constrained institutions, they are pivoting to "democratize" private equity by targeting retail investors (High Net Worth Individuals and 401k plans). TRS views this not as a positive development, but as a systemic risk. Their fear is that retail capital is "hot money"—more prone to panic and redemptions than institutional capital. If GPs become dependent on retail flows, and those flows reverse during a market downturn, it could trigger liquidity gates that trap institutional LPs like TRS. This highlights a sophisticated understanding of transmission risk: the liquidity stress of the *retail* investor could transmit to the *institutional* investor via the shared fund vehicle.

**Gold as a Liquidity Bridge:** Interestingly, TRS has successfully mitigated some liquidity pressure through a tactical allocation to Gold. The fund's commodities sleeve, driven by Gold, returned nearly **60%**, providing a source of liquid, uncorrelated returns that can be monetized to meet capital calls elsewhere in the portfolio.9 This demonstrates the value of diversifiers in a period where traditional stock/bond correlations have faltered.

#### **3.1.3 NYSTRS: The Pivot to Private Debt**

The New York State Teachers' Retirement System (NYSTRS) offers a third model of pension response: strategic substitution. While remaining over-allocated to private equity (9.6% vs. 9% target) 10, NYSTRS has not ceased investing. Instead, they have shifted their pacing plan for 2025 to emphasize **Private Debt**, targeting between **$800 million and $1.2 billion** in new commitments.11

This pivot is strategic. Private Equity buyouts rely on exits (capital gains) for liquidity—precisely the mechanism that is broken. Private Debt, however, generates current income through interest coupons. By shifting the mix toward debt, NYSTRS is attempting to manufacture the cash yield that their buyout portfolio is failing to provide. This "yield substitution" strategy allows them to maintain private market exposure while alleviating the cash flow drag.

### **3.2 The Endowment Crisis: The Structural Failure of the "Yale Model"**

In stark contrast to the managed adaptations of public pensions, elite university endowments are exhibiting signs of acute financial distress. The "Yale Model"—characterized by extreme allocations (\>50%) to illiquid alternatives—appears to be breaking under the pressure of the distribution drought.

#### **3.2.1 The Existential Crisis of Operating Budgets**

The fundamental difference between a pension and an endowment lies in their liability structure. Pensions receive regular cash inflows from employee/employer contributions. Endowments, however, are often the primary funding source for their university's operating budget. For institutions like Harvard, endowment distributions fund nearly **40%** of annual operating expenses.12

When PE distributions stop, the endowment must still pay the university. If the endowment is 50% illiquid and the liquid portion (cash/bonds) is depleted, the institution faces a solvency crisis. This dependency creates a "forcing mechanism" that does not exist for pensions.

#### **3.2.2 Yale and Harvard as Forced Sellers**

Multiple independent reports confirm that **Harvard Management Company** and the **Yale Investments Office**—the progenitors of the endowment model—are actively selling private equity stakes in the secondary market.

* **Harvard:** Is reportedly in discussions to sell **$1 billion** of private equity stakes.13  
* **Yale:** Reports indicate Yale is marketing up to **$6 billion** of private equity assets.14

**Contextualizing the Sale:**

For Yale, with an endowment of approximately $40 billion, a $6 billion sale represents **15% of the total endowment** and a significantly larger fraction of its private equity book. This is not a routine portfolio rebalancing; it is a liquidation event.

**Drivers of the Sale:**

1. **Unfunded Commitments:** Yale and Harvard hold the highest levels of unfunded commitments in the sector, at **$8.1 billion** and **$8.2 billion** respectively.15 These are contractual obligations to provide capital when called by GPs.  
2. **The Liquidity Pincer:** They are caught in a pincer movement. On one side, they must pay billions in capital calls to GPs (who are investing aggressively in a lower-valuation environment). On the other side, they must pay billions to their universities for operations. With no distributions coming in, the math does not work without asset sales.  
3. **Liquidity Risk Rankings:** Independent analyses now place Yale and Harvard near the top of liquidity risk rankings among elite institutions, trailing only Brown University.15

**Insight:** This is the most significant "Stress Signal" in the current market. When the most sophisticated investors in the asset class—those with the deepest relationships and longest time horizons—are forced to sell assets at a discount, it confirms that the liquidity squeeze is structural, not transient. It suggests that for these investors, the "illiquidity premium" has essentially become negative; the cost of holding the asset (liquidity risk) now outweighs the potential return alpha.

### **3.3 The Retail Pivot: A Desperate Search for Capital?**

As institutional capital (Endowments) retreats and pensions (TRS) slow pacing, GPs are aggressively targeting the **Private Wealth / Retail** channel to fill the funding gap.

* **Mechanism:** GPs are launching "semi-liquid" evergreen funds designed to tap into 401(k) plans and High Net Worth Individuals (HNWIs).16  
* **Risk Signal:** This migration to retail is a classic late-cycle signal. Sophisticated institutional money is exiting (via secondaries), and retail money is entering. For the banking sector, this introduces a new risk vector: retail investors are notoriously more fickle and prone to panic-redemptions than institutions. If a market shock occurs, the "semi-liquid" promise of these new funds could be tested. If GPs gate redemptions (as seen with BREIT in previous cycles), it could damage the franchise value of the GPs and cut off the last remaining source of new capital.

**4\. Secondary Market Dynamics: The Thermometer of Stress**

The secondary market serves as the real-time pricing mechanism for private equity liquidity. The dynamics observed in 2025/2026 reveal a market that is active, liquid, but deeply bifurcated based on asset quality.

### **4.1 Volume and the Sellers' Composition**

Secondary transaction volume reached a record **$162 billion** in 2024 and accelerated further in the first half of 2025\.17

* **LP Dominance:** In a shift from previous years where GP-led "continuation funds" dominated, **54%** of volume in 2025 was driven by LP sales.18 This confirms that the pressure is originating from the limited partners' need for cash.  
* **First-Time Sellers:** Perhaps the most telling statistic is that **40%** of LP sellers in 2024 were transacting for the first time.17 This indicates that liquidity stress has widened beyond the usual churn of active portfolio managers to include LPs who historically held to maturity. The stigma of selling secondaries has evaporated; it is now a survival mechanism.

### **4.2 Pricing Bifurcation: A Flight to Quality**

While overall volumes are high, pricing tells a story of extreme selectivity.

* **Buyout Portfolios:** Premium buyout portfolios are trading at approximately **94% of NAV**.19 This relatively healthy pricing reflects a "survivor bias"—only the highest quality portfolios with blue-chip GPs are clearing. Buyers are willing to pay up for known quantities.  
* **Venture Capital:** While recovering from lows, VC portfolios are trading at **75% of NAV**.19 This implies that LPs are willing to accept a massive **25% haircut** to exit their venture exposure. This deep discount confirms the "Endowment Crisis" narrative, as endowments are typically the heaviest allocators to VC.  
* **Real Estate:** Trading at **72% of NAV** (28% discount).20 This signals deep distress in the real asset portfolios, likely driven by commercial real estate valuation uncertainties.

### **4.3 Structural Innovation: Deferrals and Preferred Equity**

To bridge the gap between what sellers need (cash) and what buyers will pay, the market is utilizing complex structures.

* **Deferred Payments:** Approximately **20%** of LP transactions now involve deferred pricing, where the seller accepts a portion of the purchase price at a later date.17 This allows the seller to achieve a higher "headline price" (e.g., 90% of NAV) while the buyer improves their internal rate of return (IRR) by delaying cash outflows.  
* **Preferred Equity:** This structure allows an LP to raise cash against their portfolio without selling the underlying stakes, essentially taking a loan against the portfolio.17

**Insight:** The narrowing bid-ask spread and recovering pricing (to \~90% overall) should not be misinterpreted as a return to health. Rather, it is a sign of **capitulation**. LPs have accepted that the valuations of 2021 are gone. They are now willing to sell at 90 cents on the dollar to secure the liquidity needed to keep their programs afloat.

**5\. Capital Call Default Analysis: The Dog That Hasn't Barked (Yet)**

A central question for systemic risk monitoring is whether LPs are defaulting on their capital calls. A default by a major LP would be catastrophic for a fund, triggering cross-defaults in subscription lines and potentially collapsing the GP's franchise.

### **5.1 Default Rates: Low Incidence, High Consequence**

Currently, there is **no evidence of widespread LP capital call defaults** in the public data or industry surveys.21 The reported default rates (e.g., Proskauer's 2.46% 22) refer to *portfolio company* defaults (borrowers failing to pay the fund), not LPs failing to pay the GP.

The absence of defaults is explained by the draconian legal and economic penalties structured into Limited Partnership Agreements (LPAs):

* **The "Nuclear Option":** LPAs typically contain "forfeiture" clauses. If an LP defaults on a capital call, the GP can seize up to **100%** of the LP's existing Net Asset Value (NAV) in the fund to cure the default.23  
* **Sunk Cost Psychology:** For an LP that has already contributed millions of dollars, defaulting on a new call would mean wiping out that entire investment. This creates a massive "sunk cost" incentive to pay, even if the LP is liquidity constrained.  
* **Lender Remedies:** Lenders to the funds (providing subscription lines) often have step-in rights. As noted by Aegon Asset Management, if an LP defaults, the lender can "take over the stake" and sell it in the secondary market.24

### **5.2 The "Shadow" Default: The Rise of Personal Leverage**

The most alarming signal regarding LP liquidity is not explicit defaults, but the desperate measures LPs are taking to *avoid* default. The system is witnessing a surge in **personal leverage**.

* **Citizens Private Bank Activity:** Citizens Financial Group (CFG) is actively pitching "elastic loans" collateralized by GP and LP interests.25  
* **Target Audience:** These loans are targeted at "cash-strapped investors" in VC and PE funds.  
* **Use of Proceeds:** Crucially, the proceeds are being used for **personal liquidity**—paying tax bills, funding mortgages, and maintaining lifestyles.25 This confirms that the lack of distributions is impacting the personal solvency of the individuals who manage and invest in these funds.

**Systemic Implication:**

This converts liquidity risk into hidden credit risk. If an LP borrows from CFG to pay a capital call to a Fund (which also borrows from CFG via a subscription line), the bank is effectively leveraging the same underlying equity twice. The LP is leveraging their stake to stay in the fund, and the fund is leveraging the LP's commitment to buy assets. This "leverage-on-leverage" structure is highly correlated. If the underlying fund performance falters, the collateral for both the personal loan and the fund loan deteriorates simultaneously.

**6\. Transmission Risk to Citizens Financial Group (CFG)**

The analysis identifies Citizens Financial Group (CFG) as a primary vector for the transmission of private equity risks to the banking system. Following the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) and First Republic Bank (FRB) in 2023, CFG aggressively positioned itself to capture their market share, thereby concentrating sector risk.

### **6.1 The Aggressive Pivot to Fund Finance**

CFG has explicitly identified "Fund Finance" and Private Equity/VC banking as a key growth engine to offset sluggish loan demand in other sectors.

* **Talent Acquisition:** CFG executed a massive talent lift, hiring the core "Private Equity Finance" and "Venture Capital" teams from the failed SVB and FRB franchises. This includes the hiring of **Scott Aleali** and a team of over 50 bankers in New York and San Francisco.26 These bankers brought their client relationships—and their specific lending models—to CFG.  
* **Product Suite Expansion:** CFG is not limiting itself to low-risk subscription lines (which are secured by unfunded commitments). They are expanding into higher-risk areas:  
  * **NAV Lending:** Lending against the residual value of portfolio assets.27  
  * **GP/LP Personal Finance:** Recourse loans to individuals secured by fund carry or LP interests.25

### **6.2 The Transmission Mechanism: A "Ponzi" Finance Risk?**

The core risk for CFG is that it is financing a system that has temporarily stopped generating cash. The bank is providing the liquidity that the market (exits) is refusing to provide.

**The Risk Scenario:**

1. **Stage 1 (Current State):** PE Funds cannot exit companies. Distributions stop.  
2. **Stage 2 (Bridge Financing):** LPs run out of cash. They borrow personally from CFG (Elastic Loans) to meet capital calls. Funds borrow from CFG (NAV Loans) to pay artificial distributions to keep LPs happy.  
3. **Stage 3 (The Break):** If the exit drought continues through 2026/2027, the personal leverage reaches capacity. The interest carry on the loans consumes the borrowers' remaining liquidity.  
4. **Stage 4 (Correlation Event):** LPs begin to default on the personal loans. Simultaneously, the Funds (seeing no exits) breach covenants on the NAV loans.  
5. **Stage 5 (Impact):** CFG is left holding collateral (LP interests and private company equity) that is illiquid and potentially overvalued. Because CFG is the lender to *both* the Fund and the LP, the diversification benefit is illusory.

**Current Health Assessment:** Despite this theoretical risk, CFG currently reports strong credit quality. Kroll Bond Rating Agency recently revised CFG's outlook to "Positive," citing its high-quality balance sheet.28 The bank views the "Fund Finance" vertical as a source of high-quality assets. This suggests that *currently*, the collateral coverage is sufficient. The risk is strictly a "tail risk"—a scenario where private valuations reprice downward sharply, putting the loans underwater before the bank can liquidate the collateral.

**7\. Fund Extensions and Restructuring: The Structural Mutation**

As exits fail to materialize, the structure of private equity funds is mutating. The standard "10-year term" is becoming a suggestion rather than a rule.

### **7.1 The Rise of Continuation Funds (CVs)**

GP-led Continuation Funds have become the standard solution for the liquidity drought.

* **Volume:** Continuation funds accounted for **14%** of all sponsor-backed exit volume in H1 2024\.29  
* **Mechanism:** The GP sells assets from an older fund (e.g., "Fund V") to a new "Continuation Fund" managed by the *same* GP. New LPs (secondary buyers) provide the cash to pay out the old LPs who want to exit.  
* **Implication:** This is not a true exit; it is an asset transfer. It solves the *LP's* liquidity problem but does not prove the *asset's* valuation in an open market (like an IPO). It keeps the fees flowing to the GP and resets the clock on the investment.

### **7.2 NAV Lending as "Dividend Recaps 2.0"**

There is a rising trend of funds utilizing **NAV Loans** to pay distributions to LPs.27

* **Mechanism:** Instead of leveraging the portfolio company (a traditional dividend recap), the GP leverages the *fund itself*, using the diversified pool of assets as collateral.  
* **Purpose:** To artificially generate DPI. By borrowing cash to send to LPs, the GP improves their distribution metrics, which helps them raise their *next* fund.  
* **Risk:** This adds a layer of leverage that is cross-collateralized. If one or two major assets in the fund fail, it can drag down the entire portfolio.

### **7.3 Bureaucratic Extensions**

Explicit fund term extensions are appearing with increasing frequency. Notifications of "Fund Term Extension" are being issued for various private market funds to regulators and platforms.31 This is the bureaucratic acknowledgment that the assets cannot be sold within the standard life of the partnership.

**8\. Conclusion and Outlook**

### **8.1 Assessment of LP Stress**

The signals of LP stress are **High** and **Divergent**.

* **Public Pensions** are masking stress through pacing reductions and governance hacks (TPA). They are "bending, not breaking."  
* **Endowments** are "breaking." The active, multi-billion dollar selling by Yale and Harvard is a definitive signal that the liquidity model for the most sophisticated allocators has failed in this cycle. The dependency on endowment draws for university operations has exposed a fatal flaw in the ultra-illiquid "Yale Model."

### **8.2 Assessment of Capital Call Default Risk**

**Low Probability, High Impact.** While actual defaults remain rare due to severe penalties, the *capacity* of LPs to fund is strained. The system is relying on secondary sales and personal leverage to meet calls. This is a fragile equilibrium maintained by the expectation of a near-term exit recovery.

### **8.3 Transmission Risk to CFG**

**Moderate to High.** CFG has aggressively positioned itself as the "lender of last resort" for the PE ecosystem following the collapse of SVB/FRB.

* **Bull Case:** IPO markets reopen fully in 2026, DPI flows return, and CFG profits immensely from high-spread loans and new client acquisition.  
* **Bear Case:** The "5+ year" drought persists. The correlation between GP personal loans, LP liquidity, and Fund credit lines exposes CFG to a concentrated sector shock. The bank is effectively "shorting" liquidity volatility in the private markets.

### **8.4 Recommendations for Monitoring**

1. **Watch the "Yale Sale":** The final pricing of Yale's $6B portfolio sale will set the "mark-to-market" for the entire industry. A steep discount (\>20%) could trigger margin calls on NAV loans across the ecosystem.  
2. **Monitor CFG's "Fund Finance" Non-Accruals:** Any uptick in this specific vertical in Q1/Q2 2026 earnings would signal that the "personal leverage" bridge is collapsing.  
3. **Retail Gates:** Watch for redemption suspensions in the new wave of "semi-liquid" retail PE funds. If retail investors panic, the new capital source for GPs will dry up, exacerbating the drought for institutions.
