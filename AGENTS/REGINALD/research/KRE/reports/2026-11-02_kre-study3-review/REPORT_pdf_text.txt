KRE Study 3 — When do falling Treasury yields help or hurt regional banks? (with the rate-beta flip)
Data through the 10/30/2026 close · prepared 11/02/2026


Bottom line
    1. Falling yields have not been a reliable buy signal for KRE. Since June 2006, falling-yield windows were followed by slightly below-baseline 60-session KRE returns (−0.2% to +0.7% vs a +2.0% baseline; not significant). The
       one result that holds across window lengths, thresholds, and the removal of the COVID and SVB periods is relative: after the 2-year yield falls, KRE has tended to lag KBWB over the next 60 sessions (−1.9% to −2.2%
       vs a −0.5% baseline; Welch t ≈ −2.3 to −3.0; 25 windows in each grid, 14–15 separate episodes, 2012–2026 only). It is weaker for the 10-year.
    2. The credit split I set up in advance does not reliably separate a “constructive” rally from a “stress rebound”. Rallies where credit narrowed did no better afterwards than rallies where it widened (at W=63, the
       “constructive” cell even had a worse next 20 sessions for the 10-year). The one consistent sign: stress rebounds (KRE up, spreads wider) were followed by KRE lagging IWM over 60 sessions in most variants. But n is 5–10 per cell,
       the effect shrinks to about −2% outside the GFC, and the alternative thresholds leave 1–3 cases. The data cannot say which kind of rally this is with any confidence. That is the honest answer to the central question.
    3. The rate-beta flip is real, but it is neither new nor specific to KRE. KRE’s 63-session sensitivity to the 10-year (controlling for SPY) is −0.062 per 1pp. That is the 4th percentile since 2012 and the 4th percentile since
       2006. A negative beta was normal in 2006–2011, absent in 2013–2021, and has been the majority state since August 2023 (57% of days; record low −0.157 in May 2024). KBWB’s rate beta is negative too (−0.031, 4th
       percentile), and the two rolling betas correlate at 0.98. So this is a bank-sector regime, not a KRE-only one. What is unusual is a negative beta during a Fed hiking phase: in 2015–18 it was never negative, and in 2022–23
       it was negative on only 19% of days.
    4. Where 10/2026 sits: the trailing 20-session window (10/2–10/30) falls in the pre-defined “stress rebound” cell. The 2-year fell 27bp and the 10-year 34bp (a bull flattener). KRE rose 1.7% and beat KBWB, while the Baa
       spread, HYG-vs-IEF and HY OAS all widened. The 10/14–10/29 fall in the 10-year was entirely real yield; 10-year breakevens were unchanged (FRED DFII10/T10YIE). That fits growth fear or safe-haven buying, not
       disinflation. A 10/22-type day (10-year −17bp, SPY −1.2%, KRE +2.5%) has one precedent since 2006: 1/22/2008. The closest mechanical analogs are the post-2023 “AOCI-relief” rallies (Dec 2023, May 2024, Jan 2025, Sep
       2025, Feb 2026, Jun 2026). Five of those six had negative forward 60-session returns. Across all 10 post-2012 analogs, 7 were negative and the mean was −4.3%, against a +2.0% base. This is descriptive only, and the cases
       overlap with known shocks (tariffs in 2025).
    5. Cross-section (167 KRE names, June-2026 call reports): within KRE, sensitivity to the 10-year runs through size, CRE concentration, loan/deposit ratio and deposit costs. It does not run through securities losses.
       Banks with bigger HTM losses relative to Tier 1 did better on both falling- and rising-yield days. So the earlier “bigger HTM losses → bigger rally” result is general outperformance, not specific rate relief. Thin sample: only 8 falling
       and 14 rising days of ≥5bp since 7/1.

Net read. The current rally looks more like a flight-to-quality rebound inside a hiking cycle than a constructive easing rally. History gives a mild lean toward KRE giving back relative performance (against KBWB, and probably against
IWM) over the next ~60 sessions. It gives no reliable edge on KRE’s absolute direction. I would not call it “constructive” or “temporary” on this evidence.

Data limitations that bind this study

       HY OAS history is truncated. The free FRED CSV for BAMLH0A0HYM2 now starts on 2023-11-03. The FRED series page says that “starting in April 2026, this series will only include 3 years of observations” (ICE licence).
       ALFRED vintages returned HTTP 503 from this environment on 11/02. HY OAS is therefore used only for the current episode. Long-history classification uses two proxies, fixed in advance:
                    i. Moody’s Baa − 10y (FRED BAA10Y), from 1986. This is the primary measure.
                   ii. HYG − IEF total-return difference, from April 2007, as a cross-check.
              In the 2023-11 → 2026-10 overlap, 20-day changes in HY OAS correlate +0.65 with ΔBAA10Y and −0.76 with HYG−IEF. But the direction agrees only 70% and 73% of the time. These are rough stand-ins, and they are the
              largest single weakness of sub-question 1.
       KBWB starts on 2011-10-20. Every “vs KBWB” statistic covers 2012–2026 only (59 of 80 W=63 windows; 188 of 255 W=20 windows).
       KRE starts on 2006-06-22. So the sample has one full hiking cycle (2015–18), one fast one (2022–23), and the start of the 2026 phase.
       FDIC call reports for 9/30/2026 are not loaded (the API returned 0 records for REPDTE=20260930 on 11/02). The cross-section uses 6/30/2026.
1. Historical regimes: falling yields on non-overlapping windows
Method (fixed before any forward return was computed; full text in PREREGISTERED_SPEC.md).

        Windows: non-overlapping blocks of 20 and 63 sessions, anchored so the last block ends on 10/30/2026.
        Falling yields: Δ2y < 0 or Δ10y < 0 over the window, with the two tenors reported separately.
        Classifications:
                    a. KRE return > 0;
                    b. Δ(Baa−10y) > 0 = credit widening (HYG−IEF < 0 as a cross-check);
                     c. Δ(10y−2y) > 0 = steepening;
                    d. KRE − KBWB > 0.
        Forward returns: the next 20 and 60 sessions from the window’s end close.
        Overlap control: for W=20 with a 60-session horizon, only every third window is used, so forward periods never overlap. The other two phases are shown in Table 1f.
        Statistics: the bracket shows the difference from the unconditional baseline, meaning all windows on the same grid. ** means |Welch t| ≥ 2 for the cell against its complement (computed only when n ≥ 10). † means n < 10:
        too thin to support a conclusion.
        Pre-defined labels: CONSTRUCTIVE = falling yields + KRE up + credit narrowing. STRESS REBOUND = falling yields + KRE up + credit widening.

How to read the tables: “n (ep.) 20d” gives windows with a forward-20 return, the number of separate episodes (runs of consecutive windows) in brackets, and then the count with KBWB data. Returns are in %. A bracket is the
difference from baseline in pp.

What sub-question 1 shows

      Unconditional base rates. KRE’s next-60-session return averaged +2.0% (positive 56–59% of the time) on both grids. KRE − KBWB averaged −0.5% (positive 40–46%), reflecting KRE’s long-run lag. Every cell has to be
      compared with these numbers, not with zero.

      Falling yields alone barely change the absolute outlook. Forward-60 KRE after falling-yield windows was −0.2% to +0.7%, against the +2.0% base. That is 1.4–2.2pp worse, but no grid or tenor reaches |t| = 2.

      Relative to KBWB, the effect is consistent.

              The alternative thresholds give the same sign (−2.3% to −2.9% for the 2-year).
              Dropping windows whose forward period overlaps COVID (Feb–Apr 2020) or SVB (Mar–May 2023) strengthens it (W63, 2y: −2.5% vs +0.8% for the other windows, t −3.1).
              The median is also negative (−1.7%).
              The biggest contributors are the windows ending Jan 2024 (−13.6pp) and Apr 2025 (−8.9pp). Both are rallies driven by falling yields that later reversed.

      KRE rallies during falling-yield windows are the cases that later lag KBWB. “Falling & KRE up” gave forward-60 KRE − KBWB of −2.4% (W63, 2y, t −2.3), −3.2% (W20, 2y, t −2.6) and −3.5% (W20, 10y, t −2.9). The
      alternative thresholds give the same sign with 2–4 cases.

What sub-question 2 shows

        The current value is extreme on two measures. KRE’s rate beta is −0.062: the 3.7th percentile since 2006 and the 4.2th since 2012. Standardized (beta × σΔy / σr), it is −0.33, the 1.5th percentile. The KRE SPY beta in
        the same model is 0.24, the 1.3th percentile.
        The sign is not new.
                A negative beta was common in 2006–2011 (46–73% of days per year), but small in size. In the GFC/ZIRP era, falling yields came with bank stress.
                It was never negative in 2013–2021. That decade produced the “+0.07 median” in the brief.
                It returned in late 2022 and has been the majority state since August 2023 (57% of days; 58% of 2024; 63% of 2026 so far).
                The record low (−0.157) came in May 2024, after SVB. In that regime, higher yields meant bigger unrealized securities losses and funding pressure, so regionals traded like long-duration assets.
        It is a bank-wide regime. KBWB’s rate beta is also negative (−0.031, 4.4th percentile), and the two rolling series correlate at 0.98. KRE is somewhat more negative: the KRE − KBWB beta gap is −0.031, the 8th percentile.
        That is consistent with the decoupling from study 1, but it is a difference of degree.
        Hiking cycles.
                2015–18: the beta was always positive (median +0.12). Higher rates meant NIM expansion.
                2022–23: median +0.02, negative on 19% of days. It turned negative from Sept 2022, as AOCI losses grew.
                SVB itself (Mar–May 2023): the beta was positive (+0.13). KRE crashed while yields fell, which is the opposite of today. A negative rate beta is therefore not a crisis signature. In the acute 2023 bank run, KRE fell on
                falling yields.
                Overall, P(beta < 0 | hiking) = 8% vs 33% outside hiking. A strongly negative beta during a hiking phase, as in 2026, has almost no precedent in this sample. The closest is late 2022 (−0.03 at worst).
        Does a negative beta precede drawdowns? On the non-overlapping 63-session grid, b < 0 (n=20, 14 of them from 2006–2012) was followed by:
                forward-60 KRE of −0.9%, against a +2.0% base (t −1.1);
                KRE − IWM of −3.6% (t −1.9);
                a forward max drawdown of −14.3%, against a −13.2% base: no difference.
        With b < −0.03 (n = 8, mostly GFC), forward-60 is −8.6% and the drawdown −20%, but that sample is too thin and too GFC-heavy to use. Verdict: a weak tendency toward relative underperformance, and no evidence
        of a larger drawdown.

       Classification.
              20-session window (10/2 → 10/30): falling 2y (−27bp) and 10y (−34bp), KRE up (+1.7%), KRE beat KBWB (+0.8pp), Baa wider (+2bp), HYG < IEF (−2.4pp), HY OAS +34bp, 2s10s −7bp. That is STRESS REBOUND, with
              bull flattening and KRE outperforming KBWB.
              63-session window (8/3 → 10/30): not a falling-yield window. Both yields rose (2y +31bp, 10y +24bp). The rally is a partial retracement inside a rising-yield quarter.
       10/22 is close to unique. Only one other day since 2006 had the 10-year down ≥10bp, SPY down ≥1% and KRE up ≥1.5%: 1/22/2008, the day of the Fed’s emergency 75bp cut, whose effective date in FRED DFEDTAR is 2008-
       01-22. More broadly, on days when the 10-year fell ≥10bp, KRE rose on 0% of such days in most years from 2012 to 2021, against 47–100% since 2022. Regionals rallying on a bond rally is a post-2022 behaviour.
       Which episodes it resembles most, and why:
           1. Post-2023 “AOCI-relief” rallies: Dec 2023, May 2024, Jan 2025, Sep 2025, Feb 2026, Jun 2026. The mechanism is the same: KRE rallies because yields fall. The rate beta was already negative in five of the six;
              Jan 2025 is the exception.
              Forward-60 KRE returns: −7.9%, +5.0%, −14.6%, −4.2%, −2.5% and −3.2%. That is 5 of 6 negative, mean −4.6%.
              Four of the six lagged KBWB, Dec 2023 by −13.9pp. May 2024, the most negative-beta case (b −0.150), is the counter-example: +5.0% absolute and +5.3pp against KBWB.
              Jan 2025 ran into the tariff shock, so it is not a clean read.
           2. Late-2007 / Jan-2008 flight-to-quality rebounds. These had the same market signature: falling yields, wider credit, KRE up while SPY fell, and in Jan 2008 the 10/22-type day itself. Forward-60 returns were −16.9%
              and −1.5%. The difference is important: then, bank credit was the source of the stress. Now the stress comes from outside banking (AI capex, France, the Middle East). Note that HY OAS at 3.44 is near the top of its FRED-
              available 2023–26 range (2.59–4.61); it was wider than on 88% of days since Nov 2023. Pre-2023 levels cannot be checked from FRED’s free series, so I cannot say whether 3.44 is high or low in long-run terms.
           3. Not like SVB (Mar 2023). Then, yields fell and KRE crashed, and the rate beta was positive.
           4. Not like 2015–18 hiking. Then, regionals rose with yields.
       The current episode also differs from every analog in one way. The Fed is hiking: +25bp on 9/16/2026, a hold with two hike dissents on 10/28, and a December hike reportedly in play. In earlier falling-yield rallies the Fed
       was cutting or on hold. A hiking Fed caps how far the front end can fall, so the rate-relief channel is more fragile.

What sub-question 4 shows

       Size is the rate exposure inside KRE. Larger KRE banks gained more on falling-yield days (+9.6bp per SD, t 3.2) and lost more on rising-yield days (−5.2bp, t −3.1). Their relative rate beta is more negative (t −2.5 to −4.0
       across the robustness variants). This matches study 1’s “size = beta” finding and points to the same channel that makes KBWB’s beta negative.
       CRE concentration, loan/deposit ratio and deposit cost predict rising-yield-day losses. The t-values are −2.1 to −2.7, and the rate-beta t for CRE is −1.9 to −2.9 across variants. The within-KRE rate channel looks like
       credit and funding (“higher for longer hurts CRE borrowers and deposit costs”), not securities marks.
       Securities losses work the “wrong” way for an AOCI story. Larger unrealized losses relative to T1 come with a less negative relative rate beta (t ≈ −2.0 to −2.4, where a negative coefficient means bigger losses give a
       higher beta).
       HTM loss / T1 was associated with better returns on falling days (t −2.2, same sign convention) and on rising days (t −1.6). The fall-minus-rise difference is insignificant (t −1.0). The prior “bigger HTM losses → bigger
       rally” result is better read as general outperformance of those names than as rate relief.
       Caveats. 8–22 event days; 9 traits × 4 outcomes; earnings-season days fall inside the window. The robustness variants were dropping 10/22, using a ±3bp threshold, and using an Apr–Oct window. Across them, size (|t| 2.5–4.0),
       securities loss (|t| 2.0–2.4) and CRE (|t| 1.9–2.9) keep their sign and size for the relative rate beta, while deposit cost and loan/deposit weaken. Re-run on 9/30 call reports when FDIC loads them.



5. Confidence & gaps
Where I’m fairly confident

       The re-verified market and macro facts (§0). The Fed actions are confirmed in Fed press releases.
       The rate beta is at an extreme (1.5th–4th percentile). It is shared with KBWB, so it is not KRE-specific. A negative sign has been the norm since Aug 2023, but it is rare during a hiking phase.
       Falling yields, and KRE rallies within them, have been followed by KRE lagging KBWB over ~60 sessions. This holds on both grids for the 2-year, survives the alternative thresholds and the COVID/SVB exclusion, and the median
       agrees.

Where I’m not confident

       Whether today’s rally is “constructive” or a “temporary rebound.” The pre-defined credit split does not separate outcomes robustly. Cells are 5–12 windows; results flip with the grid phase (Table 1f); the alternative
       thresholds leave 1–3 cases; and 2008 dominates the stress-rebound cells.
       The credit measure itself. HY OAS history is unavailable from FRED’s free series, and the two proxies agree with HY OAS on direction only about 70% of the time. A licensed full ICE BofA HY OAS history, or ALFRED once it is
       reachable, could re-classify a quarter of the windows. This is the single change most likely to alter sub-question 1’s credit results.
       The analog scan (Table 3b) is exploratory. The rule was chosen to resemble the current episode, the forward periods include exogenous shocks, and 7 of 18 cases are from 2007–09.
       The cross-section rests on 8 falling days, and the FDIC mapping relies on name matching with 14 manual fixes. A mis-mapped bank would add noise rather than bias.

Where sources or data disagreed

       The brief’s SPY beta (0.21) and R² (0.17) differ from mine (0.24, 0.15). The rate coefficient matches (−0.061 vs −0.062). The likely cause is the yield source or window alignment; it does not change any conclusion.
       The brief’s “historical median +0.070” is a since-2012 median. The full-history (2006) median is +0.042.
       The press gives slightly different 10-year closes for 10/22 (CNBC 4.972%; Treasury par 4.96%). I use Treasury’s par curve throughout.

Claims resting only on secondary sources

       That futures price a December hike (CNBC).
       The names of the 10/28 dissenters (CNBC, J.P. Morgan AM; the Fed statement in hand shows only the 10–2 vote).
       The narrative causes of 10/22: the AI selloff, French fiscal stress and Mideast tensions (CNBC, Bloomberg).
       The market data for 10/22 is primary (Treasury, Yahoo prices).

What would change the conclusion

             1. Full HY OAS history re-classifying the credit cells.
             2. Breakevens and the front end starting to fall together, with HY OAS tightening, which would move the episode toward the CONSTRUCTIVE cell.
             3. The rate beta turning positive again (yields rising while KRE rises), which would mark a return to the NIM regime.
             4. 9/30 call reports showing deposit-cost or CRE deterioration among the names that led the rally.
             5. For study 4: this study suggests that low correlation plus a negative rate beta overlaps heavily with “KRE rallies on falling yields”. Any low-correlation predictive test should control for that regime.
