# 1. Executive Dashboard and System-Wide Overview
The Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) System occupies a pivotal yet increasingly complex position in the U.S. financial architecture. Originally designed to support residential mortgage lending through thrift institutions, the System has evolved into a liquidity backstop for a broad spectrum of commercial banks, credit unions, and life insurance companies. Following the extreme liquidity demands of the 2023 regional banking turmoil, system-wide advance balances contracted and then re-accelerated sharply in 2026.
Primary Office of Finance data establish the following trajectory:

Insurance share from 2026 Q1 Combined Financial Report (Advances by Member Type) and Federal Reserve Z.1 data.
The sharp rise in advances from $676.7 billion at year-end 2025 to $810.7 billion by mid-2026 (+20%) was driven primarily by increased borrowings from large members, according to the Office of Finance. Insurance companies, while representing only ~7.7% of total FHLB member institutions, accounted for 27.0% of all outstanding advances as of Q1 2026.

# 2. District-Level Analysis and Borrower Concentration
Concentration risks vary significantly across the eleven individual FHLBanks, reflecting regional member profiles and tailored business strategies. District-level disclosures reveal that specific FHLBanks have become concentrated liquidity hubs for major depository institutions and private equity-owned insurance groups.
## FHLBank of Chicago
The FHLBank of Chicago experienced significant asset expansion in early 2026. Total assets reached $163.2 billion in Q2 2026 (up from $141.2 billion at year-end 2025) and advances rose to $71.1 billion. Secondary reporting indicates elevated concentration among the top five borrowers, with insurance entities among the largest. Strong earnings continue, but reliance on a narrow group of large borrowers creates potential income volatility if key members adjust funding mix.
Source: FHLBank Chicago Q2 2026 Financial Highlights (July 22, 2026) and Office of Finance Combined Operating Highlights Table I.
## FHLBank of San Francisco
Borrower concentration at FHLBank of San Francisco remains among the highest in the System, though precise 2025 figures are limited.
- Top-5 borrower percentage: Not disclosed in the 2025 Form 10-K. The Bank states it maintains “a high concentration of advances and capital with five institutions and their affiliates” but does not quantify the share.
- Top-10 borrower percentage: 50.4% as of year-end 2024 (most recent publicly available precise figure). The 2025 Form 10-K does not publish an updated top-ten percentage.
- Single largest / >10% exposure: JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. held advances and capital stock each exceeding 10% of the Bank’s respective totals. JPMorgan is not a member of the Bank and therefore cannot replace maturing advances.
- Portfolio composition (YE 2025): Weighted toward large commercial banks ($16.7 billion) and credit unions ($14.7 billion). No insurance-specific breakout is disclosed.
Source: FHLBank of San Francisco 2025 Form 10-K. Top-ten figure reflects YE 2024 disclosure only.
## FHLBank of Des Moines
The FHLBank of Des Moines functions as the primary borrowing hub for Apollo Global Management’s insurance footprint. Athene’s Iowa-domiciled subsidiary is the single largest borrower in the district, accounting for approximately 21% of total advances outstanding at year-end 2025 according to district disclosures. Athene held $23.3 billion in FHLB advances at YE 2025 and $28.2 billion system-wide by Q1 2026. This structural concentration makes Des Moines highly sensitive to developments in the life insurance and annuity sectors.
Source: Athene Holding 10-K FY 2025; FHLB Des Moines disclosures; Bloomberg citing Office of Finance data (Q1 2026).
## FHLBank of Indianapolis
The FHLBank of Indianapolis exhibits the highest structural exposure to insurance members in the System. It serves as the primary funding conduit for Mark Walter’s insurance network (Group 1001 / TWG Global), including Delaware Life Insurance Company and Clear Spring Life and Annuity Company. As of mid-2026, Group 1001’s two primary insurance units held in excess of $6 billion in outstanding advances from FHLBank Indianapolis — a 36% surge over six months and roughly double the level at the start of 2025. Delaware Life has operated near maximum capacity, with utilization reported at approximately 97% of allowable credit capacity at year-end 2025 (secondary reporting citing statutory filings).
Source: Bloomberg (September 2, 2026) citing statutory filings and district data; FHLBank Indianapolis Form 10-Qs (top-borrower tables).

Affiliate Reclassification and Related Scrutiny
In mid-2026, Delaware Life Insurance Company restated its statutory financial statements, reclassifying approximately $17 billion to $20 billion of private credit investments from “unaffiliated” to “affiliated” status. Reported affiliated investments jumped from 3% to 42% of total invested assets at year-end 2025.

## Asset Encumbrance and Policyholder Subordination
In the event of an insurer insolvency, the statutory super-senior lien of the lending FHLBank (12 U.S.C. § 1430(e)) gives it priority claim on the pledged high-grade collateral. The remaining estate available to policyholders, annuity holders, and state guaranty funds would consist primarily of any unencumbered (and potentially less liquid) assets, including private credit exposures. This structural priority is a documented feature of the FHLB advance framework and is relevant to assessing residual risk to policyholders.
# 4. Regulatory and Supervisory Status
## Implementation of Advisory Bulletin AB 2024-03
The supervisory reform framework established by FHFA Advisory Bulletin AB 2024-03 remains in transition. Implementation status and examination outcomes are not publicly disclosed; FHFA does not publish individual FHLBank exam ratings or horizontal review results.
What is publicly documented is the System’s resistance to the directive: in an April 2026 position letter, the Council of Federal Home Loan Banks argued that AB 2024-03 “introduces operational ambiguity, restricts through-the-cycle liquidity, and risks forcing struggling institutions onto Federal Reserve emergency discount facilities during broader financial stress.”
The FHFA Office of Inspector General’s FY2025 and FY2026 Annual Plans identify FHLBank member credit risk management and third-party exposure as oversight priorities. However, no public evidence currently confirms actual changes in lending behavior toward watch-list or lower-rated members. Any assertion of reform impact should be framed as pending rather than demonstrated.
Footnote: Exam ratings and horizontal review findings are non-public. Statements regarding AB 2024-03 implementation should be limited to documented System commentary and OIG priority-setting, not inferred supervisory outcomes.
## GAO Findings (GAO-26-107373)
The December 2025 GAO study documented a dual dynamic: for small banks (under $10 billion, ~97% of active bank members), higher FHLB borrowing was associated with positive outcomes (more real-estate lending, lower failure risk). Large banks (~3% of members) accounted for approximately 74% of bank advance volume. GAO also reiterated the long-standing concern that rapid spikes in advances by distressed large institutions can temporarily mask underlying problems and shift resolution costs.
# 5. Collateral, SBLOCs, and Off-Balance-Sheet Exposure
Standby Letters of Credit (SBLOCs): A system-wide aggregate SBLOC figure is not published as a single clean primary table by the Office of Finance with consistent definitions. Individual FHLBank 10-Qs disclose outstanding SBLOCs under commitments and contingencies. District-level examples (e.g., FHLBank Indianapolis) show outstanding SBLOC balances in the $1.2–1.3 billion range in recent periods; broader “commitment” line items can be substantially larger because they incorporate facility headroom. Insurance-linked SBLOC sub-totals are generally not broken out.
Collateral haircut schedules: Not publicly disclosed. Haircuts are proprietary and member-specific. Qualitative descriptions confirm that advances are over-collateralized by eligible mission-related assets (real estate-related loans and securities, agency and U.S. Treasury securities).
Recommendation: Mark haircut schedules as “not publicly disclosed.” Use district-level 10-Q figures for SBLOCs only where specifically extracted, and avoid presenting any round system-wide SBLOC total as a primary consolidated figure without heavy caveats.
# 6. Synthesis and Remaining Data Gaps
Concentration and insurer risks have increased. Insurance entities accounted for 27% of system advances as of Q1 2026 ($198.4 billion of $734.3 billion), led by Athene ($28.2 billion system-wide by Q1 2026) and Group 1001 entities (>$6 billion at Indianapolis by mid-2026). Advances themselves rose sharply from $676.7 billion at YE 2025 to $810.7 billion by mid-2026 (+20%), driven primarily by large members.
Evidence that advances can mask stress remains strongest for the 2023 bank failures and is plausible (though less directly proven) in the insurance context, where low-cost FHLB funding has coincided with large private-credit and related-party exposures that later required restatement and attracted regulatory scrutiny.
Key remaining gaps:
- Precise 2025 top-borrower percentages at several districts remain undisclosed in public 10-Ks.
- NAIC statutory filings for private insurers (including Schedule D affiliated vs. unaffiliated designations) are not publicly accessible without subscription.
- AB 2024-03 examination outcomes are non-public.
- System-wide SBLOC and haircut detail is limited and definitionally inconsistent across districts.
