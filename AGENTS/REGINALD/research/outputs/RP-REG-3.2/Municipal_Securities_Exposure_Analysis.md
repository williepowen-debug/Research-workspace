# **Strategic Credit Analysis: Municipal Securities Exposure and Credit Risk in U.S. Regional Banking (Q3 2025\)**

## **Executive Summary: The Bifurcation of Public Sector Finance**

The third quarter of 2025 marked a pivotal period for U.S. regional banks, characterized by a stabilizing interest rate environment and a divergence in asset-liability management strategies. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the municipal securities portfolios, credit facilities, and public sector risk exposures of nine distinct regional banking institutions: **Eagle Bancorp (EGBN)**, **Burke & Herbert Financial Services (BHRB)**, **Valley National Bancorp (VLY)**, **Western Alliance Bancorporation (WAL)**, **Citizens Financial Group (CFG)**, **Zions Bancorporation (ZION)**, **M\&T Bank (MTB)**, **First Horizon (FHN)**, and **Webster Financial (WBS)**.

Collectively, these institutions represent a cross-section of the diverse approaches to municipal finance currently employed within the sector. The analysis reveals a stark bifurcation in strategy: specifically, a divide between institutions utilizing municipal obligations primarily as high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) for duration and tax-efficiency purposes (e.g., M\&T Bank, Webster Financial), and those leveraging municipal finance as a direct lending vertical to drive yield and business development (e.g., Zions Bancorporation, Western Alliance).

### **Key Thematic Findings**

**1\. Structural Divergence: Securities vs. Direct Lending**

The data indicates that the "municipal exposure" of a regional bank can no longer be assessed solely through the lens of the Schedule RC-B Investment Securities. Banks like **Zions Bancorporation** and **Western Alliance** have effectively operationalized municipal finance as a commercial lending activity. Zions reports over $4.3 billion in municipal loans—distinct from its securities portfolio—exposing the bank to "conduit risk," where the ultimate credit risk lies not with the taxing authority of a municipality, but with a private non-profit or corporate entity borrowing through a municipal vehicle. This contrasts sharply with **Citizens Financial Group**, which maintains a negligible securities footprint in this sector ($1 million), indicative of a strategy that largely eschews direct public sector investment risk.

**2\. Geographic Concentration and Idiosyncratic Risk**

The prompt requested the identification of banks with significant concentrations in stressed regions. The analysis highlights **M\&T Bank** as a primary outlier, with approximately **61%** of its municipal securities portfolio concentrated within **New York State**. While New York's credit rating remains robust (AA+/Aa1), such single-state concentration inextricably links the bank's capital resilience to the Empire State's legislative and fiscal cycles. Similarly, **Burke & Herbert**, primarily serving the DC-MD-VA corridor, exhibits a portfolio density that effectively ties its investment income to the economic health of the federal enclave.

**3\. Credit Quality and the "Unrated" Anomaly**

While the aggregate credit quality of the cohort remains overwhelmingly Investment Grade (IG), with a heavy bias toward AAA and AA-rated paper, **Western Alliance** presents a notable anomaly. A substantial portion of its Held-to-Maturity (HTM) portfolio—approximately $1.36 billion—is classified as "Unrated." In the context of regional banking, this typically signifies private placement bonds or direct purchase agreements where the bank performs internal underwriting in lieu of a public rating. This requires a different risk monitoring framework than the liquid, rated portfolios of peers like **Webster Financial**, emphasizing the need for robust internal credit models over reliance on rating agencies.

**4\. Accounting Classifications and Capital Preservation**

The interaction between Accumulated Other Comprehensive Income (AOCI) and Tangible Common Equity (TCE) continues to drive portfolio classification decisions. **Webster Financial** and **Western Alliance** utilize the HTM designation extensively for their municipal holdings to shield regulatory capital from the mark-to-market volatility associated with the duration of long-dated municipal bonds. Conversely, **M\&T Bank** retains its portfolio almost entirely in Available-for-Sale (AFS), prioritizing liquidity optionality despite the potential for AOCI drag.

**1\. The Municipal Market Environment: 2025-2026 Outlook**

To accurately assess the credit risk within these bank portfolios, one must first establish the macroeconomic and sector-specific context of the U.S. municipal bond market as of late 2025\. The credit performance of regional banks holding these assets is inextricably linked to the fiscal health of state and local issuers.

### **1.1 Fundamentals: Stability Amidst Fading Stimulus**

The "Great Pandemic Stimulus" era has officially sunset. As we move into 2026, the municipal market is transitioning from a period of artificially buoyed revenues to a normalized fiscal environment. Research indicates that while state "rainy day" funds remain near historical highs, the rate of accumulation has stalled.

* **Fading Credit Quality:** Analysts at Breckinridge note that the market enters 2026 with "strong but fading credit quality".1 The primary drivers of this fade are the exhaustion of federal ESSER (education) and CARES Act funds, which masked structural deficits in sectors like K-12 education and mass transit.  
* **Sector Bifurcation:** The divide between "Essential Service" and "Project Finance" revenue bonds is widening. Water, sewer, and electric utility credits—sectors often favored by conservative bank portfolios—demonstrate high resilience due to their monopolistic pricing power and inelastic demand. Conversely, higher education and healthcare sectors face secular headwinds from demographic shifts and labor cost inflation.  
* **Pension Liability Management:** A critical "shadow" risk for banks holding General Obligation (GO) bonds is the pension funding status of the issuer. Notably, historically stressed states like **New Jersey** and **Illinois** have seen rating upgrades in 2025 due to improved pension contributions and revenue growth.1 This is a positive development for banks with legacy exposure to these regions, such as Valley National Bancorp.

### **1.2 Valuation Technicals and Bank Demand**

The valuation environment in late 2025 presents a "tale of two halves".3 After volatility in early 2025 driven by policy uncertainty, the market stabilized.

* **Yield Curve Implications:** The municipal yield curve in Q3 2025 offered attractive absolute yields, particularly in the 20-30 year segment.4 For banks, this presented an opportunity to lock in higher book yields, but also increased the duration risk inherent in their portfolios.  
* **The "Relative Yield" Risk:** A key risk identified for 2026 is the potential for municipal-to-Treasury ratios to return to post-pandemic averages.5 If relative yields rise (i.e., munis cheapen relative to Treasuries), banks holding AFS portfolios could see renewed pressure on AOCI, impacting Tangible Book Value (TBV).

**4\. Risk Assessment: Stressed Factors and Implications**

### **4.1 State Pension Obligations and Fiscal Stress**

The requirement to assess exposure to state pension obligations reveals a hidden layer of risk for banks with high geographic concentrations.

* **M\&T Bank (New York):** By holding 61% of its portfolio in NY issuers, MTB is implicitly short the **New York State Common Retirement Fund**. While currently well-funded, any future deterioration in this pension fund would necessitate increased tax levies or state contributions, potentially squeezing the repayment capacity of the agencies MTB holds.  
* **Valley National (New Jersey):** Valley's exposure to NJ (via loans and legacy securities) touches on one of the historically most stressed pension systems in the US. However, recent data confirms NJ has improved its funding ratio to 50%.2 This positive trajectory acts as a tailwind for Valley's credit quality, reducing the "tail risk" of a NJ fiscal crisis.  
* **Pension Obligation Bonds (POBs):** While no bank explicitly disclosed POB holdings, **Western Alliance's** "taxable municipal" portfolio is the most likely candidate for such assets, as POBs are federally taxable. POBs carry higher risk as they represent "deficit financing" rather than capital investment.

### **4.2 Essential Service vs. Conduit Risk**

The analysis confirms a bifurcation in *what* banks actually own.

* **Essential Service (Defensive):** **Webster (WBS)** and **M\&T (MTB)** hold portfolios dominated by GOs and Essential Service Revenue bonds (water/sewer). These are defensive assets with near-zero default rates, even in recessions.  
* **Conduit Risk (Aggressive):** **Zions (ZION)** and **Western Alliance (WAL)** show clear evidence of "Conduit" exposure. Zions explicitly noted $11 million in nonaccrual loans linked to private commercial entities utilizing municipal structures.11  
  * *Insight:* For these banks, the "municipal" label is a misnomer. They are effectively taking **commercial credit risk** (e.g., a nursing home's occupancy rate, a private college's enrollment) wrapped in a tax-exempt structure. This requires commercial credit underwriting, not municipal credit analysis.

### **4.3 Credit Facilities and Liquidity Support**

The presence of "Unfunded Commitments" serves as a proxy for liquidity facilities.

* **Zions Bancorporation:** The **$524 million** in unfunded commitments 11 highlights its role as a liquidity provider. In a market seizure (like March 2020), Zions could see a sudden draw on these lines as municipal issuers tap standby facilities because they cannot roll over commercial paper. This correlates liquidity risk with market stress.  
* **Eagle Bancorp:** The existence of **Eagle Municipal Finance, Inc.** 16 suggests a formalized direct lending program. This allows Eagle to structure terms (covenants, collateral) more tightly than public bonds, potentially offering better recovery prospects in a default scenario compared to passive bondholders.

**5\. Conclusion**

The U.S. regional banking sector's interaction with the municipal market in late 2025 is not monolithic. It is defined by a dichotomy of purpose:

1. **The Traditionalists (MTB, WBS, BHRB):** These banks view municipal securities as a store of liquidity and tax-free income. Their portfolios are high-grade, rated, and duration-sensitive. The primary risk here is **market risk** (rates) and **geographic concentration** (e.g., MTB in NY).  
2. **The Lenders (ZION, WAL, EGBN):** These banks view municipalities as a lending vertical. Their high concentrations of loans, unrated securities, and direct purchase subsidiaries suggest a strategy of arbitrage—capturing illiquidity premiums. The primary risk here is **credit risk**, specifically the "shadow" corporate risk of conduit borrowers.

**Recommendations:**

* **Monitor Western Alliance:** The $1.36 billion "Unrated" HTM bucket requires close scrutiny. Investors should demand greater transparency on the underlying sectors (e.g., LIHTC vs. Charter Schools) to assess the true credit risk.  
* **Watch New York Fiscal Data:** For M\&T Bank, the New York State budget is a material credit document. Fiscal stress in Albany will transmit directly to M\&T’s capital ratios via its investment portfolio.  
* **Re-evaluate Zions' Risk:** Zions should be analyzed as a commercial lender to the public sector, not just a bondholder. Its $524 million in unfunded commitments represents a "sleeping" liquidity risk in a volatile rate environment.
