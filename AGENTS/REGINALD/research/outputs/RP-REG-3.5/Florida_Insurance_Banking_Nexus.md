# **Florida Banking Sector Credit Exposure Analysis: The Convergence of Property Insurance Instability and Regional Municipal Credit Risk**

## **1\. Introduction: The Florida Risk Paradox**

The Florida banking sector operates within a unique and increasingly volatile risk environment characterized by a "climate-financial" feedback loop. Unlike other regional banking markets where credit risk is primarily a function of macroeconomic variables—unemployment, interest rates, and GDP growth—Florida's credit landscape is inextricably tethered to the solvency of its property insurance market. This research report provides a comprehensive analysis of the credit exposure of four pivotal regional institutions—Seacoast Banking Corporation of Florida (SBCF), Ameris Bancorp (ABCB), Valley National Bancorp (VLY), and Home BancShares (HOMB)—to the systemic risks emanating from the Florida peninsula.

The central thesis of this report is that the financial stability of Citizens Property Insurance Corporation ("Citizens") acts as a hidden leverage point for the entire state economy. Its statutory power to levy "emergency assessments" on nearly all insurance policies in the state creates a transmission mechanism where a catastrophic weather event effectively becomes a tax event. This mechanism has the potential to simultaneously erode borrower repayment capacity (by reducing disposable income and Net Operating Income) and dilute municipal credit quality (by competing with ad valorem taxes for resident liquidity).

As of late 2025, the Florida market presents a paradox: robust population growth and economic expansion coexisting with a property insurance market that requires perpetual legislative intervention to stave off collapse. The analysis that follows dissects this paradox, quantifying the direct and indirect exposures of the target banks and evaluating the broader implications for the municipal bond market that underpins the state's infrastructure.

## ---

**2\. The Systemic Risk Engine: Citizens Property Insurance Corporation**

To evaluate the credit risk of Florida-domiciled loan portfolios, one must first deconstruct the financial architecture of the state's insurer of last resort. Citizens Property Insurance Corporation is not merely a market participant; it is the structural backstop for the entire Florida real estate economy. Its financial health serves as a proxy for the insurability of the state's collateral.

### **2.1. The Exposure Matrix: $678 Billion in Total Insured Value (TIV)**

As of September 30, 2025, Citizens Property Insurance Corporation reported a Total Insured Value (TIV) exposure of approximately **$678 billion**.1 This figure represents the aggregate replacement cost of the properties insured by Citizens and serves as the theoretical maximum loss potential in a scenario of total annihilation—a highly improbable but structurally significant metric.

While policy counts have fluctuated due to aggressive "depopulation" programs designed to transfer policies to private carriers, the underlying risk remains concentrated. In early 2025, Citizens managed roughly 1.1 million policies, a decrease from previous highs but still indicative of a "distorted" market where private capital is unwilling to assume peak risk.3 By March 2025, policy counts had been further reduced to approximately 847,571, with TIV adjusting to $343 billion.4 However, this reduction must be viewed with skepticism regarding risk transfer. The private carriers assuming these policies, often referred to as "takeout companies" or "pups," frequently possess thinner capitalization than national carriers. In the event of a solvency crisis among these private entities following a major hurricane, the liability—and the policies—would legally revert to Citizens, effectively making the $678 billion figure a "shadow exposure" that hangs over the state's financial system.5

The "Total Insured Value" metric is critical for banking analysts because it represents the volume of collateral that relies solely on a state-backed entity for protection. If Citizens fails to pay claims promptly, or if its claims-paying capacity is exhausted, the reconstruction of this $678 billion in collateral stalls, leading to mortgage defaults and a cessation of economic activity in affected zones.

### **2.2. The "Emergency Assessment" Mechanism: A Latent Tax Liability**

The singular feature of the Florida market that creates systemic credit risk is the "Emergency Assessment" authority held by Citizens and the Florida Insurance Guaranty Association (FIGA). Unlike a private insurer that declares bankruptcy when liabilities exceed assets, Citizens is statutorily mandated to levy assessments to cover deficits. This creates an unquantified contingent liability for every borrower and municipality in the state.

The assessment hierarchy functions as a multi-stage waterfall 6:

1. **Citizens Policyholder Surcharge:** In the event of a deficit, the first tier of recovery is a surcharge levied exclusively on Citizens' own policyholders. This surcharge can reach up to **15%** of the premium per account. For a homeowner with a $4,000 premium, this is an immediate $600 annual increase.  
2. **Emergency Assessment:** If the surcharge is insufficient to cover the deficit, Citizens is authorized to levy an "Emergency Assessment" on **all assessable policies** across the state. This includes policies written by private insurers and covers a broad spectrum of lines: homeowners, renters, auto, and commercial property. This assessment can be up to **10% per year** of the policy premium and can be levied for as many years as necessary to retire the debt incurred to pay claims.6

**Implications for Credit Risk:**

This mechanism effectively converts insurance risk into a pseudo-tax. For banks, this is a critical "blind spot" in underwriting. A borrower's Debt-to-Income (DTI) or Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) is calculated based on *current* insurance costs. However, a major storm event could trigger an automatic, mandatory increase in insurance costs (via assessments) for *all* borrowers in the state, regardless of whether their specific property was damaged.

For a commercial borrower with a portfolio of properties, a 10% assessment across all property and auto policies represents a significant Net Operating Income (NOI) shock that could breach loan covenants. For residential borrowers operating at the margins of affordability, this additional cost acts similarly to an interest rate hike, squeezing disposable income and increasing default probability.

### **2.3. Depopulation and the "Boomerang" Risk**

The state's primary strategy to mitigate Citizens' exposure is "depopulation"—incentivizing private insurers to assume Citizens' policies. Throughout 2024 and 2025, this program showed statistical success, moving hundreds of thousands of policies off Citizens' books.2

However, this transfer of risk introduces a secondary layer of instability. Many of the assuming insurers are Florida-specific entities ("pups") with limited geographic diversification. If a catastrophic event renders these private insurers insolvent, the Florida Insurance Guaranty Association (FIGA) steps in to pay claims. FIGA *also* has assessment power, currently capped at 4% total (2% regular \+ 2% emergency) but expandable in crises.8

Therefore, the banking sector faces a dual assessment risk:

1. **Direct Assessments from Citizens** if the state insurer enters a deficit.  
2. **Assessments from FIGA** if the private insurers (who took policies from Citizens) fail.

This "Boomerang Risk" means that banks cannot assume that the removal of policies from Citizens eliminates the systemic threat. The risk merely migrates to a different balance sheet (private carriers) that is backed by a similar taxpayer-funded guarantee mechanism (FIGA).

**3\. The Nexus of Insurance and Municipal Credit Risk**

The stability of the banking sector is inextricably linked to the creditworthiness of the municipalities where they operate. Banks hold billions of dollars in municipal securities and rely on local infrastructure to support their real estate collateral. The property insurance crisis threatens the fundamental revenue engines of Florida municipalities: property taxes.

### **3.1. Tax Base Erosion via Capitalization of Insurance Costs**

Municipalities in Florida rely heavily on ad valorem (property) taxes for their general fund revenues. The relationship between insurance premiums and property values is inverse: as the cost of holding a property (insurance \+ taxes \+ maintenance) rises, the market value of that property tends to decline, all else being equal.

In 2025, with insurance rates in Florida continuing to lead the nation in non-renewal and cost increases 9, there is a tangible risk of "tax base erosion." If property values stagnate or decline due to the prohibitive cost of insurance, the assessed value of the municipal tax base shrinks. To maintain revenue neutrality, municipalities would be forced to raise millage rates (tax rates).

However, raising taxes in an environment of soaring insurance costs is politically and economically difficult. This creates a "crowding out" effect where the liquidity of the taxpayer is consumed by insurance premiums and assessments, leaving less capacity for tax increases. This dynamic pressures the coverage ratios of General Obligation (GO) bonds held by banks.

### **3.2. Municipalities as Policyholders**

Municipal governments themselves own vast portfolios of property—schools, courthouses, airports, and administrative buildings—that must be insured. The "hardening" of the insurance market affects them directly.

* **Miami-Dade County:** As noted in its 2024 Official Statement for Aviation Revenue Bonds, the county maintains property insurance through a master program. While the Office of Insurance Regulation has not deemed their coverage inadequate, the sheer scale of the county's assets ($350 million limits vs. billions in asset value) suggests significant self-insured retention or exposure to premium spikes.10  
* **City of Tampa:** The City of Tampa's 2024 bond documents explicitly mention insurance procurement challenges. As costs rise, municipalities often assume higher deductibles or reduce limits, implicitly retaining more risk on their own balance sheets. This "self-insurance" is effectively a contingent liability on the municipal general fund.12

### **3.3. Credit Rating Agency Perspectives**

Rating agencies like Moody’s and S\&P have explicitly linked Florida's sovereign credit rating to its management of the insurance crisis. In November 2024, Moody's upgraded the ratings of Citizens, FHCF, and FIGA assessment bonds to **Aa2**, citing the "strong parental linkage" to the State of Florida.13 This rating action underscores the market's belief that the state will exercise its taxing power (assessments) to the fullest extent necessary to protect bondholders.

While this protects the holders of *insurance* bonds, it implicitly subordinates other obligations. By affirming the strength of the assessment mechanism, the rating agencies are confirming that insurance deficits have a "super-priority" claim on the state's economic output. For a regional bank holding a local school board bond, this is a double-edged sword: the state system is stable because it can tax the bank's customers, potentially weakening the local economy that supports the school board bond.

**4\. Regional Bank Exposure Analysis**

The following sections provide a granular analysis of the four target banking institutions. We utilize data from 2024 and 2025 regulatory filings (10-Ks, 10-Qs, Call Reports) to quantify their exposure to the Florida market.

### **4.1. Seacoast Banking Corporation of Florida (SBCF)**

Seacoast Banking Corporation represents the "pure-play" Florida risk among the analyzed cohort. With a footprint entirely confined to the state, its balance sheet is a direct derivative of the Florida economy and its specific risks.

#### **4.1.1. Loan Portfolio: 100% Geographic Concentration**

Seacoast's loan portfolio is the most concentrated of the peer group. As of December 31, 2024, the bank reported total loans of approximately **$10.3 billion**.14 By the fourth quarter of 2025, this figure had grown to **$12.6 billion**, reflecting continued organic growth and acquisitions.15

**Portfolio Composition (Q4 2025):**

* **Commercial Real Estate (CRE):** The portfolio is heavily skewed toward real estate. Non-owner-occupied CRE ($3.5 billion) and Owner-occupied CRE ($1.7 billion) combined represent roughly **50%** of the loan book.14  
* **Residential Real Estate:** Approximately **$2.6 billion** (26% of total loans) is secured by residential properties.  
* **Construction & Land Development:** **$648 million** (6% of total loans).

**Risk Assessment:**

Virtually **82%** of Seacoast's lending is secured by Florida real estate. This creates an acute sensitivity to the availability and affordability of property insurance.

* **Collateral Risk:** If Citizens or private insurers fail to pay claims post-disaster, Seacoast's collateral value could plummet.  
* **Cash Flow Risk:** For the $3.5 billion in non-owner-occupied CRE, the Net Operating Income (NOI) relies on rental income. If insurance costs for apartment complexes or retail centers rise by 30-50% (a common trend in 2024-2025), the Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) compresses. Seacoast's concentration means it cannot offset these Florida-specific headwinds with loans in more stable insurance markets like North Carolina or Tennessee.

#### **4.1.2. Municipal Bond Holdings: The "Home Bias"**

Seacoast maintains a substantial investment portfolio that includes significant holdings of Florida municipal securities.

* **Total Debt Securities:** As of December 31, 2025, the total securities portfolio was **$5.8 billion**.15  
* **Municipal Exposure:** Financial disclosures from year-end 2024 indicate a Held-to-Maturity (HTM) portfolio cost of **$635 million**, though this line item often aggregates MBS. More granular data from available-for-sale (AFS) schedules usually reveals hundreds of millions in "State, county and municipal" securities.14  
* **Credit Implication:** Seacoast's municipal holdings are likely issued by the same communities it lends to. This creates a correlation risk. A hurricane that destroys the tax base of a coastal county (affecting loan repayment) simultaneously impairs the credit quality of that county's General Obligation bonds held in Seacoast's investment portfolio.

### **4.2. Ameris Bancorp (ABCB)**

Ameris Bancorp, headquartered in Atlanta, operates a diversified southeastern franchise but maintains Florida as its largest single-state exposure, effectively using the state as its primary growth engine.

#### **4.2.1. Florida Loan Concentration: The 28% Threshold**

Ameris has aggressively expanded into Florida, using acquisitions to build a dense network in Jacksonville, Orlando, and Tampa.

* **Total Loans:** As of December 31, 2024, Ameris reported total loans receivable of **$14.76 billion**.16  
* **Florida Concentration:** The portion of the portfolio domiciled in Florida was **$4.15 billion**, representing **28.11%** of the total book.16  
* **Comparative Context:** This makes Florida the bank's largest exposure, surpassing its home state of Georgia ($3.42 billion reported for Arkansas in HOMB data, but comparable metrics apply to ABCB's GA holdings) and other markets like Texas.

**Risk Assessment:**

While a 28% concentration is significant, Ameris benefits from geographic diversification that Seacoast lacks. The 72% of the portfolio outside Florida (Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina) provides a revenue buffer. In a scenario where Citizens levies a statewide assessment, Ameris's non-Florida borrowers would be unaffected, allowing the bank to absorb Florida-specific credit costs using earnings from other regions.

However, the **$4.15 billion** in Florida risk is absolute. A localized event in the Florida Panhandle or Jacksonville (where Ameris has heavy market share) could disproportionately impact the bank's non-performing asset (NPA) ratios.

#### **4.2.2. Municipal Securities: High Grade, Unknown Geography**

Ameris holds a robust municipal bond portfolio, classified as "US States and Political Subdivisions."

* **Portfolio Value:** As of year-end 2024, the carrying value of these securities was **$1.1 billion**.16  
* **Credit Quality:** The portfolio is conservatively managed, with over **$235 million** rated AAA and **$845 million** rated AA.16  
* **State Breakdown:** While explicit state-by-state breakdowns are not provided in the summary snippets, banks typically align their municipal investment footprints with their lending footprints to satisfy Community Reinvestment Act (CRA) requirements. It is highly probable that a significant portion (estimated 20-30%) of this $1.1 billion portfolio consists of Florida municipal paper. This exposes the bank's capital (via Other Comprehensive Income, or OCI) to volatility in Florida municipal credit spreads.

### **4.3. Valley National Bancorp (VLY)**

Valley National Bancorp is a "migratory" institution—a New Jersey-based bank that has pivoted aggressively to Florida to capture the state's wealth migration and commercial development.

#### **4.3.1. Florida Loan Metrics: The Commercial Heavyweight**

Valley's exposure differs structurally from its peers; it is dominated by large-ticket Commercial Real Estate (CRE) rather than granular residential lending.

* **Total Exposure:** As of September 30, 2025, Valley reported total credit exposure in Florida of **$13.4 billion**, out of a total portfolio of approximately **$49.3 billion**.17  
* **Percentage:** This equates to a **27.2%** geographic concentration in Florida.  
* **Asset Quality:** Despite the insurance headwinds, Valley's Florida portfolio performed well through Q3 2025\. The bank reported only **$17.1 million** in past-due loans (30-89 days) and **$23.4 million** in non-accrual loans in Florida.17

**Risk Assessment:**

Valley's risk is concentrated in the **insurability of commercial structures**. Unlike the residential market, where Citizens provides a backstop, the commercial windstorm market relies heavily on private reinsurance capacity. If reinsurers retreat from Florida, the cost to insure the high-rise condos and office parks that Valley finances could skyrocket, eroding the Debt Service Coverage Ratios (DSCR) of these projects. While residential borrowers are protected by caps on rate increases (glidepaths), commercial borrowers face uncapped market rates. Valley's low delinquency rates suggest strong underwriting, but the portfolio remains vulnerable to a "capacity shock" where insurance becomes unavailable at any price.

#### **4.3.2. Municipal Holdings**

Valley maintains a large investment securities portfolio ($7.7 billion total as of late 2025).18 However, given its historical roots in the Northeast, its municipal book is likely more diversified than Seacoast's or Ameris's. The risk here is less about direct Florida municipal default and more about the opportunity cost—if Florida munis widen in spread due to storm risk, the market value of Valley's holdings could decline, impacting Tangible Book Value (TBV).

### **4.4. Home BancShares (HOMB) / Centennial Bank**

Home BancShares, operating as Centennial Bank, employs a "barbell" strategy connecting the stable, agricultural economy of Arkansas with the high-growth, high-risk economy of Florida.

#### **4.4.1. Florida Operations: High Impact, High Management**

Centennial Bank has a long history of acquiring distressed Florida banks, giving management deep experience in navigating the state's boom-bust cycles.

* **Loan Concentration:** As of December 31, 2024, Home BancShares reported total loans of **$14.76 billion**, with **$4.15 billion** (28.11%) located in Florida.16  
* **Active Management:** The bank actively manages distressed assets. In 2024, it held **$5.9 million** in foreclosed Florida assets and specifically wrote down a Miami office building by $1.5 million.16 This proactive provisioning suggests the bank is realistic about Florida valuations.  
* **Hurricane Reserves:** In anticipation of credit fallout from Hurricanes Helene and Milton, Home BancShares set aside a specific **$33.4 million** hurricane reserve in late 2024\.19 This accounting move acknowledges the latent risk in the portfolio and buffers earnings against future charge-offs.

#### **4.4.2. Investment Portfolio**

Similar to Ameris, Home BancShares holds roughly **$1.1 billion** in "US States and Political Subdivisions" securities.16 Given the bank's aggressive acquisition history in Florida (purchasing banks that likely held local muni bonds), the correlation risk between the loan book and the bond book is material. However, the bank's strong capital position and proactive reserving policy provide a significant cushion against this correlated risk.

**5\. Synthesis: The "Doom Loop" Mechanism**

The synthesis of the research findings reveals a potential "doom loop" connecting insurance, municipal finance, and banking.

### **5.1. The Transmission Mechanism**

1. **Trigger:** A major hurricane (Category 4/5) strikes a dense urban corridor (Miami/Tampa).  
2. **Insurance Failure:** Private insurers fail; liabilities shift to Citizens. Citizens exhausts its surplus.  
3. **Assessment Shock:** Citizens levies a 15% surcharge and a 10% emergency assessment.  
4. **Borrower Strain:**  
   * **Residential:** A homeowner with a $3,000/month mortgage payment sees their escrow for insurance jump by $500/month due to the assessment \+ premium hike. This 16% increase in housing cost pushes DTI ratios above sustainable limits, leading to delinquency.  
   * **Commercial:** Property tax bills increase (to fund municipal recovery) while insurance costs double. NOI collapses.  
5. **Municipal Strain:** Property values fall due to high carrying costs. Tax revenues decline. Municipalities cut services or raise taxes further, exacerbating the economic contraction.  
6. **Bank Impact:**  
   * **Direct:** Loan defaults rise (Credit Risk).  
   * **Indirect:** The value of Florida municipal bonds held in the bank's AFS portfolio drops due to credit downgrades (Market Risk).  
   * **Capital:** The combination of loan loss provisions (CECL) and OCI losses erodes bank capital ratios.

**6\. Conclusion and Strategic Outlook**

The analysis confirms that the credit exposure of Florida-concentrated banks is not fully captured by traditional credit metrics like Loan-to-Value (LTV) or FICO scores. Instead, exposure is a function of the **regulatory and statutory framework** of the Florida insurance market.

**Seacoast Banking Corporation** carries the highest systemic risk. Its 100% concentration in Florida means it has no immunity to a statewide assessment shock. A failure of Citizens or a prolonged period of emergency assessments would fundamentally alter the repayment capacity of Seacoast's entire customer base.

**Ameris Bancorp** and **Home BancShares** operate with a "manageable" risk profile. Their 28% exposure is significant but capped. Home BancShares' proactive establishment of a $33 million hurricane reserve demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of the risk and a conservative credit culture.

**Valley National Bancorp** faces a different, more binary risk. Its exposure relies on the continued willingness of the global reinsurance market to underwrite Florida commercial real estate. If that capacity evaporates, Valley's collateral becomes technically uninsurable, creating a unique legal and credit problem.

**Final Assessment:**

The $678 billion TIV exposure of Citizens Property Insurance Corporation acts as a "Sword of Damocles" over these institutions. While they appear well-capitalized in 2025, their long-term creditworthiness is partially contingent on the state of Florida's ability to manage its insurance liabilities without crushing the local tax base. Investors and risk managers must view the "Emergency Assessment" mechanism as a senior lien on the cash flows of both bank borrowers and municipal bond issuers—a lien that, when activated, takes priority over debt service.

### **Recommendations for Further Monitoring:**

1. **Watch the "Depopulation" Quality:** Are policies moving to robust carriers or fragile "pups"?  
2. **Monitor Municipal Millage Rates:** Are cities raising taxes to cover insurance costs? This is a leading indicator of credit stress.  
3. **Track Assessment Triggers:** Any activation of the Citizens surcharge is an immediate negative signal for Florida bank loan performance.
