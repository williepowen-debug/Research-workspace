Bank OZK (CERT 110): GFC-Era Credit Quality Analysis 2007–2012
Was George Gleason's Track Record Genuinely Tested?

Executive Summary
Bank of the Ozarks (FDIC CERT 110, now Bank OZK, NASDAQ: OZK) navigated the Global Financial Crisis in a way that demands careful interpretation rather than a simple verdict. On one hand, the bank's own-originated net charge-off rate consistently beat industry averages by 100–200+ basis points throughout the worst credit environment in modern history, and it never posted a quarterly net loss. The ROA stayed above 1.20% in every single quarter from 2007 through 2012 — a feat almost no peer CRE-heavy bank achieved. On the other hand, the bank entered the crisis running a CRE concentration ratio of ~381% of total risk-based capital (well above the 300% regulatory threshold), and the single largest credit event — a ~$10.5M charge-off on Arkansas residential lot developer Gary Combs — came from its community banking book, not from the sophisticated CRE construction underwriting that characterizes today's Real Estate Specialties Group (RESG). The verdict: Gleason demonstrated real credit discipline in a severe downturn, but the portfolio he managed through the GFC was a materially different animal than the $20B+ RESG franchise that has dominated the bank since ~2014.
No FDIC enforcement actions (cease-and-desist, consent orders, or civil money penalties) appear against Bank of the Ozarks (CERT 110) during 2007–2012. The bank instead served as an acquiring institution for six FDIC-assisted failed bank deals, a position regulators reserve for demonstrably sound institutions.[1][2][3]

Quarterly Data: Net Charge-Off Rate (Annualized)
All data sourced from FDIC Call Reports (CERT 110). Annualized NCO rate = (quarterly net charge-offs / gross loans) × 4. Industry figures are Federal Reserve charge-off release for all FDIC-insured commercial banks.[4]

Key Observation: OZK beat the industry NCO rate in every single quarter except 2009Q2 — and that anomaly, while technically "above" the industry average by 18 bps, was entirely attributable to a single concentrated credit, not a systemic portfolio problem. The bank's 2025 charge-off rate of ~0.5% was described as its highest in 15 years — confirming that 2009–2010 was the prior stress event.[8]

Quarterly Data: Noncurrent Loan Ratio
Noncurrent loans (90+ days past due or on nonaccrual) as a percentage of gross loans. Critical interpretation note: The dramatic rise in NCL beginning 2010Q4 and peaking at 7.87% in 2011Q2 is overwhelmingly driven by FDIC-covered loans acquired from failed banks — not deterioration in OZK's own originated portfolio.[9]

The diagnostic tell for the covered-loan distortion is the 2011Q2 combination: NCL/Loans surged to 7.87% while ROA simultaneously spiked to 3.74%. No bank generates 3.74% ROA with 7.87% noncurrent loans unless the noncurrent loans are substantially loss-share-protected and generating income (FDIC loss-share reimbursements and purchase accounting accretion). The FDIC provided 80% loss coverage on Park Avenue Bank's $514M loss-share pool and First Choice Community Bank's $260.7M pool.[10][11]

CRE Concentration Ratio (CRE / Total Risk-Based Capital)
Numerator: Construction & Land Development + Multifamily + Non-Owner-Occupied Nonfarm Nonresidential loans. Denominator: Tier 1 Capital + ALLL (approximation of Total Risk-Based Capital). Regulatory concern threshold: 300% (per 2006 Joint Guidance); concentration high-growth warning: 300% concentration + 50%+ growth in 36 months.[12]

Two structural features enabled the high concentration without triggering regulatory action. First, construction loan concentration peaked at 267% of risk-based capital (2007Q4), which is below the 300% ADC-specific threshold in the joint guidance. Second, Tier 1 capital ratios remained consistently above 10% — well-capitalized status — throughout, providing the regulatory buffer against concentration risk. The bank proactively raised capital in late 2008 (Tier 1 rose from $265M in Q3 to $347M in Q4 2008), reducing the CRE ratio from 394% to 286% in a single quarter.[13][12]

Total Assets Growth Trajectory

Total growth from end-2007 to end-2012: +44% ($2.70B → $3.89B), spanning the worst credit cycle in modern history. By contrast, the median community bank with significant CRE concentration shrank assets during this period as charge-offs consumed capital.[14][15]

FDIC Enforcement Actions: 2007–2012
No formal enforcement actions (consent orders, cease-and-desist orders, civil money penalties, or prompt corrective action directives) were found against Bank of the Ozarks (CERT 110) during the 2007–2012 period. The FDIC's own enforcement records show that the bank was selected as a preferred acquirer for six failed bank resolutions during peak crisis years — a status the FDIC reserves for institutions with strong capital, sound management, and regulatory goodwill.[3][16][1]

These acquisitions were optically the cheapest capital ever deployed by OZK — they acquired billions in assets at massive discounts with government-backed loss protection — and they explain much of the apparent NCL/ratio deterioration in 2010–2012 without representing credit failures in OZK's own book.

Peer Comparison: GFC-Stressed CRE-Heavy Banks
Synovus Bank (CERT 873, Columbus, GA — ~$32B peak assets, GFC-stressed but survived)
Synovus was a Georgia-based regional bank heavily exposed to ADC and CRE across the Southeast — a direct peer comparison for the type of CRE book that caused failures in OZK's own operating markets.[15]

Synovus posted negative ROA for multiple quarters and annualized NCO rates that hit 7.53% — roughly 3x OZK's worst quarter. The comparison illustrates that CRE-heavy regional banks in the same geographic footprint (Georgia, Florida) suffered catastrophic credit losses that OZK avoided.
Westernbank Puerto Rico (CERT 31027, failed April 2010)
A textbook example of a CRE-concentrated institution that did not survive. Westernbank had NCL/Loans of 18%+ for 8+ consecutive quarters before failure, while managing to stay technically alive due to deferred charge-off recognition. Its Tier 1 capital fell below 3% by early 2010.[15]

Per FDIC research, failed institutions during the GFC had ADC concentrations roughly three times the average of non-failed institutions, and their NCL rates averaged more than 10% of loans at the point of failure. OZK's peak organic NCL of 1.25% was well inside the safe zone.[13]

The 2009Q2 Charge-Off Anomaly — Deep Dive
The one quarter where OZK's annualized NCO rate (2.90%) marginally exceeded the industry average (2.72%) deserves scrutiny. Gleason disclosed on the earnings call that the Combs charge-off of approximately $10.5M resulted from a settled legal dispute over residential lot development in Goshen and Prairie Grove, Arkansas. The original loan balance was $25.6M — partially recovered through deposits and a letter of credit from another bank — and the remaining collateral was written down to $12.8M based on updated appraisals. This was:[5]
A single borrower event, not a portfolio trend
In residential land/lots — the subsegment of CRE most devastated by the housing collapse
In Northwest Arkansas community banking, not RESG
The bank remained profitable with ROA of 1.42% even in that same quarter
The ORE balance then spiked from $22.5M to $63.7M the following quarter (2009Q3) as the Combs properties were transferred onto OZK's books and recognized as other real estate owned — the large spike in ORE confirms the nature of the event as a specific property-level resolution rather than broad portfolio deterioration.

The RESG Counterfactual — Critical Assessment
What RESG Is and When It Was Built
Bank of the Ozarks formed the Real Estate Specialties Group (RESG) in 2003 in its Dallas office, with the explicit goal of originating high-quality construction loans on marquee real estate projects. However, RESG was still a relatively small division during the GFC. The bank's total assets were only ~$2.5–3.9B from 2007–2012, and the majority of the loan portfolio consisted of community banking — small business, agricultural, consumer, and local commercial real estate typical of an Arkansas community bank.[19][20]
RESG's ascent to dominance came primarily post-2012, particularly through the 2014–2016 acquisition spree and the organic growth surge in urban construction lending (New York, Miami, Chicago, Los Angeles). By 2023, OZK was reportedly the largest construction lender in the U.S. By 2024, RESG comprised ~54–58% of the bank's loan composition. The CRE-to-equity ratio had climbed to approximately 620% of equity by 2024 — double the regulatory concern threshold.[21][22][8]
What the GFC Track Record Actually Proves
The GFC-era performance genuinely demonstrates:
Gleason can maintain credit discipline on CRE in a major downturn — the community bank CRE book (local Arkansas, community-scale developers) suffered minimal losses even as Georgia and Florida peers were obliterated
Proactive capital management — the Q4 2008 capital raise reduced CRE concentration from 394% to 286% in one quarter, showing willingness to dilute to protect the balance sheet
Opportunistic positioning — using crisis-era FDIC deals to acquire $2B+ in assets at deep discounts while peers were contracting
No regulatory issues — clean enforcement record even while running CRE concentration above 300% in 2007–2008
What It Does Not Fully Prove
RESG-style national construction lending at scale was not the dominant exposure in 2007–2012. A $300M+ loan on a Miami supertall or a New York mixed-use tower is fundamentally different from a $5–20M Arkansas community real estate loan
Geographic concentration — OZK's GFC book was mostly in Arkansas, a state with relatively modest housing boom/bust dynamics versus Florida, Georgia, Nevada, and Arizona
Sponsor quality — RESG explicitly targets "marquee projects" with institutional sponsors and heavy equity requirements, but that underwriting framework was still being developed during the GFC period[23]
Portfolio size — at $2.5B in assets with ~$1.7B in loans, a single $10.5M charge-off moved the needle on NCO rates; at $35B+ in assets today, systemic RESG credit stress would be a completely different magnitude problem
Current CRE ratio at ~620% of equity is categorically different from the 381% the bank entered the GFC with, and the underlying collateral (speculative construction in high-cost urban markets at peak-cycle valuations) is qualitatively different from what Gleason managed in 2008–2009
Gleason himself acknowledged in 2024 that "these high interest rates cause stress, and some of that stress is going to translate through into losses," as non-performing loans grew to their highest since at least 2009. The bank's 2025 charge-off rate of 0.5% was described as its highest in 15 years — meaning the current cycle is now stress-testing the portfolio that actually matters: the post-2012 RESG book.[21][8]

Bottom-Line Verdict on Gleason's Track Record
The GFC record is genuine, not fabricated. Bank OZK ran significantly above-average CRE concentration (381–394% at peak) entering the worst CRE downturn since the 1990s S&L crisis, produced profitable results every single quarter, kept own-originated charge-offs well below industry in virtually all periods, never received an enforcement action, and actually expanded through crisis-era FDIC acquisitions. Compared to CRE-heavy peers — Synovus posted 7.53% annualized NCOs and deeply negative ROA; Westernbank Puerto Rico was closed by the FDIC — Gleason's management of the GFC was genuinely exceptional.
However, the analytical limitation is categorical: the bank that navigated the GFC was a different institution from the bank operating today. The GFC portfolio was ~$2B in community-scale Arkansas-centric CRE. The current exposure is a $35B+ national franchise with CRE-to-equity at roughly 620%, concentrated in speculative construction lending in the highest-cost urban markets in the country, at what may be a cyclical peak in construction costs and valuations. The 2007–2012 track record is meaningful evidence of underwriting discipline and management quality — but it does not constitute a complete stress test of the specific risk profile OZK carries today. The stress test of RESG's current book is, for the first time, happening now in 2024–2026.[8][21]
