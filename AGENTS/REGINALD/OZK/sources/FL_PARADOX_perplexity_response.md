# Perplexity Response — FL Paradox Prompt (Mar 24, 2026)

Bank OZK's Florida luxury condo book is structurally better positioned than 2006–08, but the "Florida Paradox" is not bulletproof: the key tail risks are (a) a deep price reset that makes even 40–45% deposits feel cheap to walk from, (b) insurance/SB 4‑D‑driven carrying-cost shock that crimps absorption and refi take‑outs, and (c) mid‑cycle demand air-pocket in the ultra‑luxury buyer pool. Below is a stress-test of the three channels you flagged, with emphasis on how and where a construction lender can actually take pain.

## 1. Pre‑sale walkaway risk

**Typical luxury pre‑construction deposit structure (Miami 2024–26)**

Most prime Miami luxury towers run buyers to 30–50% total deposits before closing, staged over the construction cycle. A very common pattern in the $1M+ segment is:
- 10–20% at contract, following a small refundable reservation.
- 10% at groundbreaking.
- 10–15% at structural/top‑off or similar milestone.

Result: 30–40% of purchase price is non‑refundable and sitting in escrow pre‑delivery, with some ultra‑luxury projects pushing closer to 50%.

**Legal/economic conditions for walkaways**

Florida condo purchase agreements are drafted strongly in the developer's favor: once the rescission period passes and contingencies are satisfied, deposits are typically hard, with limited buyer outs (material project change, missed outside delivery dates, certain title/approval failures).

Buyers walk when:
- Market values fall enough that losing 30–40% is better than closing on an over‑market unit (classic 2008 dynamic).
- Financing disappears or underwriting standards tighten such that buyers cannot get end loans at anything like the original LTV/DSCR expectation.
- Currency controls / capital‑flight rules (for foreign buyers) or sanctions make funding/closing impossible; developers sometimes negotiate partial deposit forfeiture vs. litigation.

Practically, high‑net‑worth/ultra‑luxury buyers who are all‑cash or lightly levered are much less rate‑sensitive, but they are price‑sensitive as investors: perceived overpayment plus rising carrying costs (taxes/insurance/HOA) is the main trigger for strategic defaults rather than true inability to pay.

**Recent evidence of pre‑sale issues (2025–26)**

Public market commentary and brokerage reports for Miami's $1M+ condo segment show a softer but functioning luxury market: 2025 luxury condo sales volumes fell and days‑on‑market increased, but prices at the high end remained relatively firm and closings continued.

The visible stress has been concentrated in existing condos, where insurance, SB 4‑D compliance, and reserve funding have made many buildings less financeable and less attractive, depressing resale prices and stretching marketing times.

I do not see hard data yet on 2025–26 cancellation rates for top‑tier pre‑construction, and headline projects like Waldorf Astoria Miami, Baccarat, and other branded towers continue to be marketed as 90%+ pre‑sold with no publicized contract collapse.

The main sign of stress is elongated sell‑outs and occasional end‑buyer attempts to re‑trade or assign contracts rather than wholesale non‑closing.

Given that OZK's Miami book skews to ultra‑luxury, highly branded, high‑presale product with a wealthy, often all‑cash buyer base (e.g., Four Seasons Coconut Grove with 59%+ all‑cash in the $10M+ tier), your buyer pool is less prone to walk simply due to rate moves, but they can still walk if value perception collapses or legal/regulatory friction at delivery is high.

**What happened in 2008 / walkaway rates**

Downtown and coastal South Florida condos saw peak‑to‑trough price declines in the 30–40%+ range; one study pegged average price cuts of roughly 38–42% for condos and townhomes in key Miami submarkets in 2008 alone.

## Walkaway history (cont'd)

That drawdown was sufficient in many towers to wipe out or exceed pre‑construction deposits, especially where buyers had only 20% down or used assignment/spec strategies. Contract fallout was widespread, to the point that bulk investors and lenders effectively recapitalized entire buildings; many original pre‑sale contracts never turned into closings.

Exact "walkaway rate" data for that cycle is patchy, but case studies consistently describe:
- 30–50% or more of contracts in some towers failing to close at original terms.
- Developers re‑marketing units at steep discounts or selling blocks to opportunistic funds.
- Lenders taking control, extending terms, or enforcing guarantees as construction loans went to maturity with unsold/uncashed‑out inventory.

Relative to that history, OZK's disclosed weighted average LTV ~39% and LTC ~46% on its Miami condo exposure gives substantially more equity cushion than typical 2006–07 condo capital stacks (often financed to 60–70% of cost and 70%+ of value). But a 40–50% market price shock is not outside historical precedent in South Florida and would start to breach that cushion if presales unravel and unsold inventory rises.

## Transmission mechanism to a construction lender if presales fail

Even with strong presale coverage on paper, a construction lender is hurt when:
- Closings don't occur as scheduled
- Loan maturity hits with insufficient sale proceeds to repay principal.
- Lender must extend, refinance, or take title, often while carrying a large, illiquid condo inventory into a soft market.

**Deposit erosion and legal friction**
If many buyers default, deposits are forfeited to the developer, but that cash may be needed to pay down contractor liens, operating costs, or to discount units; it is not a clean 1:1 offset to the construction loan. Litigation risk (buyers challenging enforceability of contracts, alleging misrepresentation or material adverse changes) can delay sales and cloud title.

**Valuation and covenant issues**
Appraised value at or near completion may fall below underwritten exit value; LTV at completion rises, impairing refinance options. If OZK underwrote to >100% presale coverage of the loan (11 of 14 loans fully covered by presales), but actual realized net sale proceeds (after haircuts/discounts) are materially lower, that buffer evaporates quickly.

**Guarantor/sponsor support**
Sponsors may be required to fund interest carry, cost overruns, or shortfalls if presales fail; in a severe downturn their balance sheets or liquidity may not be sufficient, pushing losses up to the lender.

Given OZK's low LTC and high presale coverage, realistic lender loss only emerges when you assume a combined scenario: 30–40% price decline on unsold and defaulted units, plus slower absorption that forces maturity extensions and higher interest carry, plus some erosion in sponsor/guarantor strength, especially on multi‑tower exposure in the same submarket.

## 2. SB 4‑D / SIRS impact on new luxury condos

**Core of SB 4‑D / SIRS after Surfside**

Florida's post‑Surfside regime requires milestone structural inspections for condo/co‑op buildings 3+ stories, generally at 30 years of age (25 years if near the coast) and every 10 years thereafter. Structural Integrity Reserve Studies (SIRS) must identify and fully fund reserves for key structural components (roof, waterproofing, fire systems, elevators, etc.). For associations that must obtain a SIRS, unit owners can no longer waive or underfund reserves for those structural items; the reserve waiver prohibition is effective for budgets adopted on or after December 31, 2024.

**Treatment of new construction**

The SIRS and milestone schedule is age‑triggered: new high‑rise condominiums (3+ habitable stories) are not exempt, but their first milestone inspection is generally due at age 30 (25 on the coast), with SIRS timing aligning with that milestone or by 2025–26 for older existing buildings.

Recent statutory tweaks clarify that SIRS obligations apply only to buildings with three habitable stories or more; certain three‑story configurations with two living floors over a non‑habitable parking level, or small buildings with up to four dwellings and three or fewer habitable stories, are now exempt from SIRS funding, though they still follow older reserve laws. For the kind of branded, 30–60 story towers OZK is financing, these are firmly within SIRS scope; the practical effect is that while inspections and full funding of structural reserves are many years away, developers and marketers must disclose this regime to buyers now, and underwriters factor it into projected HOA dues.

### Does SB 4‑D/SIRS help or hurt new luxury product?

**Relative advantage vs. legacy stock**

Existing, older coastal condos face immediate SIRS and inspection deadlines in 2025–26, with many engineering reports revealing underfunded reserves and large special assessments. These buildings often have legacy construction issues, older systems, and complex remediation, making the all‑in cost of ownership and insurability much worse than pro‑forma buyer expectations a decade ago.

New buildings can be designed and budgeted from day one with code‑compliant structural systems, more resilient envelopes, and a reserve schedule that smooths funding over many years, making them comparatively more attractive despite higher HOAs; this "flight to quality" is already visible in commentary about buyers preferring newer product to avoid uncertain structural/assessment risk.

**Absolute cost headwind even for new**

SIRS rules still raise the structural reserve component of HOAs for new towers versus the pre‑Surfside regime, because boards no longer have the legal option to waive or materially underfund reserves on the structural line items. For ultra‑luxury buildings with extensive amenities and complex mechanical systems, fully funding structural reserves will meaningfully increase monthly dues versus a world where owners could elect to waive them.

Combined with higher insurance and property taxes, the total carrying cost per square foot for a new waterfront luxury condo has moved sharply higher, which can reduce the pool of marginal buyers and potentially nudge some contract holders to re‑assess at closing.

**Net:** for OZK's new construction collateral, SB 4‑D/SIRS is a relative positive versus older product (they are on the right side of the regulatory line), but it still pushes total ownership cost higher and therefore modestly increases demand risk and end‑value sensitivity even in new towers. It also creates headline risk: any post‑completion engineering/defect issue in a branded tower will now surface quickly through mandated studies, which can impact resale pricing and absorption if quality is not first‑rate.

## 3. Insurance crisis and transaction risk

**Current insurance cost trajectory**

Florida remains one of the most expensive states for property insurance; estimates for 2024–25 put average homeowners insurance (for a $300k coverage benchmark) in the roughly $5.7k–$8.7k per year range, more than double to quadruple the U.S. average. For coastal condos, especially high‑rise luxury product, premiums have surged because of hurricane risk, reinsurance costs, and past litigation; although there are early signs of selective premium decreases by some carriers for 2025–26 (e.g., Florida Peninsula seeking rate cuts up to ~12% for condo policies), these are off a very elevated base.

Market commentary notes that high insurance and assessment costs have already made Florida condos "a much less desirable purchase," with evident price softening and sluggish sales in the broader condo market, especially older buildings.

**Impact on luxury condo closings and pre‑sale conversions**

Insurance is part of the HOA line item, and combined with SIRS‑driven reserves, many buildings have seen sharp HOA increases; these hit end‑buyer affordability and bank underwriting simultaneously.

For financed buyers, higher HOA plus insurance can push debt‑to‑income ratios above lender thresholds or make DSCR on investment purchases unattractive, causing some to fail financing contingencies or simply decide not to close. In ultra‑luxury, a significant share of purchases are all‑cash and less DTI‑constrained, but even here, high carrying costs reduce the appeal of low‑yield "pied‑à‑terre" holdings and second/third homes and can slow absorption or encourage contract assignment/dispositions pre‑closing.

Analysts and brokers explicitly cite insurance and condo assessments as key contributors to the 2025 slowdown in existing condo sales and to the "buyer's market" conditions, with high inventory and longer marketing times.

### Evidence of deals falling through on insurance grounds

Direct statistics on "insurance‑driven" closing failures are scarce, but trade stories and brokerage commentary report:
- Buyers walking after discovering that building master policies or unit policies are dramatically more expensive than pro‑forma; in some cases, insurers decline to write coverage at reasonable terms for specific buildings with structural or litigation issues.
- Loan denials where buildings fail lender insurance eligibility criteria, particularly for older towers with open safety/inspection issues under the new regulatory regime.

This behavior is most acute in the older, mid‑market condo stock, not in brand‑new, best‑in‑class luxury towers, which tend to be the easiest to insure; however, the entire market's perception of "Florida condo risk" is affected, which can reduce investor appetite and slow pre‑sales even of new product.

For a construction lender, the insurance channel matters mainly through:
- **Exit cap rates and price levels** – Higher operating costs require higher rents or lower prices; condos cannot raise "rents," so prices/absorption adjust.
- **Financing take‑out** – For buyer end‑loans, lender eligibility criteria for buildings (insurance, SIRS compliance, structural status) can hinder closings, especially for foreign or leveraged buyers.
- **Sponsor operating cash flow** – Increased insurance during construction (builder's risk, flood, wind) and early operations can pressure sponsor liquidity, particularly if sales slow, increasing refinance and covenant risk.

### How this feeds back into OZK's "Florida Paradox"

Pulling this together, a stress case where OZK actually takes losses would look roughly like:
- 30–40% nominal price correction in Miami–Fort Lauderdale–WPB/SWFL luxury condos (consistent with 2008 precedent).
- Higher for‑sale inventory and weak absorption as insurance, HOA (SIRS) and tax costs discourage marginal buyers.
- Material pre‑sale fallout (20–40% of contracts in a given tower either not closing or closing only after discounts), despite strong initial deposit structures.
- Sponsors forced into maturity extensions with unsold inventory and weakened equity, especially in multi‑project exposure (e.g., several loans to the same development group/Miami submarket).

Given the disclosed average LTC ~46% and LTV ~39% and the fact that 11 of 14 Miami condo loans have pre‑sales covering the full loan amount, this scenario likely still leaves some recovery margin for well‑located, branded product, but repeat‑2008 style price declines plus deposit walkaways are sufficient to put real principal at risk on the Florida book if correlated across projects.

The paradox is that the very factors that support OZK's thesis—concentration in new, branded, ultra‑luxury product; presale‑heavy structures; wealthy all‑cash buyers—also concentrate exposure to a relatively small, sentiment‑driven global buyer pool that can step back simultaneously when Florida's regulatory and insurance regime becomes too onerous or when global risk appetite fades.
