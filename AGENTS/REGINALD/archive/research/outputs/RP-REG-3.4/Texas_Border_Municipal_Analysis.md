# **Sovereign Edge, Fiscal Center: A Comprehensive Analysis of Municipal Credit Stress and Regional Banking Interdependence in the Texas Border Region**

## **1\. Executive Intelligence Briefing**

The economic geography of the Texas border region—spanning the Rio Grande Valley (RGV), Laredo, and El Paso—represents a distinct sovereign-subnational interface where international trade dynamics, municipal fiscal policy, and regional banking stability intersect to form a unique credit ecosystem. As of early 2026, this region is characterized by a "divergent resilience," where municipal credit quality remains generally robust due to the structural tailwinds of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), yet faces intensifying localized risks ranging from legacy pension liabilities to existential environmental constraints.

This report provides an exhaustive examination of the credit profiles of seven key municipal entities—the Cities of El Paso, McAllen, Laredo, and Brownsville, and Hidalgo, Cameron, and Webb Counties—and delineates the symbiotic relationship between these public sector entities and the regional banking sector. The banking landscape, dominated by institutions such as Cullen/Frost Bankers (CFR), Prosperity Bancshares (PB), and International Bancshares Corporation (IBC), has become the primary shock absorber for the region's fiscal risks. This interdependence has deepened following the retreat of major global financial institutions from the Texas municipal market due to state-level political sanctions, effectively "domesticating" the region's credit risk within regional balance sheets.

The analysis reveals that while the aggregate credit trajectory is stable-to-positive, evidenced by Brownsville’s historic upgrade to **AA+** by S\&P Global Ratings in 2025 1, systemic vulnerabilities are concentrating in specific nodes. The City of El Paso contends with a structural reliance on high property tax rates and significant underfunding in its locally administered fire and police pension funds.2 Laredo faces immediate credit pressure from water security issues in the Rio Grande basin, a factor explicitly cited by rating agencies as a constraint on its **AA** rating.4 Meanwhile, regional banks have accumulated significant concentrations of Texas municipal debt—Cullen/Frost’s portfolio is 100% Texas-domiciled—creating a closed-loop risk system where bank capital is highly correlated with local fiscal health.5

## ---

**2\. Macro-Regional Economic Framework**

The fiscal health of the target municipalities is inextricably linked to the velocity of cross-border trade and the broader macroeconomic environment of Texas. The region operates as a "logistics super-cluster," leveraging its proximity to Northern Mexico's manufacturing base to generate tax revenues that insulate it from some national economic downturns while exposing it to geopolitical and infrastructure risks.

### **2.1 The Cross-Border Trade Engine and USMCA Maturation**

The maturation of the USMCA has solidified the Texas border as the primary artery for North American commerce. As of 2025, Texas-Mexico trade represents the dominant economic driver for the region, with Mexico remaining Texas' largest trading partner.6 The trend of "nearshoring"—the relocation of supply chains from Asia to Mexico—has catalyzed industrial development in border municipalities, directly bolstering property tax rolls and sales tax receipts.

* **Trade Volume:** Texas-Mexico goods trade accounts for nearly 40% of the total U.S.-Mexico trade volume.7 Laredo, specifically, processes approximately 16,000 trucks daily, cementing its status as the number one land port in the Americas.  
* **Revenue Sensitivity:** Municipal budgets are highly sensitive to this flow. Bridge toll revenues in McAllen and Laredo are not merely ancillary; they are core proprietary revenue streams pledged to debt service and used to subsidize General Fund operations. For instance, McAllen’s international toll bridge system provides critical transfers that allow the city to maintain a lower property tax rate relative to peers.6  
* **Infrastructure Dependency:** The economic model is predicated on physical infrastructure. Any restriction in capacity—whether through federal border policy or physical degradation—poses an immediate threat to municipal liquidity. The recent expansion of the World Trade Bridge in Laredo and the Anzalduas Bridge in McAllen reflects a "build or stagnate" imperative.

### **2.2 Fiscal Environment: The Post-Inflationary Landscape**

The high-inflation period of 2023-2025 fundamentally altered municipal balance sheets. While inflation eroded the purchasing power of capital budgets, it provided a temporary windfall in sales tax collections.

* **Revenue Surges:** Brownsville reported a 112.5% increase in sales tax revenue over the eight-year period ending 2024, a growth rate that has allowed for four consecutive years of property tax rate compression.8 McAllen saw a 2.8% increase in sales tax in FY 2024 alone.6  
* **The "Fiscal Cliff":** As inflation cools in 2026 and federal pandemic-era stimulus (ARPA) expires, municipalities are facing a "fiscal cliff." Cities like El Paso and Fort Worth have reported structural budget gaps as they attempt to absorb personnel costs previously covered by federal grants into their general funds.9 This transition is testing the "No New Revenue" tax rate limits set by Texas state law, forcing tough decisions regarding service levels and fee increases.

### **2.3 The "Texas AG Effect" and Capital Market dislocation**

A defining feature of the current municipal finance landscape is the impact of Texas Senate Bills 13 and 19, which prohibit state and local entities from contracting with financial institutions that "boycott" the fossil fuel or firearms industries.

* **Market Exit:** Major global underwriters, including Barclays, Citigroup, and UBS, have effectively been banned from the Texas municipal bond market.11  
* **Regional Opportunity:** This regulatory environment has created a vacuum that regional banks have aggressively filled. **Texas Capital Bank (TCBI)** has launched a dedicated Public Finance vertical with the explicit goal of becoming a top-3 underwriter in the state, capitalizing on the displacement of Wall Street firms.13 This shift has profound implications: the credit risk of Texas border cities is no longer dispersed globally but is increasingly concentrated within the balance sheets of Texas-based financial institutions.

**3\. The Western Anchor: El Paso Region Analysis**

The El Paso metropolitan area represents the western pole of the Texas border economy. Its integration with Ciudad Juárez creates a massive transnational conurbation with a distinct industrial and fiscal profile.

### **3.1 City of El Paso: Fiscal Structure and Credit Profile**

**Credit Ratings and Outlook:**

* **S\&P Global Ratings:** **AA** (Stable Outlook) – Assigned to Series 2025 General Obligation Bonds in June 2025\.14  
* **Moody’s Investors Service:** **Aa2** (Stable).  
* **Fitch Ratings:** **AA** (Stable).

**Revenue Trends and Tax Policy:**

The City of El Paso operates under a constrained revenue framework. To combat "taxpayer fatigue" in a region with lower-than-average per capita income, the city council has pursued a policy of tax rate compression.

* **Tax Rate Compression:** For the 2024-2025 fiscal cycle, the city adopted a tax rate of approximately **$0.761405 per $100 valuation**, a significant reduction from the \~$0.90 rates seen in the 2020-2021 period.2  
* **Assessment Reliance:** This rate reduction implies that the city is relying entirely on property value appraisals to maintain revenue neutrality or slight growth. The "No New Revenue" Maintenance & Operations (M\&O) tax rate was calculated at $0.530850, leaving little room for discretionary spending expansion without voter approval.2  
* **Revenue Mix:** In FY 2024, the city reported General Fund revenues of $543 million.15 Property taxes remain the largest source, followed by sales tax and franchise fees. The reliance on property tax is structurally higher in El Paso than in McAllen or Laredo, where bridge tolls play a larger role.

**Pension Funding Status: A Critical Weakness:**

El Paso’s most significant long-term credit vulnerability lies in its uniformed service pension funds. Unlike civilian employees who participate in the well-funded Texas Municipal Retirement System (TMRS), El Paso’s fire and police personnel participate in locally administered defined benefit plans under the Texas Local Fire Fighters Retirement Act (TLFFRA).

* **Funding Ratios:** As of the 2024 reporting period, the **El Paso Firemen’s Pension Fund** reported a funded ratio of **75.76%**, while the **El Paso Police Pension Fund** stood at **75.18%**.3  
* **Amortization Stress:** Historically, these funds have experienced amortization periods extending toward "infinity," triggering the requirement for a "Funding Soundness Restoration Plan" (FSRP) under the oversight of the Texas Pension Review Board (PRB).16 While recent reforms and market performance have stabilized the funds, they remain classified as "at-risk" systems relative to the TMRS benchmark (which typically targets 100% funding). The city faces ongoing pressure to increase contribution rates, which competes directly with capital improvement needs in the general budget.

**Debt Profile:** El Paso is a frequent issuer of General Obligation (GO) debt. The "Debt Rate" component of the property tax was $0.230555 in 2024-2025, indicating that nearly 30% of the property tax levy is dedicated to debt service.2 This high fixed-cost burden reduces fiscal flexibility in the event of an economic downturn.

### **3.2 El Paso County: Debt and Governance**

**Credit Profile:**

* **Ratings:** El Paso County generally tracks the city's rating, holding **Aa1/AA+** designations due to a broader tax base that captures industrial developments outside the city limits.  
* **Certificates of Obligation (COs):** The county utilizes non-voter-approved debt instruments extensively. As of August 2024, El Paso County had **$86.5 million** in CO debt outstanding.17 This represents 38.2% of the issuer's tax-supported debt, a figure that rating agencies monitor as a governance factor regarding transparency and voter consent.

**Economic Dependency:** The county’s economic engine is heavily dependent on the Bridge of the Americas (BOTA) and the Ysleta-Zaragoza International Bridge. The recent proposal by the General Services Administration (GSA) to restrict commercial cargo at BOTA was met with fierce opposition from local chambers of commerce, highlighting the region's vulnerability to federal border policy shifts.18 The county's economic resilience is effectively a derivative of the Ciudad Juárez maquiladora output.

**4\. The Logistics Core: Laredo & Webb County Analysis**

Laredo serves as the primary logistics hub for the entire North American continent. Its fiscal profile is dominated by trade-related revenues and an emerging environmental crisis.

### **4.1 City of Laredo: The "Water-Credit" Nexus**

**Credit Ratings:**

* **S\&P Global Ratings:** **AA** (Stable Outlook) – Affirmed September 9, 2025\.4  
* **Moody’s:** **Aa2**.  
* **Fitch:** **AA**.

**Environmental Credit Stress:** In a significant shift for municipal credit analysis, S\&P Global Ratings explicitly cited **environmental factors as "elevated"** in its 2025 rationale for Laredo. The agency noted that "ongoing water delivery uncertainty intensifies credit pressure on utilities in the Rio Grande Basin".4

* **The Risk:** Laredo relies almost exclusively on the Rio Grande for its water supply. Drought conditions and treaty disputes with Mexico regarding water releases have stressed this supply.  
* **Credit Impact:** The inability to guarantee water availability poses a direct threat to the tax base. If the city is forced to implement moratoriums on new water connections (as seen in other drought-stricken Texas communities), commercial and residential development—and thus tax base growth—will halt. S\&P warned that this issue requires "longer-term solutions" to maintain the current rating.

**Pension Liabilities:**

* **Laredo Firefighters Retirement System:** This fund has historically been one of the most distressed in the state. In 2020, the amortization period spiked to **56.8 years**, far beyond the statutory 30-year maximum.16  
* **Status:** As of 2024, the system remains under strict scrutiny. While funded ratios have improved marginally into the **60-65%** range, the city has been forced to negotiate contribution increases. This creates a "crowding out" effect where public safety pension costs consume a growing share of General Fund revenues.

**Debt Issuance:**

Laredo remains active in the capital markets to fund infrastructure. In September 2025, the city issued:

* $31.725 million in Certificates of Obligation.  
* $23.575 million in Tax Notes.  
* $46.14 million in GO Refunding Bonds.4 The use of refunding bonds indicates a proactive management strategy to lower debt service costs in a volatile interest rate environment.

### **4.2 Webb County: A Fiscal Fortress**

**Credit Profile:**

* **S\&P:** **AA** (Stable).19  
* **Fitch:** **AA**.  
* **Moody’s:** **Aa3**.

**Fiscal Strength:**

Webb County presents a stronger fiscal profile than the City of Laredo in several key metrics.

* **Reserves:** The county maintains a General Fund balance exceeding **60%** of annual expenditures, significantly higher than its policy requirement of 35%.19 This reserve provides a massive buffer against economic volatility.  
* **Revenue Growth:** The 2025-2026 budget projects a **$16.2 million (13.04%)** increase in property tax revenue, driven by a 12% increase in assessed valuations.20 This indicates robust underlying economic growth in the logistics and energy sectors within the county.

**Pension Super-Funding:**

A critical differentiator for Webb County is its participation in the **Texas County & District Retirement System (TCDRS)**. Unlike the city's struggling fire plan, the county's pension plan is **overfunded**.

* **Funded Ratio:** Reports indicate a funded ratio of approximately **115-120%**.21  
* **Implication:** This overfunded status allows the county to potentially reduce employer contribution rates in times of stress, providing fiscal flexibility that the City of Laredo lacks.

**5\. The Trade Corridor: Rio Grande Valley Analysis**

The Rio Grande Valley (RGV), anchored by McAllen and Brownsville, has transformed from an agricultural center to a diversified economy driven by healthcare, education, retail, and aerospace.

### **5.1 City of McAllen: The Financial Anchor**

**Credit Ratings:**

* **Fitch Ratings:** **AA+** (Stable Outlook) – Reaffirmed November 2024\.23  
* **S\&P:** **AA+**.  
* **Moody’s:** **Aa2**.

**Revenue Composition and Trends:**

McAllen exhibits a unique revenue structure heavily dependent on cross-border consumerism and toll infrastructure.

* **Sales Tax Dominance:** The city serves as a retail hub for wealthy Mexican nationals. In FY 2024, sales tax revenues increased by **$2.6 million (2.8%)**, continuing a decade-long growth trend where sales tax receipts have risen 52.9% since 2015\.6  
* **Toll Bridge Transfers:** McAllen owns and operates the McAllen-Hidalgo and Anzalduas International Bridges. These assets generate significant surplus revenues that are transferred to the General Fund to subsidize operations. In the 2025 budget, transfers from the Bridge Fund accounted for approximately **12%** of General Fund resources, a critical diversification tool.24

**Pension Status:**

* **Civilian:** The city's TMRS plan is robust, with a funded ratio of **82.6%** as of the 2024 valuation.25  
* **Fire:** The **McAllen Firemen’s Relief and Retirement Fund** has historically been a point of concern. In 2018, the amortization period was "infinite." However, reforms have improved this metric. As of late 2024, the funded ratio stood at **68.7%** with an amortization period of **34.6 years**.26 While improved, it remains a "System at Risk" relative to state guidelines, requiring continued monitoring.

**Budget Strategy:** The city operates with extreme fiscal conservatism. It budgeted for a total General Fund expenditure of **$161.3 million** in FY 2025-2026, anticipating an ending fund balance of **$102.4 million**.27 This level of reserves (over 60% of expenditures) is among the highest for any municipality in the state, underpinning its 'AA+' rating.

### **5.2 City of Brownsville: The Turnaround Story**

**Credit Ratings:**

* **S\&P:** **AA+** (Stable) – Upgraded January 2025\.1  
* **Moody’s:** **Aa3** (Stable) – Affirmed September 2024\.28  
* **Fitch:** **AA**.

**Economic Transformation:**

Brownsville has achieved the most significant credit improvement in the region. The upgrade to **AA+** by S\&P marks a historic high for the city.

* **Drivers:** The upgrade reflects "vigorous economic growth" catalyzed by the aerospace sector (SpaceX at Boca Chica) and the energy sector (LNG export terminals at the Port of Brownsville).  
* **Revenue Growth:** Sales tax collections surged **112.5%** over the eight years ending 2024, an annualized growth rate of 14.1%.8 This explosive growth has allowed the city to lower its property tax rate for four consecutive years while increasing total revenue.  
* **Debt Issuance:** In 2025, Brownsville issued **$54.46 million** in Combination Tax & Revenue Certificates of Obligation to fund streets and downtown improvements.1

### **5.3 Hidalgo & Cameron Counties**

**Hidalgo County:**

* **Credit:** Rated **AA/Aa2**.  
* **Debt:** The county is the largest issuer of Certificates of Obligation in the region, with **$326.2 million** outstanding as of 2024\.17 This reflects the immense capital needs of a rapidly urbanizing county managing complex drainage systems.  
* **Pension:** Healthy TCDRS funded ratio of **86%**.29

**Cameron County:**

* **Credit:** Rated **AA** (Stable) by S\&P as of November 2025\.30  
* **Risk Factors:** S\&P explicitly flags **"extreme weather events"** (hurricanes) as a neutral-to-negative credit factor. While the county has financial resilience, its coastal exposure represents a perpetual catastrophic risk to the tax base.  
* **Outlook:** Stable, supported by pledged revenue stability from toll bridges and parks, but constrained by lower reserve levels per capita compared to Hidalgo.

**6\. Structural Vulnerability: Public Pension Systems**

A bifurcated pension risk landscape exists in the region.

| Entity Type | Pension System | Funding Mechanism | Funded Ratio Status (2024/2025) | Risk Level |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Counties (Webb, Hidalgo)** | **TCDRS** | Cash Balance | **86% \- 120%** 22 | **Low/Positive** |
| **City Civilians** | **TMRS** | Cash Balance | **\~82% \- 89%** 25 | **Low** |
| **City Fire/Police (El Paso, Laredo, McAllen)** | **TLFFRA/Local** | Defined Benefit | **60% \- 75%** 3 | **High** |

**Analysis:**

The "Sovereign Edge" faces a distinct internal liability crisis in its uniformed services. While the state-administered systems (TMRS/TCDRS) are models of solvency, the locally managed fire and police funds in El Paso, Laredo, and McAllen suffer from structural deficits.

* **Impact:** The amortization periods for these funds often exceed the 30-year statutory maximum, forcing cities to divert general tax revenues into pension stabilization rather than infrastructure. This creates a "service solvency" risk where future budgets are consumed by legacy costs.

**7\. Regional Banking Ecosystem: Market Structure & Exposure**

The banking sector in the Texas border region is a mix of large regional consolidators and entrenched local sovereigns.

### **7.1 The "Big Regionals": Exposure and Strategy**

**Cullen/Frost Bankers (CFR):**

* **Municipal Bond Dominance:** Cullen/Frost acts as a primary repository for Texas municipal credit risk. As of Q3 2024, **100%** of its available-for-sale municipal bond portfolio ($5.2 billion) consisted of securities issued by Texas entities.5  
  * *Risk Mitigation:* Approximately **72.6%** of this portfolio is guaranteed by the Texas Permanent School Fund (PSF) or is pre-refunded, mitigating default risk. However, the bank remains highly exposed to interest rate risk and the general liquidity of the Texas municipal market.  
* **Expansion:** Frost is aggressively expanding its branch footprint in the RGV, competing directly for high-value commercial deposits.

**Prosperity Bancshares (PB):**

* **Consolidation Strategy:** Prosperity has cemented its position as the second-largest bank by deposit share in Texas community markets.31  
* **Recent M\&A:** In 2025/2026, Prosperity completed the acquisitions of **American Bank** (Corpus Christi) and **Lone Star State Bank of West Texas**.32 This deepens its exposure to the coastal and western border regions. Note: Prosperity did *not* acquire Lone Star National Bank of Pharr, which remains independent.  
* **Footprint:** The bank operates **30 locations** in the South Texas area alone 33, making it a systemic lender to the region's small business sector.

**Texas Capital Bank (TCBI):**

* **Public Finance Pivot:** Identifying the "Barclays Void," Texas Capital has launched a dedicated public finance business to underwrite Texas municipal debt.13  
* **Exposure:** This strategy increases TCBI's direct credit exposure to municipalities like El Paso and McAllen, moving beyond lending to becoming a market maker for their debt.  
* **Sector Bets:** TCBI recently acquired a **$400 million healthcare portfolio**.34 Given the dominance of the healthcare sector in McAllen and El Paso economies, this likely includes significant border region exposure.

**Independent Bank Group (IBTX):**

* **Exposure:** Moderate. While active in Texas, IBTX's loan portfolio is weighted toward the "Texas Triangle" (Dallas/Houston/Austin) and Colorado. It does not exhibit the same depth of concentration in the border region as Prosperity or IBC.

### **7.2 The "Local Sovereigns": Deep Concentration Risk**

**International Bancshares Corp (IBC):**

* **Laredo Dominance:** Headquartered in Laredo with **$16.6 billion** in assets, IBC is the dominant financial institution on the border.35  
* **Existential Risk:** IBC explicitly lists "Mexico political and economic risk" as a material risk factor in its SEC filings.36 Its balance sheet is a direct reflection of cross-border trade velocity. It serves as a primary depository for Laredo and Webb County, creating a tight liquidity loop between the bank and the local government.

**Lone Star National Bank (Pharr):**

* **RGV Powerhouse:** With **$3.2 billion** in assets 37, Lone Star National controls a significant share of retail deposits in Hidalgo County. It remains independent, serving as a key lender for local commercial real estate.

**WestStar Bank (El Paso):**

* **Western Hub:** With **$3.1 billion** in assets 38, WestStar is the dominant local bank in El Paso. It is deeply exposed to the El Paso commercial real estate market and the maquiladora supply chain.

**8\. The Credit-Collateral Nexus**

The intersection of municipal finance and banking creates specific systemic risks.

### **8.1 The "Barclays Void" and Regional Underwriting**

The exit of global banks from Texas municipal underwriting has forced border cities to rely on regional banks.

* **Concentration:** When Brownsville issues COs, the debt is increasingly likely to be held or underwritten by a syndicate involving **Frost**, **Texas Capital**, or **Piper Sandler**. This concentrates the credit risk of a hurricane in Cameron County or a water crisis in Laredo within the Texas regional banking system, rather than dispersing it to global capital markets.

### **8.2 Deposit Collateralization and Liquidity Crowding**

Texas law requires public funds to be collateralized by high-quality securities.

* **Mechanism:** As municipalities like McAllen build massive reserve funds ($100M+), local banks like Rio Bank or Lone Star National must pledge Treasuries or Agency MBS to secure these deposits.  
* **Risk:** This ties up the banks' high-quality liquid assets (HQLA). In a rising rate environment, the value of this collateral falls, requiring banks to pledge *more* securities. This can theoretically crowd out private sector lending capacity as bank balance sheets become encumbered by municipal wealth.

**9\. Strategic Risk Scenarios & Outlook**

### **9.1 Scenario A: The Water Crisis Shock**

* **Trigger:** Amistad and Falcon reservoir levels drop to critical lows, forcing Laredo to halt new water taps.  
* **Impact:** Construction stops. Tax base growth flatlines. S\&P downgrades Laredo to A-range.  
* **Bank Contagion:** Local banks (IBC, Falcon) see a spike in NPLs from developer loans. Collateral values for CRE drop.

### **9.2 Scenario B: The Trade Disruption**

* **Trigger:** Federal border closure due to migration or cartel violence.  
* **Impact:** Bridge toll revenues (McAllen/Laredo) collapse. Municipal debt service coverage ratios tighten.  
* **Bank Contagion:** IBC's trade finance book takes losses. Sales tax receipts plummet, widening municipal budget deficits.

### **9.3 Conclusion**

The Texas border region is a financial ecosystem defined by **resilience through isolation**. The region has insulated itself from national commercial office distress but created its own unique risk profile centered on water, trade, and pensions. The regional banks act as the primary shock absorbers.

**Final Assessment:**

* **Brownsville & McAllen (AA+):** Stable, diversified, and resilient.  
* **El Paso & Laredo (AA):** Stable but structurally strained by pensions and water respectively.  
* **Banking Sector:** **Cullen/Frost** and **Texas Capital** are the winners of the new "domesticated" municipal market, but **IBC** and **WestStar** retain the critical, if concentrated, role of funding the trade corridors that power the entire system.

**Strategic Recommendation:** Stakeholders must monitor the **funded status of local fire pensions** as a leading indicator of fiscal rigidity, and **Rio Grande water levels** as a leading indicator of economic capacity. These physical and actuarial metrics are now as critical to regional creditworthiness as interest rates.
