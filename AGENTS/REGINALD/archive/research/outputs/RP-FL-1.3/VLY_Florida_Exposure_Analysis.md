# **Strategic Analysis of Valley National Bank’s Commercial Real Estate Exposure in the Florida Market**

The expansion of Valley National Bank into the Florida commercial real estate (CRE) sector represents a defining chapter in the institution's nearly century-long history. Originally a regional player rooted in the New Jersey and New York tri-state area, the bank has aggressively repositioned itself to capture the high-growth dynamics of the Southeastern United States.1 As of the final quarter of 2025, Valley National Bank manages a Florida CRE portfolio valued at approximately $7.4 billion, a figure that accounts for 28% of its total commercial real estate loan book.3 This exposure is not merely a statistical concentration but a fundamental shift in the bank's risk-reward profile, as it navigates the unique stressors of the Florida market, including a significant condominium reserve crisis, escalating property insurance premiums, and shifting legislative mandates regarding structural integrity.3

The institution's total loan portfolio, which stood at $50.1 billion by the end of 2025, is anchored by a $29.2 billion commercial real estate segment.6 The management of the Florida portion of this book requires a nuanced understanding of geographic sub-markets, as the risks inherent in the Miami-Dade corridor differ substantially from those in the Tampa or Orlando regions. By examining the geographic distribution, asset-type composition, and the regulatory environment governing Florida real estate, a comprehensive picture emerges of an institution attempting to balance aggressive growth with sophisticated de-risking strategies, such as the strategic sale of performing loans and a shift toward owner-occupied lending.7

## **Geographic Distribution and MSA Concentration Analysis**

The geographic footprint of Valley National Bank in Florida is heavily weighted toward the state's most populous and economically active metropolitan statistical areas (MSAs). This concentration is a legacy of strategic acquisitions, most notably the purchase of USAmeriBank and CNLBank, which provided the bank with immediate scale in the Tampa and Orlando markets, respectively.2 The distribution as of mid-2025 reveals a tiered exposure model that prioritizes the "South Florida" wealth corridor while maintaining significant secondary positions in the Gulf Coast and Central Florida regions.3

### **Distribution of Florida/Alabama CRE Portfolio by MSA**

| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach | $3,330 | 45% |
| Tampa-St. Petersburg-Clearwater | $1,406 | 19% |
| Orlando-Kissimmee-Sanford | $962 | 13% |

| **Total Florida/Alabama CRE** | **$7,400** | **100%** |

The Miami-Fort Lauderdale-West Palm Beach MSA serves as the dominant anchor, representing 45% of the regional exposure.3 This $3.33 billion concentration places the bank at the center of the nation's most active but volatile real estate markets. The South Florida market has been a primary recipient of domestic net migration and corporate relocations, which has historically supported property values and rental growth.11 However, this region is also the epicenter of the structural integrity crisis following the Surfside collapse, making the bank's exposure here a focal point for risk assessment.12

The Tampa-St. Petersburg-Clearwater MSA, representing $1.406 billion or 19% of the exposure, reflects the bank's strong presence on the Gulf Coast.3 Tampa has emerged as a major hub for financial services and healthcare, sectors that the bank has targeted through its specialty lending verticals.9 The Orlando-Kissimmee-Sanford MSA follows with $962 million, or 13% of the portfolio, where the local economy is heavily influenced by the tourism and hospitality sectors, as well as an expanding industrial base.3

The remaining 23% of the exposure, totaling $1.702 billion, is categorized as "Other," encompassing smaller Florida markets and parts of Alabama.3 This diversified bucket likely includes markets such as Jacksonville and the Florida Panhandle, where the bank’s Crestview-Fort Walton Beach-Destin MSA assessment area encompasses Okaloosa and Walton counties.14 In these more rural or secondary markets, the bank’s portfolio tends to be more heavily weighted toward residential properties and small business loans.14

## **Asset Class Composition and Underwriting Standards**

A detailed examination of the $7.4 billion Florida and Alabama portfolio reveals a focus on traditional commercial asset classes, with office and multifamily properties being the most prominent. The bank's underwriting philosophy in these segments is characterized by a high degree of collateralization and a preference for strong cash flow metrics, as evidenced by the weighted average Loan-to-Value (LTV) and Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) figures.3

### **Portfolio Metrics for Florida and Alabama Assets**

| Asset Category | Exposure (USD) | Weighted Avg. LTV | Weighted Avg. DSCR | Avg. Loan Size |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Office (FL/AL) | $1.1 Billion | 58% | 2.08x | $1.6 Million |
| Non-Co-op Multifamily (FL/AL) | $1.1 Billion | 60% | 1.41x | $3.8 Million |
| Total Regional CRE | $7.4 Billion | 60% | 1.82x | N/A |

The office sector in Florida represents $1.1 billion of exposure, which is notably 37% of the bank's total office-related loan book.3 Despite the national narrative of an office sector in decline, Valley's Florida office portfolio maintains a robust weighted average DSCR of 2.08x and an LTV of 58%.3 The relatively small average loan size of $1.6 million suggests that the bank is not primarily financing large, downtown "trophy" towers that are most susceptible to high vacancy rates.3 Instead, the portfolio appears to favor suburban medical offices, professional suites, and mixed-use properties that serve local service economies. This granular approach mitigates the risk of a single large tenant departure causing a localized default.16

In the multifamily segment, the bank holds another $1.1 billion in Florida and Alabama loans, representing 18% of its total non-co-op multifamily portfolio.3 The underwriting metrics here are more compressed than in the office sector, with a DSCR of 1.41x and an LTV of 60%.3 This lower DSCR is reflective of the broader "insurance stress" in Florida, where soaring property insurance premiums have significantly increased operating expenses for apartment owners, thereby reducing the net operating income (NOI) available for debt service.11 The DSCR formula, ![][image1], demonstrates that any inflationary pressure on expenses—such as the 30% to 50% insurance premium hikes seen in some Florida markets—directly impacts a borrower's ability to maintain these coverage ratios.18

The bank also maintains exposure to industrial and retail sectors in Florida, although specific dollar amounts for these segments are not publicly itemized at the regional level.3 Market data indicates that Florida's industrial sector remained the strongest asset class through 2025, with vacancy rates staying low in major logistics nodes like Miami and Tampa.11 Retail assets in Florida have shown a "patchwork" performance, with prime tourist-oriented centers in Orlando and Miami performing well, while secondary strip centers face more scrutiny from lenders.11

## **The Florida Condominium Crisis: Structural and Financial Repercussions**

One of the most significant external risks to the Florida CRE portfolio is the legislative response to the 2021 Surfside building collapse. Florida Senate Bill 4-D, enacted in 2022, and its 2023 follow-up, SB 154 (the "glitch bill"), have fundamentally altered the financial landscape for condominium associations.5 These laws mandate that buildings three stories or higher must undergo milestone inspections and structural integrity reserve studies (SIRS).5

### **Legislative Requirements for Florida Condominiums**

| Mandate | Statutory Deadline | Financial Implication |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Milestone Inspection (Phase 1\) | Dec 31, 2024 / 2025 | Visual assessment of structural health |
| Structural Integrity Reserve Study | Dec 31, 2024 | Requirement to identify repair costs |
| Elimination of Reserve Waivers | Jan 1, 2025 | Forced collection of 100% of reserve needs |

The most critical change for lenders is the elimination of the ability for associations to vote to waive or underfund their reserves.12 Historically, many Florida condo boards kept monthly fees low by deferring maintenance and failing to fund reserves for major structural components like roofs, plumbing, and electrical systems.5 As of January 1, 2025, any budget adopted by an association must include 100% of the recommended reserve funding based on the SIRS.5 For older buildings, particularly those 30 years or older in coastal areas, the resulting special assessments or fee increases can be staggering, sometimes reaching hundreds of thousands of dollars per unit owner.5

Valley National Bank has positioned itself to both mitigate this risk and capitalize on it. The bank has a dedicated community association lending division that provides loans and lines of credit to HOAs and condominium associations.23 These loans are typically used to fund the very repairs mandated by the milestone inspections.4 Crucially, the bank's association loans are generally secured by the association's ability to levy assessments and its receivables, rather than a physical mortgage on the property.25 This structure is advantageous because it allows the bank to avoid the "foreclosure crisis" at the individual unit level; the bank’s primary concern is the collective cash flow of the association rather than the market value of a single distressed unit.24

However, the risk remains that if a significant percentage of unit owners within an association default on their fees due to the "reserve shock," the association itself could become insolvent.24 To counter this, the bank’s underwriters analyze factors such as the number of leased units, the history of assessment collections, and the presence of any pending litigation.24 The bank also integrates treasury management solutions for these associations, ensuring that reserve funds are held in FDIC-insured accounts, such as HOA-specific CDARS and money market sweep accounts, which provide liquidity and security for the association’s capital.23

## **Insurance Stress and Escrow Management**

The property insurance crisis in Florida is a systemic pressure that affects every loan in the CRE book. Rising premiums are driven by a combination of litigation costs, reinsurance market volatility, and the increasing frequency of severe weather events.3 Valley National Bank manages this risk primarily through its annual escrow analysis process.27

The escrow analysis is performed once a year based on the loan's origination date.27 This process calculates the estimated amount needed to pay real estate taxes and insurance bills for the upcoming 12 months, plus a "cushion" or "required low balance" of up to two months, as permitted by the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA).27 When insurance premiums spike—as they have across Florida—the analysis typically reveals an "escrow shortage" or a "deficiency".27

### **Definitions of Escrow Conditions**

| Condition | Definition | Impact on Borrower |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Shortage | Positive balance, but insufficient for future bills | Increased monthly payments over 12 months |
| Deficiency | Negative balance in the escrow account | Repayment required, often over 2 months |
| Required Low Balance | A "cushion" maintained in the account | Limits the risk of a zero-balance event |

If a borrower fails to maintain a required insurance policy, the bank utilizes Lender-Placed Insurance (LPI), often referred to as "forced-place" insurance.28 While LPI protects the bank's interest in the physical collateral, it typically only covers the outstanding loan balance and is significantly more expensive than standard policies.28 This can create a "debt spiral" for marginal borrowers, where the high cost of forced-place insurance further compromises their ability to make mortgage payments, potentially leading to the "rising foreclosures" mentioned in the research prompt.28

For properties in Special Flood Hazard Areas, the bank strictly enforces flood insurance requirements, often requiring the escrowing of premiums regardless of other loan terms.28 The maximum coverage available through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) is frequently insufficient for large commercial assets, requiring the bank to mandate supplemental private flood insurance to fully protect the collateral value.28

## **Risk Mitigation and the Brookfield Transaction**

A central component of Valley National Bank's strategy in 2024 and 2025 has been the aggressive reduction of its CRE concentration ratio. In early 2024, the bank's ratio of CRE loans (excluding owner-occupied) to risk-based capital was near 400%, a level that attracts significant regulatory scrutiny from the FDIC and the Federal Reserve.7 By the end of 2025, this ratio had been successfully driven down to 333%.6

The most impactful single event in this de-risking effort was the December 2024 sale of approximately $1 billion ($925 million net) in performing CRE loans to Brookfield Asset Management.7 This transaction was notable for its execution at only a 1% discount to the par value of the loans.7 In a market where many regional banks were struggling to offload CRE exposure without taking massive 10% to 20% haircuts, the Brookfield deal signaled that Valley’s portfolio was comprised of high-quality, well-performing assets.7

### **Strategic Impact of the Brookfield Loan Sale**

| Strategic Objective | Result of Transaction |
| :---- | :---- |
| Concentration Reduction | Accelerated move toward 375% (and eventually 333%) target |
| Capital Preservation | 1% discount limited the hit to retained earnings |
| Relationship Maintenance | Bank retained servicing rights, keeping the customer relationship |
| Liquidity Generation | Provided capital for higher-yielding C\&I loan originations |

By selling "performing" loans, the bank was able to improve its risk profile without the "fire sale" optics of a distressed asset disposal.7 The retention of servicing rights is particularly significant, as it allows the bank to continue earning fee income from the loans while removing the credit risk and capital requirements from its balance sheet.7 This "asset-light" model is a key reason why the bank was able to report record earnings in the fourth quarter of 2025 despite the challenging CRE environment.9

## **Asset Quality: Non-accruals and Specific Credits**

While the overall asset quality of the bank has been stable, with net charge-offs for 2025 falling to 24 basis points, certain segments of the Florida book have shown signs of stress.9 Non-accrual loans totaled $433.9 million by the end of 2025, representing 0.87% of total loans.6 This represents a slight uptick from the 0.72% reported mid-year.31

A specific case study in this trend is the migration of a $35 million construction loan to non-accrual status in the third quarter of 2025\.32 Management noted that this migration was driven by updated appraisals that reflected the higher cost of completion and a slower absorption rate in the local market.32 However, the bank does not anticipate a loss on this credit, as the current appraisal still supports the loan-to-value requirements.33 This exemplifies the "extend and pretend" or "work-out" culture currently prevalent in the banking sector, where lenders prefer to restructure loans or wait for projects to reach completion rather than forcing a foreclosure that would crystalize a loss in a stagnant market.20

The bank’s construction loan portfolio, which includes many of these high-risk Florida projects, decreased significantly in 2025, falling from $3.3 billion to $2.5 billion.8 This decline was largely due to the completion of projects and their migration into the permanent CRE portfolio.31 This transition is positive for the bank’s risk profile, as completed and occupied buildings are generally easier to finance and carry less risk than properties still under construction.20

## **Competitive Landscape and Market Sentiment**

Valley National Bank operates in a highly competitive Florida market, where it vies for market share against national giants like JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo, as well as regional competitors like BankUnited and M\&T Bank.15 The competitive advantage for Valley has historically been its "relationship-focused" strategy, which emphasizes personalized service and local decision-making.9 This is particularly evident in the "specialty healthcare vertical" and the "Southeast franchise," where the bank has leveraged its expertise to win clients that might be overlooked by larger, more bureaucratic institutions.9

However, market sentiment regarding regional banks with high CRE exposure remained "cautiously optimistic" but wary through 2025\.20 High-profile issues at other institutions, such as New York Community Bancorp, which faced a credit downgrade due to its CRE concentration, served as a warning sign for the industry.21 Valley’s ability to outperform its peers in terms of stock price and profitability in late 2025 was largely attributed to its successful capital management and its clear communication regarding its de-risking efforts.9

The bank's use of "Proptech" and data-driven insights is another differentiator.38 By leveraging technology to build a scalable infrastructure and remove redundancies, the bank aims to create a better customer experience while simultaneously improving its ability to monitor credit risk in real-time.38 This technological commitment is essential for managing a portfolio that is 28% exposed to the complexities of the Florida market.3

## **Economic Indicators and the "Maturity Wall"**

As the bank looks toward 2026, it must navigate what many analysts call the "CRE maturity wall." Nationwide, approximately $957 billion in commercial real estate loans are scheduled to mature in 2025, with another $539 billion in 2026\.17 These loans were largely originated in a low-interest-rate environment, and their refinancing at current market rates poses a significant risk to debt service coverage ratios.17

### **National CRE Loan Maturity Forecast (USD Billions)**

| Year | Maturing Loan Volume | % of Total Outstanding |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 2025 | $957 Billion | 20% |
| 2026 | $539 Billion | 11% |
| 2027 | $550 Billion | 11% |
| 20-Year Average | $350 Billion | N/A |

In Florida, the impact of this maturity wall is amplified by the insurance and condo reserve issues already discussed. Borrowers who might have qualified for refinancing based on their property value are now finding that their NOI is insufficient to support the higher interest payments.39 Valley’s strategy of shifting toward "owner-occupied" CRE is a direct response to this risk.6 Owner-occupied loans are generally viewed as lower risk because the borrower's business operations—rather than third-party rental income—provide the primary source of repayment.6 By the end of 2025, owner-occupied loans were a primary driver of the bank's sequential growth, while "transactional" or non-relationship CRE was allowed to run off.6

## **Future Outlook and Management Guidance for 2026**

The management of Valley National Bank has provided specific guidance for 2026 that reinforces its commitment to the Florida market while continuing its diversification efforts. The bank expects mid-single-digit overall loan growth, with a target of 10% growth in the C\&I sector and only low-single-digit growth in the CRE sector.9

### **2026 Operational and Financial Targets**

| Metric | Target / Guidance | Strategic Intent |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Overall Loan Growth | Mid-single digit | Balanced growth across segments |
| C\&I Loan Growth | \~10% | Diversification away from CRE |
| CRE Loan Growth | Low-single digit | Controlled exposure and runoff |
| Net Interest Margin (NIM) | \>3.1% | Profitability through asset repricing |
| Loan-to-Deposit Ratio | Targeting 90% | Improved liquidity and funding profile |

A key component of this guidance is the reduction of the loan-to-deposit ratio to 90%.32 To achieve this, the bank is focusing on "relationship-driven" deposit gathering, particularly in its Southeast franchise.9 In the fourth quarter of 2025, the bank grew core deposits by $4 billion, a 9% year-over-year increase, which allowed it to pay off higher-cost brokered deposits and FHLB advances.9 This improved funding mix is essential for maintaining the net interest margin (NIM), which is expected to expand by another 15-20 basis points in 2026 as lower-yielding fixed-rate loans reprice into a higher-rate environment.9

## **Conclusions and Risk Profile Synthesis**

The analysis of Valley National Bank's $7.4 billion Florida CRE exposure suggests a sophisticated, multi-pronged approach to risk management. The bank's concentration in the Miami MSA and its significant exposure to the office and multifamily sectors are balanced by conservative underwriting metrics, a pivot toward owner-occupied lending, and a demonstrated ability to execute large-scale loan sales to institutional partners like Brookfield.3

The Florida condominium crisis and the state’s property insurance stress are formidable challenges that will continue to pressure the loan book through 2026\. However, the bank’s specialized HOA lending unit and its rigorous escrow analysis process provide structural mitigants that are not present in more generalist lending institutions.24 The reduction of the CRE concentration ratio to 333% by late 2025 significantly reduces the institution’s vulnerability to a broad CRE downturn and satisfies regulatory expectations for capital adequacy.6

Ultimately, the "specific composition and risk profile" of VLY's Florida exposure is one of managed volatility. The bank is deeply embedded in the Florida economy and stands to benefit from the state's continued population and business growth.9 However, its success will depend on its ability to navigate the "maturity wall" and the escalating costs of property ownership in the Southeast. By prioritizing relationship-based lending and maintaining a fortified balance sheet with a 10.5%-11% CET1 ratio, Valley National Bank is positioning itself to be a long-term winner in the Florida market, even as short-term headwinds persist.9
