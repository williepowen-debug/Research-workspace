# **Strategic Assessment of Nevada Municipal Credit Markets and Regional Bank Securities Exposure: A 2025-2026 Fiscal Perspective**

The municipal credit landscape in Nevada has undergone a profound structural transformation over the last two decades, evolving from a posture of high-growth vulnerability during the 2008 financial crisis to one of institutionalized fiscal resilience in the mid-2020s. This transition is characterized by a sophisticated interplay between robust reserve accumulation, the diversification of once-monolithic local economies, and the aggressive management of existential environmental risks, particularly concerning water security in the Colorado River Basin. For institutional investors and regional banking entities, Nevada represents a complex credit environment where high investment-grade ratings are increasingly supported by statutory mechanisms that mandate fiscal discipline. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the state's credit conditions, the fiscal health of its primary political subdivisions, the specialized credit profile of its regional water authorities, and the nuanced exposure of the commercial banking sector to these municipal instruments.

## **The Macroeconomic Framework and Sovereign Credit Profile**

The State of Nevada enters the 2025-2026 biennium with a sovereign credit profile defined by significant liquidity and a stabilized revenue outlook. Major rating agencies have converged on a high investment-grade assessment, with S\&P Global Ratings affirming a 'AA+' long-term rating on the state’s limited-tax general obligation (GO) bonds and a 'AA' rating on its appropriation-backed certificates of participation (COPs).1 Moody’s Investors Service maintains a comparable 'Aa1' rating, reflecting the state’s high creditworthiness relative to other U.S. states.2 This positioning is a direct result of the state’s adherence to statutory mechanisms for building reserves during economic expansions and maintaining a low debt burden as a proportion of the total budget.1

### **Economic Indicators and Revenue Trajectories**

Nevada’s real Gross State Product (GSP) per capita has demonstrated a steady upward trajectory, reaching an estimated $63,361 in 2024\.1 While this remains slightly below the national average—at approximately 93% of U.S. PCPI—it represents a significant recovery from the economic dislocations of the early 2020s.1 The state’s financial performance, as adjusted by S\&P Global Ratings, shows an operating fund revenue of $6.568 billion projected for 2025, with an operating result representing 11.8% of revenue.1

The General Fund revenue forecast, provided by the Economic Forum in December 2024, projects a 2.4% increase to $6.276 billion in fiscal year 2026 and a further 2.9% increase to $6.457 billion in 2027\.3 These projections are notably 43% to 46% above pre-pandemic peaks, suggesting that the inflationary environment and organic growth in the leisure and hospitality sectors have permanently reset the state’s revenue floor.3 However, the state’s fiscal staff remains cautious regarding the volatility of mineral-revenue projections, which are often excluded from long-term planning-level projections to maintain conservative debt capacity estimates.4

### **Debt Capacity and Affordability Constraints**

A critical component of Nevada’s credit narrative is the management of its General Obligation (GO) debt capacity. The State Treasury’s 2025-2027 Affordability Analysis suggests that the state can issue approximately $2.1 billion in GO debt over the next three biennia without breaching constitutional limits or adversely affecting credit ratings.4 This capacity is driven by the rapid amortization of existing debt, with approximately 57% of current state debt scheduled to be retired within five years and 80% within ten years.4

Nevertheless, the 2025 legislative session highlighted the tension between capital needs and bonding limits. The Legislature authorized approximately $1.5 billion in bond funding, which exceeded the Treasurer's original affordability projection of $1.2 billion by roughly $253 million.6 While Treasurer Zach Conine noted that this does not create an immediate fiscal vacuum, it effectively reduces the affordability range for future budget cycles, as the state must prioritize the repayment of these accelerated issuances.6 This legislative propensity for higher bonding, specifically for housing initiatives and school facility modernization, remains a secondary credit consideration for agencies monitoring the state's long-term leverage profile.4

## **Institutional Credit Profiles: Major Nevada Issuers**

The decentralization of Nevada’s economic growth has led to divergent credit stories across its primary political subdivisions. While the Las Vegas metropolitan area remains the dominant force, Northern Nevada and the southern peripheral cities have developed distinct credit identities supported by specialized tax liens and diversification efforts.

### **Clark County and the Southern Economic Engine**

Clark County, encompassing the Las Vegas Strip and the surrounding metropolitan area, holds the state’s most prestigious municipal credit rating. S\&P Global Ratings assigned a 'AAA' rating to the county's series 2025 GO refunding bonds, affirming the county’s extraordinarily large tax base and its demonstrated resilience through economic cycles.7 The county’s stable outlook is supported by a robust reserve position and a prudent financial management framework that has successfully navigated the transition from a gaming-dependent economy to a broader entertainment and logistics hub.7

As of June 30, 2025, Clark County’s total general obligation debt subject to the 10% of assessed value (AV) limit stood at $2.627 billion.8 When including self-supporting bond bank bonds, such as those issued for the Southern Nevada Water Authority (SNWA), the total GO debt rises to $3.272 billion.8 The county’s debt management policy emphasizes maintaining direct debt at levels manageable relative to population growth and per capita income, with a strategic focus on prepayment options to capture savings during periods of interest rate volatility.8

### **The Ascendance of Henderson and North Las Vegas**

The City of Henderson has maintained a 'AA+' rating from S\&P since 2016, positioning it as one of the highest-rated incorporated cities in the state.9 Henderson’s credit strength is derived from its "very strong" economy and its success in minimizing exposure to the tourism and gaming industries through the recruitment of technology and manufacturing firms, including Google and Amazon.9 The city’s fiscal framework is anchored by a minimum reserve policy of 16.6% for its general fund and a property tax rate that remains among the lowest in the region, ranging from $0.71 to $0.77 per $100 of AV.9

In contrast, the City of North Las Vegas represents a notable "turnaround" story in municipal finance. Once on the brink of state receivership following the 2008 crisis, the city received upgrades to 'AA-' from S\&P and 'A+' from Fitch in late 2024\.12 These upgrades reflect the city's disciplined approach to budgeting, the reduction of long-term liabilities, and the maintenance of an ending fund balance greater than 18%.13 North Las Vegas has capitalized on its vast tracts of available land to become a logistics hub, which has diversified its revenue streams away from residential property taxes and gaming fees.13

### **Northern Nevada and Washoe County Dynamics**

The Northern Nevada economy, centered on Reno and Washoe County, has seen a similar trend of credit improvement. In March 2024, Moody's upgraded the City of Reno’s GOLT bond ratings from 'A1' to 'Aa3', citing consistent economic growth and a diversified revenue stream.14 This action impacted $525 million in outstanding obligations and reflected the city's robust reserve levels and prudent fiscal management.14

Washoe County holds a 'Aa2' rating from Moody's, while the Washoe County School District maintains a 'AA' rating from S\&P.15 The school district’s credit is particularly interesting due to its use of multiple revenue streams; while its GO bonds are secured by a full-faith-and-credit property tax pledge, they are additionally secured by a 0.54% sales-and-use tax.15 This dual-security structure provides a buffer against property tax volatility, though rating agencies primarily evaluate the credit based on the strength of the GOLT pledge.15

## **Water Authority Credit: Southern Nevada Water Authority (SNWA)**

In the context of Nevada's arid geography, the creditworthiness of its water agencies is a fundamental pillar of regional stability. The Southern Nevada Water Authority (SNWA) manages the regional water supply for the Las Vegas Valley, a responsibility that carries significant capital intensity and environmental risk.

### **Credit Evolution and Financial Performance**

The SNWA’s credit trajectory over the last 15 years reflects the broader institutionalization of fiscal discipline in Nevada. In 2011, S\&P lowered the SNWA’s issuer credit rating (ICR) to 'A+' due to a lack of willingness to increase wholesale rates to levels sufficient for 1x debt service coverage without relying on cash reserves.17 At that time, the authority was struggling with a precipitous decrease in connection charge revenue following the housing collapse.17

However, by February 2025, S\&P raised the SNWA’s ICR to 'AA+', citing its demonstrated resilience and stable financial performance through both a prolonged drought cycle and multiple economic recessions.18 The authority’s ability to use conservative forecasting to mitigate hydrological volatility has become a cornerstone of its credit profile.18 The SNWA's revenue base is highly concentrated among its member agencies, with the Las Vegas Valley Water District accounting for 64.6% of deliveries, followed by Henderson at 19.6% and North Las Vegas at 13.5%.18

### **Capital Needs and Rate Adjustment Capacity**

The SNWA’s capital improvement plan is driven by the need to ensure supply reliability amidst declining Lake Mead elevations. As of late 2025, combined water storage in the Colorado River's primary reservoirs was at 31% of capacity.19 In response, the SNWA has implemented a multi-year schedule of rate increases to fund both infrastructure and conservation programs.

The Integrated Resource Planning Advisory Committee (IRPAC) recommendations, adopted in 2020, provided the political and fiscal roadmap for these increases, ensuring that the authority can fund its "pay-go" portion of capital expenditures while excluding recurring operating expenses from connection charge revenues.20 This structural separation of capital and operating funds is viewed by credit analysts as a significant qualitative strength.21

## **The Pension Paradigm: NVPERS and Long-term Liabilities**

The Public Employees' Retirement System of Nevada (NVPERS) represents the largest long-term liability for Nevada’s municipal issuers. The health of this system is a primary factor in the "fixed-cost profile" that rating agencies often cite as a credit weakness for the state's political subdivisions.15

### **Investment Returns and Actuarial Health**

For the fiscal year ended June 30, 2024, NVPERS reported an exceptional 11.94% net of fee return, bringing the system's total market value to $64.1 billion.22 This performance significantly exceeded the system’s long-term investment return assumption of 7.25%.22 Over the past decade, NVPERS has consistently ranked in the top percentiles of large public pension funds, a feat attributed to its simple, low-cost investment approach.22

Despite these strong returns, the system’s funded ratio remains in the mid-70% range, reflecting the lingering impact of past market volatility and the "ratcheting" effect of contribution rates. In 2022, a negative return of 5.1% resulted in a material increase in net pension liability across the state's municipalities.23 Consequently, credit analysts expect that employer contribution rates will rise in the medium term to ensure the system remains on its path to full funding.23 For Clark County and its component units, these pension and OPEB costs are managed through a combination of pay-as-you-go financing and irrevocable trusts, which totaled approximately $241.1 million in 2023\.23

## **Commercial Banking Exposure to Nevada Municipal Debt**

Regional banks operating in Nevada maintain significant portfolios of municipal securities, which serve as a critical source of tax-exempt income and high-quality liquid assets (HQLA). However, the 2023 banking crisis has fundamentally altered how these institutions classify and manage these holdings, with a heightened focus on interest rate risk and duration.

### **Zions Bancorporation and Nevada State Bank**

Zions Bancorporation, which operates Nevada State Bank, has a deeply entrenched presence in the state’s municipal market. As of December 31, 2024, Zions reported a total investment securities portfolio of $18.76 billion, with $9.1 billion classified as Available-for-Sale (AFS) and $9.67 billion as Held-to-Maturity (HTM).24 Within the broader Zions footprint, the bank serves thousands of local government entities, providing not just securities investment but also essential treasury management services.25

The bank's strategy in 2024 was defined by a deliberate reduction in the size of its investment portfolio—by nearly $2 billion or 9.2%—as it sought to maintain a shorter duration to mitigate unrealized losses in a high-rate environment.25 This "duration management" is a critical response to the regulatory scrutiny of HTM portfolios, which can trap banks in low-yielding assets during inflationary cycles. Zions' net interest margin (NIM) increased to 3.05% in late 2024, partly due to the downward repricing of interest-bearing liabilities faster than the yield on its earning assets, which include its municipal holdings.26

### **Western Alliance Bancorporation (WAL) and Bank of Nevada**

Western Alliance Bancorporation, the parent company of Bank of Nevada and First Independent Bank, has also prioritized liquidity fortification. In 2024, WAL grew its deposits by $11 billion, significantly outpacing loan growth and allowing the bank to deploy excess liquidity into HQLA.27 By year-end 2024, WAL’s CET1 capital ratio stood at a robust 11.3%, with $12.6 billion in unencumbered liquid securities.28

## **Stress Modeling: Credit Sensitivity and Downgrade Triggers**

While the current credit environment in Nevada is stable, it remains subject to specific "trigger events" that could lead to negative rating actions. These stressors are primarily environmental, demographic, and economic.

### **The Water Crisis and "Dead Pool" Scenarios**

The most acute long-term risk to Nevada municipal credit is the escalation of the Colorado River crisis. A "dead pool" scenario at Lake Mead—where water levels drop below 895 feet—would not only jeopardize the SNWA’s ability to deliver water but would fundamentally damage the tax base of Clark County.8 S\&P Global Ratings notes that prolonged drought conditions, coupled with extreme heat, could make the region "undesirable to visit," leading to a collapse in tourism revenue and a subsequent decline in property valuations.8

While the SNWA’s low-level intake (Intake No. 3\) provides significant physical protection, the economic perception of water scarcity could be enough to trigger a downgrade. A transition from "Stable" to "Negative" outlooks for southern issuers would likely occur if the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation’s 24-month study consistently forecasts Lake Mead elevations below 1,020 feet, which would trigger more severe mandatory shortages for Nevada.19

### **Tourism Revenue Decline and Sales Tax Sensitivity**

Nevada’s heavy reliance on the leisure and hospitality sector makes its credit sensitive to national economic downturns. A stress scenario involving a 10% or greater decline in gaming revenue or hotel occupancy would immediately impact the state’s General Fund and the City of Reno’s "Room Tax" revenue bonds.31

In a 'AAA' stress scenario analysis, S\&P evaluated how sensitive certificates of participation and room tax bonds are to a downgrade based on declining Net Cash Flow (NCF).32 For Reno’s senior-lien room tax bonds, which were recently raised to 'BBB', the rating is highly dependent on sustaining coverage levels through downturns.31 A sustained period of budgetary imbalance, or a material drawdown of the state's $1.3 billion in available reserves to cover operating shortfalls, would be the primary trigger for a sovereign-level rating action.1

### **Property Tax and 2008-Style Risks**

A 2008-style property tax collapse remains a low-probability but high-impact risk. However, the institutional framework in Nevada has been modified to mitigate this. Specifically, the state's statutory combined overlapping levy limit of $3.64 per $100 of AV provides a "hard ceiling" that protects against the predatory over-leveraging of property.1 Furthermore, the fact that 57% of state debt is retired within five years ensures that even in a severe downturn, the state can "outlive" the crisis by allowing current debt to roll off without the need for massive new issuances.4

## **Conclusion and Strategic Market Outlook**

The Nevada municipal credit market in 2026 is defined by a successful transition from a growth-oriented, high-risk profile to a mature, institutionalized framework. The state’s AA+ rating is well-supported by a rapid debt amortization schedule and a legislative commitment to maintaining high reserve levels. While Southern Nevada remains vulnerable to the existential threat of Colorado River depletion, the proactive management by the SNWA and the aggressive economic diversification in cities like Henderson and North Las Vegas have created multiple layers of credit protection.

For regional banks, Nevada municipal bonds remain a high-conviction asset class, providing tax-efficient yields and regulatory liquidity. However, the lessons of the regional banking crisis have necessitated a more active approach to duration management. Banks like Zions and Western Alliance are likely to maintain their concentration in Nevada credits but will do so with a preference for shorter-dated instruments or those with floating-rate components to hedge against continued inflationary pressures.

Ultimately, Nevada’s credit narrative is one of managed risk. The high correlation between its local economy and national consumer discretionary spending will always prevent a "risk-free" designation. However, the state’s institutional strength, coupled with its ability to generate significant surpluses during economic expansions, ensures that it remains a premier destination for municipal capital, provided the environmental risks of the desert Southwest are managed with the same rigor as its fiscal balance sheets.
