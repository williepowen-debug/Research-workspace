# RQ-CREED-012: Insurance as CRE Amplifier

## HEADLINE

Commercial property insurance has emerged as a critical CRE distress amplifier in 2025-2026. Multifamily insurance costs surged 119% since pre-pandemic levels (from $30 to $65 per unit monthly), while insurance expenses as a percentage of gross rental income doubled from historical 1-2% to 1.6-3.3% by 2023. With $930B+ in CRE loans maturing in 2026 and insurance-driven NOI compression weakening DSCR ratios, refinancing pressure is intensifying across climate-exposed markets—particularly Florida, California, Texas, and Gulf Coast properties—creating a perfect storm for loan defaults and asset devaluations.

## 2. IMPACT ON NOI AND DSCR — MARGIN COMPRESSION QUANTIFIED

### NOI Compression Effects

**Insurance premiums have transformed from a predictable expense into a volatile factor that can dramatically alter returns, valuations, and investor sentiment.**

- When insurance premiums rise, they directly reduce NOI—a critical metric driving property valuations and investor returns
- Even modest increases can cause cap rate compression and lower asset values
- Before pandemic: Insurance typically **1-2% of gross rental income**
- By 2023: Insurance reached **1.6-3.3% of gross rental income**
- **This represents a 100-165% increase in insurance as % of operating costs**

For multifamily specifically:
- Rental incomes need to increase by an additional **1.3%** to offset insurance premium increases
- Insurance premiums increased from **8% of total operating costs historically to 17%** currently
- This reflects rising natural disaster risks, higher property replacement costs, and tightening insurance market conditions

### DSCR Impact and Lending Requirements

**Current DSCR Requirements (2025-2026):**

| Property Type/Risk Profile | Minimum DSCR Required |
|---------------------------|----------------------|
| Standard CRE | 1.20x - 1.25x |
| Riskier properties (office, retail, value-add) | 1.30x or higher |
| Properties with uncertainty (market softness, tenant rollover) | Elevated DSCR + reduced LTV |

**DSCR Loan Interest Rates (Dec 2025):** 6.25% - 8.00%

**Margin Compression Mechanics:**

1. Higher insurance costs directly reduce NOI
2. Lower NOI weakens DSCR ratios
3. Weakened DSCR makes refinancing more challenging in tighter credit environment
4. A lower DSCR forces lenders to shrink LTV to maintain margin of safety
5. Properties with high insurance premiums become less attractive vs. comparable properties with lower operating expenses
6. For leveraged properties, this creates a potential death spiral limiting exit strategies

### Market Context for Refinancing Pressure

- Prime rate settled at **6.75%** as of December 2025 (down from peaks above 8% in 2023-2024)
- Average rate on CRE loans in 2025: **6.24%**, up from **4.76%** on maturing debt
- Properties in disaster-prone regions with older infrastructure face lender hesitation
- Combination of rising insurance costs, elevated DSCR requirements, and tighter lending standards creates significant margin compression challenges

## CREED IMPLICATIONS: NUMBERED FINDINGS RELEVANT TO CRE DISTRESS THESIS

### 1. Insurance-Driven NOI Compression Creates Hidden DSCR Deterioration

Insurance expenses doubled from 1-2% to 1.6-3.3% of gross rental income, representing a 100-165% increase in operating cost burden. For multifamily, insurance now represents 17% of total OpEx vs. 8% historically. This directly compresses NOI and weakens DSCR ratios at precisely the moment when lenders require elevated DSCR (1.25x-1.30x minimum) to refinance. Properties that penciled at origination now fail debt service coverage tests.

**Distress Mechanism:** Legacy loans underwritten with 1-2% insurance assumptions now face 2-3x that cost, creating unmodeled NOI compression that breaks refinancing feasibility.

### 2. The $930B+ 2026 Maturity Wall Meets Rising Insurance Costs

Over $930 billion in CRE loans mature in 2026, with average refinancing rates at 6.24% vs. 4.76% on maturing debt. Borrowers face not only a 148 basis point interest rate increase but also dramatic insurance cost increases that further erode DSCR. First half of 2025 recorded nearly 150 CRE foreclosures—highest midyear total since 2014. Q3 2025 distressed CRE volume reached $126.6 billion, up 18% year-over-year.

**Distress Mechanism:** Dual pressure of higher interest rates + insurance cost inflation creates refinancing impossibility for leveraged properties, forcing defaults and foreclosures.

### 3. Geographic Concentration of Insurance Stress in High-Value Markets

Florida, California, Texas, and Gulf Coast—representing massive concentrations of CRE value—face the most severe insurance pressure. Florida saw some carriers exit entirely; California premiums rose 15-40% in 2025; Texas and Louisiana saw 24-34% premium increases (2021-2024). These are not peripheral markets but core CRE investment zones.

**Distress Mechanism:** Highest-value CRE markets face greatest insurance cost inflation and carrier exit risk, concentrating distress in markets with largest loan volumes and most systemic importance.

### 4. Multifamily Particularly Vulnerable Despite "Safest" Asset Class Perception

Multifamily insurance costs surged 119% from pre-pandemic levels ($30 to $65 per unit monthly), with 45% annual increase in 2024 alone. High-risk markets like Fort Lauderdale hit $1,430/unit annually (+53%). Multifamily has been viewed as the defensive CRE play, but insurance cost inflation is uniquely severe for this sector, requiring rental income increases of 1.3% just to offset insurance—difficult in rent-growth-constrained environment.

**Distress Mechanism:** The "safe haven" CRE asset class faces disproportionate insurance cost pressure, potentially destabilizing the sector considered most resilient to distress.

### 5. Carrier Exits Create Uninsurability Risk—Not Just Cost Risk

It's not merely that insurance is expensive; in some markets and for some property types, it's becoming unavailable. Several national carriers pulled out of Florida commercial market; carriers fleeing California; reciprocal exchanges (lower-quality alternative insurers) formed at accelerating pace (36 since 2017, half since 2024). Many properties risk being underinsured due to outdated assessments.

**Distress Mechanism:** Uninsurability creates binary financing failure—lenders cannot finance uninsured or underinsured properties, forcing immediate distress regardless of cash flow or property performance.

### 6. Citizens (FL State Insurer) Depopulation Exposes CRE to Private Market Repricing

Citizens successfully reduced exposure by 73% (from 1.4M to 385K policies projected by end 2025), with 1,605 commercial policies recently removed and 600 more exiting. While depopulation is good for Citizens' solvency, it exposes CRE owners to private market pricing, with Citizens planning 10.4% average increase for commercial lines in late 2026 because current rates are "below actuarially sound levels."

**Distress Mechanism:** CRE owners on Citizens face dual risk—forced migration to higher-cost private insurance OR rate increases from Citizens to actuarially sound levels. Both outcomes increase distress.

### 7. Credit Rating Agencies Flag Insurance as Systemic CRE Credit Risk

Moody's reports that Real Estate Development and REITs saw "triple-digit year-over-year increases in credit risk" as of Q2 2025, with high-yield default rates projected to rise from 3.2% (2025) to above 4% (Q1 2026). Moody's explicitly notes that insurance, where available, is "the most important direct mitigant" for physical climate risk—implying that where insurance is unavailable or unaffordable, real estate credit risk skyrockets. S&P Global identifies Real Estate sector as among most exposed to high financial risk from climate change.

**Distress Mechanism:** Rating agencies pricing in insurance as systemic risk factor, leading to downgrades, higher financing costs, and reduced investor appetite for climate-exposed CRE.

### 8. Climate Repricing is Accelerating, Not Stabilizing

Despite "softening" rhetoric for 2025-2026, underlying drivers accelerate: global insured cat losses reached $137B in 2024 (40% above 10-year average) and projected $145B in 2025. U.S. nat-cat losses at $117B in 2024, exceeding 10-year average by 52%. California now allows forward-looking catastrophe models instead of historical data—this means recent "stabilization" is temporary and will reverse as insurers price in future climate scenarios, not past losses.

**Distress Mechanism:** Any current insurance cost stabilization is a temporary reprieve before forward-looking climate models drive next wave of increases, creating rolling distress cycles.

### 9. Self-Insurance/Captive Formation Signals Wealthiest Owners Exiting Traditional Market

Captive insurance adoption accelerating among CRE owners with large portfolios. Parametric insurance market growing 9.7% CAGR, reaching $20.59B in 2026. This signals that owners with capital and sophistication are abandoning traditional insurance market. This leaves traditional market with adverse selection—only properties that cannot self-insure remain, concentrating risk and driving further premium increases for remaining participants.

**Distress Mechanism:** Flight of best risks to captives and parametric structures concentrates poor risks in traditional market, driving further premium increases in death spiral that makes traditional financing impossible for lower-quality properties.

### 10. Adaptation ROI is Strong ($3.55 return per $1 spent) But Requires Upfront Capital Distressed Properties Lack

S&P Global study shows flood mitigation returns $3.55 for every $1 spent, but distressed CRE owners facing NOI compression and refinancing pressure have no capital for adaptation measures. This creates bifurcation: well-capitalized owners can invest in mitigation to reduce insurance costs; distressed owners cannot, ensuring their costs remain high and distress deepens.

**Distress Mechanism:** Climate adaptation has positive ROI but requires capital that distressed properties cannot access, creating self-reinforcing cycle where distressed properties cannot mitigate their way out of distress.

### 11. Insurance Now Larger Share of Debt Service Burden Than Modeled at Origination

With insurance doubling as percentage of OpEx and interest rates up 148bps on average, the "debt service" concept must now include insurance as a quasi-debt obligation—it's mandatory for financing and has similar payment priority to debt. Legacy DSCR calculations that modeled 1-2% insurance now face 2-3% insurance, meaning effective debt service is higher than modeled, systematically overstating coverage ratios across the CRE lending universe.

**Distress Mechanism:** Systematic understatement of true debt service burden across CRE universe creates hidden insolvency—properties appear to have adequate coverage but actually do not when insurance is properly accounted for.

### 12. 2025 Hurricane-Free Year Creates False Sense of Stability Before 2026+ Reversion

The insurance market "stabilization" narrative for 2025-2026 is heavily influenced by 2025 being the first year in a decade without a U.S. hurricane strike. This is statistical anomaly, not new normal. Gulf Coast faces 70% chance of named storm within 50 miles of Texas coast in coming seasons—well above historical average. When hurricane season normalizes or worsens, insurance market will reharden violently, catching overleveraged owners in false stability trap.

**Distress Mechanism:** Current insurance cost stabilization is hurricane-dependent anomaly that creates false confidence, leading to overleveraging and insufficient reserves before inevitable return to elevated hurricane activity triggers next distress wave.
