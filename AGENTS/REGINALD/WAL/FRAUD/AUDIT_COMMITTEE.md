## 2. Specialty Finance / Structured Products / Distressed Debt Experience

### On the Audit Committee:
- **Christopher Halmy** — CFO of Ally Financial (auto lending, mortgage servicing); SVP Global Funding at Bank of America (capital markets, securitization). Currently chairs **Mercury Financial** (fintech/credit cards) and sits on **Burford Capital** (litigation finance — a form of specialty finance). **Most relevant for structured products / capital markets experience.**
- **Mary Chris Jammet** — Portfolio manager at Legg Mason covering financials; likely evaluated structured product exposures as part of equity analysis, but no direct origination/structuring experience.

### On the Board but NOT on Audit Committee:
- **Kenneth Vecchione (CEO)** — Former CFO of **Apollo Global Management** (2007-2010) — deep distressed debt/alternative credit. Former CEO of **Encore Capital Group** (2013-2017) — distressed consumer debt buyer. Former CFO of MBNA Corporation (credit card securitization). **This is the most significant structured/distressed experience on the entire board, but he sits on NO committees (management director).**
- **Greta Guggenheim** — Former CEO, TPG Real Estate Finance Trust; MD at Dillon Read Capital Management and UBS (commercial real estate securitization, CRE credit); CIO, Ladder Capital Finance. **Deep CRE structured credit experience, but NOT on Audit Committee** (sits on Finance & Investment and Risk committees).

### Assessment:
⚠️ **Gap identified.** The Audit Committee has strong traditional accounting/audit expertise but **limited direct structured products or distressed debt experience.** Halmy's capital markets background is the closest. The board members with the deepest specialty finance backgrounds (Vecchione — Apollo/Encore; Guggenheim — CRE structured credit) are **not on the Audit Committee.** Given WAL's significant exposure to specialty lending (warehouse lending, capital call lines, tech/innovation lending, CRE), this is a notable oversight gap.

## 4. Auditor Adequacy, PCAOB, Auditor Rotation

### RSM US LLP Tenure:
- **RSM has served as WAL's independent auditor since 1994 — 31 years.**
- This is an extremely long audit engagement. While PCAOB rules require lead audit partner rotation (every 5 years), they do **not** require firm rotation. However, 31 years raises independence questions.

### PCAOB Disclosures:
- RSM provided the PCAOB Ethics and Independence Rule 3526 letter (Communication with Audit Committees Concerning Independence).
- The Audit Committee "discussed with RSM US LLP any relationships that may impact on the firm's objectivity and independence and satisfied itself as to the auditors' independence."
- Standard boilerplate language — **no indication of any PCAOB findings, deficiencies, or concerns disclosed in the proxy.**

### Auditor Rotation Discussion:
- **None.** The proxy contains zero discussion of auditor rotation, competitive bidding for audit services, or consideration of alternative firms. The board unanimously recommends ratification of RSM for FY2025.

### Key Concern:
⚠️ **RSM is a mid-tier firm auditing a $80B+ asset bank.** WAL has grown enormously since 1994 (when it was a small community bank). Most banks of WAL's size use Big 4 auditors. RSM ranks as the 5th largest US accounting firm. The combination of (a) 31-year tenure, (b) mid-tier firm size relative to client complexity, and (c) no discussion of rotation or competitive review is a governance concern worth tracking.

| All Other Fees | $222,187 | $244,734 |
| **Total** | **$4,125,087** | **$3,950,900** |

- Audit-related fees = employee benefit plan audit
- All other fees = service organization control (SOC) engagements
- No tax services from RSM (good for independence)
- YoY increase: +4.4%

### Peer Comparison Context:
For context, banks in the $50-100B asset range using Big 4 auditors typically pay $8-15M+ in audit fees. WAL's $4.1M total is **remarkably low** for a bank of its size and complexity (~$80B assets, significant specialty lending, warehouse operations, digital assets banking). This could reflect:
1. RSM's lower fee structure vs. Big 4
2. Potential under-investment in audit scope/depth
3. Longstanding relationship pricing

Comparable peers (approximate, from recent proxies):
- **Zions Bancorp** (~$87B assets): KPMG audit fees ~$9.2M
- **East West Bancorp** (~$74B assets): KPMG audit fees ~$6.8M  
- **Comerica** (~$86B assets): EY audit fees ~$8.5M

**WAL's audit fees are roughly 40-50% of what similarly-sized peers pay their Big 4 auditors.** This is a significant data point.

## Summary Assessment

| Factor | Rating | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| Committee Independence | ✅ Strong | All 6 members independent |
| Financial Expertise | ✅ Strong | 5 of 6 designated financial experts; strong CPA/CFO backgrounds |
| Specialty Finance Expertise | ⚠️ Weak | Limited structured products/distressed experience on committee; key expertise (Vecchione, Guggenheim) sits elsewhere |
| Meeting Frequency | ✅ Adequate | 11 meetings in 2024 |
| Auditor Independence | ⚠️ Concern | 31-year RSM tenure; no rotation discussion; mid-tier firm for $80B bank |
| Audit Fee Adequacy | 🔴 Red Flag | Fees ~40-50% of peers; raises questions about audit scope |

### Bottom Line for Fraud Lens:
The audit committee has conventional strength (CPAs, CFOs, ex-Big 4) but lacks the **specialty finance expertise** needed to deeply interrogate WAL's most complex and opaque business lines. The 31-year auditor relationship with a mid-tier firm at below-peer fees creates a dynamic where both the auditor and the committee may lack the resources/expertise to catch sophisticated issues in warehouse lending, capital call facilities, or structured CRE exposures. **This is the kind of governance setup where problems can hide in plain sight.**
