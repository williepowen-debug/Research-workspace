## Observed booking versus assessment

[Reuters August7](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/india-s-reliance-books-supertanker-at-record-freight-price-to-lift-iraqi-crude-ce7f50d2dc80f321) reported, using three shipping sources, a Reliance booking with Sinokor for two million barrels of Iraqi crude at WS1200. Brokers calculated charter hire of $23–25m versus approximately $2m before the war. Neither company commented. WALTER arithmetic: $11.50–12.50/bbl versus about $1/bbl; historical example, not an October quote. Reuters said Iraqi crude discounts made the cargo attractive despite freight. Contract, completed voyage and exact cost inclusions were not obtained.

TCE means time-charter-equivalent: voyage freight receipts less modeled voyage expenses, divided by modeled round-trip days. It is not charter cash due per day, a transit toll, realised fleet earnings, or net profit. The direct-Hormuz assessment has a known liquidity/professional-judgment caveat in HAWK/RED's September11–14 work. Publishing a number does not prove an executable quote or a booked Ras Tanura cargo. Do not multiply headline TCE by crossing days to invent a voyage bill. A complete delivered-cost estimate requires the actual charter terms, applicable insurance, fuel, waiting and transfer expenses, with inclusions checked to avoid double-counting.

## Why movement and high costs coexist

[Gibson September18](https://www.gibsons.co.uk/report/vlccs-rewrite-the-record-books/) describes Gulf shuttles feeding ship-to-ship transfers outside Hormuz, increasing ships required per barrel; diversions and unavailable/damaged hulls further reduce effective supply. This is the mechanism evidence, not a newly measured causal decomposition. Oman loading avoids that ship's Hormuz leg but can remain expensive because tankers are tied up and the upstream shuttle/transfer still has costs. The TD3C–TD34 difference is not a pure insurance premium: route length, logistics, vessel assumptions and assessment conditions differ.
