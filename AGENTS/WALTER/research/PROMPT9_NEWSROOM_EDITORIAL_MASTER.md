# Editorial triage: how newsrooms decide what matters

**Newsrooms have developed rigorous, battle-tested systems for routing information under extreme time pressure — and these systems offer a blueprint for any information triage operation.** The core architecture rests on tiered urgency codes, structured budget meetings that force zero-sum tradeoffs, news value scoring frameworks, and a disciplined craft of compression that distills complex events into actionable summaries. These aren't theoretical constructs. They are operational systems refined over 175 years of wire service practice, stress-tested daily against the chaos of simultaneous breaking events, finite display space, and the constant tension between speed and accuracy. The principles transfer directly to any system that must ingest a firehose of information, prioritize it, summarize it, route it to the right audience, and communicate confidence levels — while avoiding the cognitive biases that have produced journalism's most spectacular failures.

## The wire's urgency tiers create a shared vocabulary for priority

The Associated Press operates on a strict priority hierarchy that functions as an information routing protocol. At the top sits the **Flash** — reserved for events that will be among the top stories of the year. A Flash is a headline-length transmission, typically one sentence, that interrupts all other wire activity. In the teletype era, it triggered ten bells on the machine. AP Vice President for Standards John Daniszewski has described it as a code "encoded into the metadata" that "moves the information across the internet with the utmost priority, interrupting everything else that AP's computers might be doing." Flashes are extraordinarily rare. One veteran wire journalist who spent half a century at UPI and AP wrote exactly one in his entire career — LBJ's withdrawal from the 1968 presidential race. The JFK assassination and the September 11 attacks triggered Flashes.

Below the Flash sits the **Bulletin**, historically signaled by five bells, reserved for stories worth front-page consideration — a major Supreme Court ruling, death of a former president, significant military action. Bulletins run no more than one or two paragraphs, followed by a notation promising more. The **Urgent** tier handles noteworthy but smaller-scale news: a fixable crack discovered on Hoover Dam, a skyjacking, final scores of the World Series. An ordinary news day might generate five to twenty-four Urgents. The modern system adds the **APNewsAlert**, displayed in red for initial alerts and yellow for follow-ups, which serves as the visible successor to the older labels.

Reuters operates a parallel system built around the **Snap** (equivalent to a Flash/Bulletin) and a bell-alert hierarchy historically capped at five or seven bells depending on the era. For financial news, Reuters has deployed automated parsing tools like "FactGenie" that can halve the time to generate non-corporate alerts, with human reporters verifying numbers and selecting leads within 30 to 180 seconds of a data release.

The critical design insight is the **breaking news sequence** — a progressive disclosure protocol. When a major event occurs, the wire transmits: (1) a one-line alert, (2) a short usable story within minutes, (3) updated leads as developments warrant, (4) a comprehensive "writethru" combining all information with context. This mirrors agile delivery: ship the minimum viable information immediately, then iterate. Each transmission carries metadata encoding its priority tier, enabling automated routing by downstream systems without human intervention at every node.

When multiple breaking stories land simultaneously, wire editors rank them using criteria codified by the American Society of News Editors: Is the story **inherently important** (major public affairs impact)? Is it **intrinsically compelling** (touches a common chord)? Does it have **local interest** for the receiving audience? Does it illuminate a social trend or offer clear analysis of a complicated issue? David Manning White's landmark 1950 "Mr. Gates" study of a Peoria wire editor found that **90% of incoming wire copy was rejected** — the baseline rejection rate for a well-functioning triage system is not 50% but closer to nine in ten.

## The news budget forces zero-sum decisions on a finite surface

The "news budget" is not a financial document but a **structured inventory of available stories competing for limited space** — the editorial equivalent of a product backlog ranked against a fixed sprint capacity. The "news hole" — space remaining after advertising — historically consumed roughly **45% of a typical U.S. daily newspaper**. Every story added requires another subtracted. This constraint is the engine of editorial discipline.

The AP's budget system, called the **digest**, functions as a table of contents for the wire. It moves up to eight times daily on weekdays, starting at 3:00 AM Eastern. Each entry contains a **slug** (one-word identifier), a **budget line** (three to four sentences explaining what the story is and why it matters), and a **tag line** (author, length, available art). Twice daily, around 8:00 AM and 6:00 PM, the AP transmits a **Page One Advisory** — an explicit recommendation of which stories deserve prominent display, separate from the full inventory. This two-layer system — comprehensive inventory plus curated recommendation — is a powerful pattern for any information routing system.

Budget meetings follow a remarkably consistent structure across newsrooms. A French journalism training manual documents a representative flow: at 5:30 PM, each section drafts a preliminary budget for the following day. At 6:00 PM, a sit-down provisional meeting reviews all sections. The next morning at 9:30, standing section meetings finalize daily budgets. At 10:00 AM, the Editor-in-Chief chairs a **standing** operational meeting — participants stand because "the vertical position is the surest way to shorten meetings" — limited to twenty minutes and focused exclusively on two decisions: front page story selection and calibration (adjusting story lengths). The output is a finalized budget that serves as the day's operating plan.

The tradeoffs discussed in these meetings are explicit and recurring. **Local versus national**: community papers must balance proximity against broader significance. **Hard news versus features**: the most important stories are not always the most interesting. **Breaking versus enterprise**: breaking news consumes immediate resources while investigative work requires sustained investment. **Speed versus accuracy**: a tension so acute that AP fired a reporter in 2022 over a premature story about a missile strike in Poland. **Depth versus breadth**: do fewer stories well, or cover everything thinly. When space runs short, the hierarchy of cuts is also codified: wire copy gets trimmed before local content, feature stories get held for later publication, sidebars get cut before main stories, and stories below a certain urgency threshold move to inside pages or die.

## News values operate as a multi-factor scoring system

The question "what makes a story front-page worthy?" has been systematically studied since Johan Galtung and Mari Ruge published their foundational taxonomy in 1965. Their twelve news factors — frequency, threshold, unambiguity, meaningfulness, consonance, unexpectedness, continuity, composition, reference to elite nations, reference to elite people, personalization, and negativity — operate as a scoring system with three key properties. **Additivity**: the more factors a story satisfies, the higher its probability of selection. **Complementarity**: factors reinforce each other. **Exclusion**: stories low on all factors are filtered out entirely.

Tony Harcup and Deirdre O'Neill updated this taxonomy twice, most recently in 2017, adding factors reflecting the digital era: **exclusivity** (stories the outlet generated first), **shareability** (likelihood of social media propagation — described by one editor as "stuff that makes you laugh and stuff that makes you angry"), **audio-visuals** (arresting imagery), and the **news organization's own agenda**. Their revised list of fifteen factors provides a practical checklist for evaluating any piece of information's display priority.

In practice, editors evaluate stories on a two-axis matrix: **importance** (civic necessity, impact, consequence) against **interest** (audience appeal, engagement potential). The front page's sweet spot is high importance and high interest. But editors also apply the **composition** principle — Galtung and Ruge's often-overlooked eighth factor — selecting stories partly to balance the overall page. A heavy day of hard news may prompt editors to include a lighter feature specifically to vary the reader's experience. Ann Reisner's 1992 study of sixty-four recorded news conferences found that editors signal front-page worthiness by **elaborating and defending** their choices — the more an editor argues for a story, the stronger the signal. She also found that bureaucratic politics shape placement: section editors seek Page One slots to increase their division's prestige, producing a balancing pattern across desks over time.

Stories get **killed** for a specific set of reasons that clusters into two categories. Legitimate kills include insufficient sourcing, legal risk, changed circumstances that overtake the story, and simple quality failure — the story lacks focus or doesn't serve the audience. Illegitimate kills are more instructive: "catch and kill" arrangements (buying exclusive rights to suppress), owner or publisher pressure, advertiser pressure, and government lobbying. Rupert Murdoch reportedly used a three-part technique to suppress stories at the Sunday Times: question the facts, question the significance, then question the cultural context.

## Compression is a discipline with specific, teachable techniques

The craft of headline writing and budget line composition is journalism's purest expression of information compression — distilling complex events into a handful of words that convey what matters and why. The American Copy Editors Society codifies the process. First, read the entire story. Then identify the single point — if you struggle to name it, the story itself may lack focus. Jot down four or five key words, rank them by importance, and construct the headline around the most important words first.

The operating rules are precise. Use **present tense** for past events (creating urgency). Eliminate articles (a, an, the) and forms of "to be." Use numerals, not spelled-out numbers. Follow subject-verb-object structure. Use only names that are recognizable without explanation. The Defense Information School's checklist adds: show how this particular story differs from others, and use strong verbs but avoid commanding ones. The legendary **"barroom test"**, attributed to J. Montgomery Curtis of the American Press Institute, captures the quality standard: "Write the headline, then imagine swinging open a barroom door and shouting it. If a lot of heads turn, you probably have a good headline."

Several anti-patterns are equally codified. Never lift a phrase verbatim from the lead — the headline should add value. Avoid the "may" or "might" construction — if your best headline involves someone who "might" do something, the story probably lacks focus. Apply the **reverse meaning test**: if the opposite of your headline is more interesting, you're stating the obvious ("Minister professes belief in God" — the reverse is the actual news). Maintain a **single-thought discipline** — headlines with subordinate clauses indicate wandering focus. And always perform a final check for unintended double meanings. When the New York Times ran "TRUMP URGES UNITY VS. RACISM" after the 2019 mass shootings, executive editor Dean Baquet admitted the headline process had become "too disconnected" — senior leadership had gotten "casual about when we look at it."

Wire service **budget lines** serve a distinct function from headlines: they are three-to-four-sentence pitches that help downstream editors decide whether to use a story. The budget line must answer "so what?" — explaining not just what happened but why it matters to the receiving audience. Bloomberg's compression model is particularly rigorous: from stories of 1,000 to 2,500 words, Bloomberg editors distill a "very specific four-paragraph lead model with no sacrifice in news judgment, context, or perspective." The underlying cognitive technique across all compression work is consistent: **lead with impact, prioritize the new information, prefer concrete over abstract, and let the "so what" come first**.

## Verification protocols balance the cost of being wrong against being late

When conflicting reports arrive before a story is confirmed, newsrooms apply a graduated verification framework. The **two-source rule** — requiring at least two independent sources before publishing a major claim — is the most widely recognized standard, often attributed to Woodward and Bernstein's Watergate reporting. The rule has a pragmatic exception: a **"golden source"** — such as a police chief speaking about their own investigation — can satisfy the standard alone. At the Houston Chronicle, a senior editor must approve any unnamed source, a single unnamed source is "rarely enough," and one of a handful of top editors must sign off before publication.

The deeper framework, as articulated in the Verification Handbook, evaluates three dimensions: the **documentation** available, sources' **knowledge, reliability, and honesty**, and the **number, variety, and reliability** of sources. Craig Silverman's rules for the Columbia Journalism Review crystallize the operational heuristic: "Verification before dissemination" and, most critically, **"People will forget who got it first, but they remember who got it wrong."** This reflects a fundamental asymmetry: the reputational cost of error almost always exceeds the cost of delay.

Journalists communicate uncertainty through a **specific evidentiality spectrum** — a graduated vocabulary of confidence levels. "Confirmed" sits at the top: multiple credible independent sources verify the claim. "Sources tell us" indicates direct-knowledge sourcing. "We're getting reports" signals that multiple sources match but understanding is preliminary. "It appears" relies on observable evidence not yet confirmed. "Could not be independently confirmed" means the newsroom tried and failed to verify. "Unconfirmed reports suggest" carries the lowest confidence. The AP Stylebook instructs: "If a statement can't be confirmed, or can't be immediately confirmed, say so," and recommends labels like "developing story" or "details are still emerging." This graduated language serves a critical function — it lets information flow while explicitly encoding the system's confidence in its accuracy.

Wire service corrections follow their own protocol hierarchy. A standard correction appends a conspicuous note to the original piece. A **"kill"** — the most extreme action — instructs all subscribers to completely remove a story. Reuters' current policy for online corrections strikes through the incorrect portion while leaving the erroneous post visible as "a transparent record of what occurred." But as the Reuters/David Cay Johnston incident demonstrated, once transmitted, wire content propagates through the entire media ecosystem almost instantly, making full retraction effectively impossible.

## Story fatigue demands deliberate re-angling, not repetition

Two-thirds of American adults report feeling "worn out" by the volume of news, according to Pew Research Center surveys, and global interest in news has declined from **71% to 56%** between 2015 and 2024 per the Reuters Institute. Academic research identifies "issue fatigue" as a feedback loop: heavy coverage of a single topic creates fatigue, which generates negative evaluations of coverage, which deepens fatigue.

The primary editorial weapon against fatigue is the **second-day lede** — a technique for advancing continuing stories by finding genuinely new angles rather than repeating known facts. The Missouri Group's *News Reporting & Writing* textbook defines it as reporting "a new event in a continuing story." Modern practice, as Purdue OWL notes, demands more: since readers often learn breaking news via social media in real time, the print follow-up must "do more than merely regurgitate yesterday's news" — it must explain **why** and **how**, not just what.

Dmitry Shishkin's **User Needs Model** offers the most systematic framework for re-angling. Newsrooms default to "Update me" stories — here's what happened next — when they should diversify across seven distinct reader needs: **Educate me, Give me perspective, Inspire me, Connect me, Divert me, Help me, Keep me on trend.** When an editor has three versions of an "Update me" headline, Shishkin advises demanding a fourth, fifth, and sixth, "because those extra three ideas may be fresh takes, and not done by anyone already." He reports average read depth increasing month over month when editors apply this model. The Journalism Education Association's **Seven Paths** framework offers a complementary tool: any news event can generate stories framed as issue/trend, explanatory, profile, comparison, data analysis, process/how-to, or impact/consequences.

Historical precedent shows what works. During Vietnam, when body counts bored reporters, correspondents like Peter Arnett and Morley Safer shifted to atmosphere, irony, and emotion — producing some of the war's most powerful journalism. ABC's *Nightline* was born from nightly Iran hostage crisis updates that were often about the *lack* of progress — demonstrating that repetition itself can serve accountability when framed correctly. During COVID, the San Jose Mercury News deployed Q&A formats extensively; the Christian Science Monitor began labeling stories with driving values (freedom, equity, community) so readers could see **why** coverage mattered, not just what it contained.

## Famous failures reveal systematic biases in editorial judgment

Editorial judgment failures cluster around identifiable cognitive biases and structural pressures. Understanding these failure modes is essential for any system that aims to improve on human triage.

**Stories killed that shouldn't have been.** The New York Times held James Risen's NSA warrantless wiretapping story for **thirteen months** after White House officials — including Condoleezza Rice and CIA Director George Tenet — argued it would damage national security. The story was published in December 2005 only after Risen threatened to include it in his book *State of War*. The delay potentially affected the 2004 presidential election and caused Edward Snowden to take his NSA documents to The Guardian instead of the Times. NBC News killed Ronan Farrow's Weinstein investigation despite multiple on-camera accusers and a damning audio recording; producer Rich McHugh said the kill order came from "the very highest levels." Farrow took the story to The New Yorker, where editor David Remnick immediately recognized its strength. Harvey Weinstein reportedly exulted: "If I can get a network to kill a story, how hard can a newspaper be?"

**Stories that led but shouldn't have.** The Times' Iraq WMD coverage placed multiple front-page stories by Judith Miller presenting unverified claims as established fact, relying heavily on exile sources with vested interests in regime change. The paper's own 2004 editors' note admitted that editors "at several levels who should have been challenging reporters and pressing for more skepticism were perhaps too intent on rushing scoops into the paper." The Richard Jewell case saw the Atlanta Journal-Constitution's headline — "FBI suspects 'hero' guard may have planted bomb" — launch a media frenzy against an innocent man. A former FBI agent called it "confirmation bias on steroids." Jewell was cleared after 88 days; the actual bomber wasn't identified for years. The Chicago Tribune's "DEWEY DEFEATS TRUMAN" resulted from a confluence of flawed polling, a printers' strike forcing early press time, and editorial bias — the Tribune had called Truman a "nincompoop."

**The systematic pattern** across these failures involves five recurring biases. **Confirmation bias** dominates nearly every case — newsrooms sought evidence confirming their existing narrative. **Authority bias** led the Times to defer to White House arguments on the NSA story and NBC to yield to Weinstein's legal pressure. **Groupthink** produced convergent assessments (Dewey will win, Iraq has WMDs, Jewell fits the profile) that suppressed dissent. **Narrative gravity** warped judgment once a story fit a pre-existing frame — contradicting evidence was systematically downweighted. And **missing white woman syndrome**, coined by Gwen Ifill in 2004, demonstrates how newsroom demographic homogeneity produces systematic coverage bias: Black Americans constitute 31% of missing persons but their cases receive roughly one-tenth the coverage of comparable white cases.

The most important structural lesson is that **internal dissent was present in virtually every failure** — Risen fought for the NSA story, McHugh fought for the Weinstein story, James Goodale fought for the Pentagon Papers, skeptical reporters at the Times challenged WMD coverage. In each case, dissent was initially overruled by more senior decision-makers. Dean Baquet drew the explicit lesson: "I am much, much, much more skeptical of the government's entreaties not to publish today than I was ever before."

## Conclusion: transferable principles for information routing systems

Seven architectural principles emerge from newsroom practice that transfer directly to any curated information routing and display system.

**First, tiered urgency classification creates shared priority language.** The Flash/Bulletin/Urgent hierarchy gives every participant in the system — from field reporter to display editor — an identical vocabulary for priority. Encoding priority in metadata rather than labels enables automated routing without human intervention at every node.

**Second, progressive disclosure beats batch delivery.** The wire's alert-to-writethru sequence ships minimum viable information immediately, then iterates with increasing depth and context. This serves both speed-sensitive and depth-sensitive consumers from a single pipeline.

**Third, finite display surfaces are features, not bugs.** The news hole — fixed space remaining after constraints — is what forces disciplined prioritization. Without hard capacity limits, triage degrades into accumulation. The 90% rejection rate documented in the Mr. Gates study is the natural operating point of a well-functioning filter.

**Fourth, structured budget meetings with standing participants and time limits produce better decisions** than open-ended deliberation. The twenty-minute standing meeting focused exclusively on two questions (what gets prominent display, and how long should each item be) is a remarkably efficient decision protocol.

**Fifth, multi-factor scoring outperforms single-criterion ranking.** The Galtung-Ruge additivity principle — stories satisfying more news value factors score higher — and the importance/interest two-axis matrix provide robust frameworks for evaluating competing items. The composition principle adds portfolio-level thinking: individual item selection must account for the overall mix.

**Sixth, graduated confidence language is essential infrastructure.** The journalistic evidentiality spectrum — from "confirmed" through "sources say" to "unconfirmed reports suggest" — provides a model for communicating uncertainty without either suppressing information or presenting speculation as fact. Any system handling uncertain information needs this vocabulary built into its schema.

**Seventh, systematic debiasing requires structural safeguards, not just awareness.** The failure mode analysis shows that awareness of cognitive biases is insufficient — confirmation bias, authority bias, and groupthink operate even in organizations that explicitly teach against them. Effective countermeasures are structural: elevating internal dissent, requiring devil's advocate review for high-stakes decisions, enforcing demographic diversity among decision-makers, and conducting post-mortems that examine the decision process rather than just the outcome.
