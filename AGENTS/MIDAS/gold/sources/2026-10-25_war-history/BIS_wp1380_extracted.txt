Abstract

This paper analyses the state-dependent effects of geopolitical risks on gold prices (i.e. the
safe-haven role of gold), conditional on financial conditions. Geopolitical risks are often
associated with tighter financial conditions. However, recent data reveal a decoupling be-
tween such shocks and more restrictive financial conditions, raising the question of how gold
returns are affected by geopolitical shocks, conditional on the macro-financial environment.
Using monthly data from 1990 to 2026, we find that increases in geopolitical risks – both
geopolitical threats and realised events – increase gold returns, with stronger effects ob-
served during periods of loose financial conditions. We decompose this relationship into two
mechanisms: (i) the credit and liquidity risk channel and (ii) the opportunity cost channel.
Our findings indicate that the relationship operates most clearly through the first channel,
suggesting that the key factor is investors’ capacity to rebalance, while the evidence for the
second channel is less conclusive. Finally, we also find that the largest impact occurs when
periods of loose financial conditions are compounded with periods of geopolitical escala-
tions, as seen in 2024–26. These insights highlight the relevance of understanding gold as a
safe-haven asset amid complex macro-financial conditions.

Introduction

Gold occupies a distinctive position within the international financial system, combining excep-

tional market scale with unique institutional attributes and informational value, which confer

its status as a safe-haven asset during times of stress. Unlike most financial assets, it carries

no counterparty or issuer risk: as a stateless asset not backed by any government, it cannot

be frozen, sanctioned or devalued via redenomination – and it cannot be printed.1 Compared

with previous years, gold is now held primarily as a reserve and investment asset rather than

for industrial use, and its price largely reflects its safe-haven characteristics, making it a highly

sensitive barometer of systemic stress.2 Recently, gold prices rose to unprecedented levels, re-

gaining academic attention for their dynamics amid geopolitical fragmentation.

There is a well-documented empirical literature on gold’s safe-haven status with respect

to economic uncertainty, mostly focused on financial risks (Baur and McDermott, 2010; Beck-

mann, Berger, and Czudaj, 2015; Huynh, 2020), and risks related to policy uncertainty (Balcilar,

Gupta, and Pierdzioch, 2016; Raza et al., 2018; Bilgin et al., 2018; Beckmann, Berger, and Czu-

daj, 2019; Gozgor et al., 2019). However, there is limited analysis with respect to geopolitical

events and geopolitical threats, a growing literature facilitated by the construction of the geopo-

litical risk (GPR) index proposed by Caldara and Iacoviello (2022).

In addition to this, it is difficult to consider in isolation the link between the effect of

geopolitical risks on agents’ decisions to seek refuge in gold prices without accounting for the

macro-financial landscape, which may serve as a catalyst for this mechanism – in this case,

global financial conditions. Usually, geopolitical risks go hand in hand with tighter financial

conditions (Caldara and Iacoviello, 2022; IMF, 2025); however, one exception to this rule is

the recently observed period where there is a decoupling between geopolitical risks and tighter

financial conditions, which may potentially benefit gold’s safe-haven status. As observed in

Figure 1, the historical positive correlation between high geopolitical risks and tight financial

conditions (dots in blue) has reversed in recent years (dots in red).

Between 2024 and 2026, gold prices surged to unprecedented levels (Figure 2). Prices rose

by over 130% between January 2024 and April 2026, when gold peaked. Newspapers and finan-

cial experts attributed this surge to major geopolitical conflicts, including the ongoing Russian

invasion of Ukraine and escalating tensions in the Middle East, which boosted demand for gold

by some central banks and investors (ECB, 2025). However, such a sharp increase in gold prices

had not been seen during earlier spikes in geopolitical risks (Figure 2a).

spreads (proxy of credit and liquidity risk conditions), and the weakening of the US dollar by

around 11.5% against other currencies have loosened global financial conditions. In this par-

ticular case, it is unavoidable to question whether this global loosening of financial conditions

affected the safe-haven status of gold during major geopolitical events.

With this background, in this paper we study the state-dependent effects of geopolitical

shocks on gold price returns conditional on global financial conditions, a research question not

addressed before. Using an extended monthly sample spanning from 1990 to 2026 and perform-

ing several state-dependent econometric models, we find evidence of non-linear effects on the

safe-haven status of gold, conditional on global financial conditions. Specifically, we find that,

on average, a 1 standard deviation increase in threats (the subcomponent related to geopo-

litical threats) increases gold returns by 0.45% (at month = 0), while a 1 standard deviation

increase in GPR events (the subcomponent related to geopolitical realised events) increases gold

returns by 0.60% (at month = 4). During rising GPR risks (a regime defined by escalations in

geopolitical risks), baseline results rise to 1.00% and 0.80%, respectively. During loose financial

conditions, baseline results rise to 0.60% and 1.00%, respectively. When combining the regimes

The literature directly addressing this question is scarce, leaving a clear gap for contribu-

tion. Few works have analysed the particular implications of geopolitical risks (both threats

and geopolitical events) on gold price returns. Baur and Smales (2020) and Das, Kannadhasan,

and Bhowmik (2019) are the two direct works analysing the linear impact of geopolitical risks

on gold returns. Both find that the contemporaneous response of geopolitical threats positively

influences gold price returns, and find no effect from geopolitical events. We find these latter

results somewhat unconvincing, as major geopolitical events can indeed have the potential to

force agents either to seek safe-havens or to liquidate their gold reserves to provide additional

liquidity. We argue that these nuanced results may be a product of the contemporaneous anal-

ysis they performed and that a dynamic analysis with longer-horizon windows can provide a

better understanding of these patterns.5

Finally, regarding the gold price return responses conditional on the state of the macroecon-

omy, one single work stands out. Chen, Miao, and Tee (2023) evaluate the effects of economic

uncertainty shocks on gold prices in the zero lower bound (ZLB) regime, finding that the re-

All told, the contribution of this paper to the literature is mainly twofold. First, it builds on

Baur and Smales (2020) and Das, Kannadhasan, and Bhowmik (2019) and further extends their

contemporaneous analysis to a dynamic examination of geopolitical risk shocks on gold returns

– an approach needed to better understand the link between geopolitical risk and fluctuations

in gold prices. Second, it is the first work to analyse the non-linear effects of geopolitical risk

shocks, conditional on the macro-financial state of the economy (in this case, global financial

conditions) – a focus not explored before.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses the relationship

between this work and the literature. Section 3 outlines the empirical strategy implemented.

Section 4 describes the data. The main results are presented in Section 5, and an analysis of the

underlying financial mechanisms is provided in Section 6. A robustness analysis is presented in

Section 7. Finally, the last section presents conclusions.

2 Relation to the literature

In this first section, we situate our contribution within the three strands of literature most

relevant to our analysis: research on gold as a hedge and safe-haven asset, the financial conse-

quences of geopolitical risk, and the role of liquidity and macro-financial conditions in shaping

gold responses. We connect these strands by studying the dynamic response of gold returns to

geopolitical shocks and examining how this response varies with the prevailing geopolitical and

financial environment.

Most empirical research on gold belongs to the portfolio allocation literature and evaluates

whether gold is negatively or weakly correlated with other assets during periods of market

stress (Agyei-Ampomah, Gounopoulos, and Mazouz, 2014; Beckmann, Berger, and Czudaj,

2015; Baur and McDermott, 2010; Reboredo, 2013; Ciner, Gurdgiev, and Lucey, 2013). Much

of this literature relies on daily data and therefore concentrates on immediate flight-to-safety

behaviour. At the opposite end of the frequency spectrum, studies using annual or historical

data examine gold’s long-run performance over several decades or centuries (Erb and Harvey,

2013; Boubaker et al., 2020; Barro and Misra, 2016). Comparatively less is known about the

within-12-month response of gold prices to uncertainty shocks.6

Evidence from time-frequency methods nevertheless indicates that the horizon of analysis

matters. Bredin, Conlon, and Potì (2015), for example, find that gold can retain its safe-haven

properties for horizons of up to one year during financial crises. Related wavelet-based research

These methods provide useful evidence on horizon dependence, but they primarily examine

variation in comovement across frequencies rather than tracing the response to a particular

shock. Our analysis complements this literature by using monthly local projections to estimate

the response of gold returns to geopolitical shocks over a 12-month horizon.

A second strand of research examines the relationship between geopolitical risk and gold

prices (Annex Table 2). The Geopolitical Risk Index developed by Caldara and Iacoviello

(2022) distinguishes between geopolitical threats and realised geopolitical events and provides a

systematic measure of their evolution over time. Using this framework, Baur and Smales (2020)

and Das, Kannadhasan, and Bhowmik (2019) find that gold responds positively to geopoliti-

cal threats, while the response to realised events is generally not statistically different from zero.

These findings raise the question of whether realised geopolitical events have no effect on

gold or whether their effects simply emerge with a delay. Threats can be incorporated into

prices immediately because they alter investors’ expectations about future conflict. Realised

events, by contrast, may propagate gradually through revisions to the expected duration and

severity of the conflict, policy responses and international portfolio reallocation. By estimat-

ing horizon-specific responses, our analysis distinguishes an absence of contemporaneous effects

from an absence of effects altogether.

An additional empirical challenge is that geopolitical events frequently coincide with changes

in financial volatility and global risk appetite. Although the GPR index contains information

distinct from standard measures of financial uncertainty (Baur and Smales, 2020; Caldara and

Iacoviello, 2022), failing to account for their comovement could conflate the response to geopo-

litical developments with a broader flight-to-quality episode. In our baseline specification, we

therefore calculate the residual of each GPR component with respect to the VIX and use the

standardised residual as the geopolitical innovation.

A third strand of literature shows that gold’s safe-haven properties are not invariant across

economic and financial environments. The paper most closely related to our analysis is Chen,

Miao, and Tee (2023), who use a non-linear Interacted-VAR to examine whether gold prices

respond differently to uncertainty shocks during normal monetary-policy periods and episodes

in which the federal funds rate is constrained by the ZLB. They find that the response of

gold prices to VIX and EPU shocks is substantially stronger and more persistent at the ZLB.

Their counterfactual analysis further suggests that this state dependence is associated with the

monetary-policy regime rather than simply with the business cycle. These findings demonstrate

that the macro-financial environment can amplify gold’s response to uncertainty shocks. Our

analysis extends this insight by studying geopolitical rather than general uncertainty shocks

and by conditioning their effects on a broader measure of global financial conditions, rather

than exclusively on the monetary-policy rate.

The market turmoil at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic illustrates the complemen-

tary role of funding liquidity in gold’s safe-haven property. During the initial liquidity shortage,

investors reportedly liquidated profitable gold positions to raise cash and meet margin calls

elsewhere (Lee, 2020; Grosskopf, 2020). Consistent with this mechanism, Echaust and Just

(2022) and Cheema, Faff, and Szulczyk (2022) find that gold did not provide consistent protec-

tion against extreme downside risk during the pandemic shock. When funding constraints are

binding, investors may be unable to reallocate towards gold even when demand for safe-assets

is elevated.

State dependence also arises from portfolio characteristics affecting gold returns. Zulaica

(2020) shows that gold’s effectiveness as a hedge or safe-haven in central bank reserve portfolios

depends on factors such as portfolio duration and the volatility of the numeraire currency. More

generally, this evidence implies that safe-haven properties depend not only on the adverse shock

itself, but also on investors’ balance sheets, opportunity costs and the ability to adjust their

portfolios. We extend this insight by using global financial conditions as a state variable that

captures whether the broader environment facilitates or constrains reallocation towards gold

following geopolitical shocks.

This paper makes three key contributions to the existing literature. First, it extends the pre-

dominantly contemporaneous analysis of geopolitical risk and gold by examining the dynamic

responses of gold returns to both geopolitical threats and realised geopolitical events. Second, it

demonstrates that these responses are state-dependent, systematically varying with geopolitical

risk and global financial condition regimes. The central implication is that gold’s safe-haven

behaviour cannot be captured by a single unconditional coefficient; rather, it depends on both

the nature of the geopolitical shock and the macro-financial conditions under which investors

react. Third, this paper contributes to the literature by being the first to distinguish between

geopolitical risks and financial risks, effectively isolating flight-to-quality effects from safe-haven

effects during periods of geopolitical tension.

3 Empirical strategy

To address these objectives, our empirical strategy examines how gold returns respond to geopo-

litical risks and how that response varies with the macro-financial environment. We proceed in

three steps: we first construct a measure of geopolitical risks that is independent of financial

risks, we then estimate its dynamic effect on gold returns, and we finally allow that effect to

differ across macro-financial conditions.

In all specifications, causal inference is supported by the narrative short-run restriction

imposed on the link between geopolitical shocks and gold prices. Given the nature of the shocks

considered – geopolitical threats and geopolitical acts – it is implausible that monthly gold

returns could causally drive either type of geopolitical disturbance within the 12-month horizon

of our analysis, over the sample period (1990–2026). Moreover, the use of state-dependent

models introduces predetermined variation in the transmission mechanism, effectively treating

shifts across regimes as external to the baseline contemporaneous relationship. By exploiting

this variation, we capture non-linearities in the response of gold price returns to geopolitical

shocks while preserving the causal interpretation of our estimates.

4 Data

The sample consists of monthly data and covers January 1990 to March 2026. The choice of

the monthly frequency reflects the objective of the paper. Most studies of gold as a safe-haven

asset use daily data and focus on immediate market reactions. Monthly data allow us instead to

study whether the effects of geopolitical developments persist or emerge gradually over subse-

quent months, while remaining sufficiently frequent to capture considerable changes in financial

conditions and geopolitical risks.

The independent variables and controls are:

• Geopolitical risks: We measure geopolitical risks using the newspaper-based Geopoliti-
cal Risk Index developed by Caldara and Iacoviello (2022). They define geopolitical risks

as the threats, realisations, and escalation of adverse events associated with wars, terror-

ism, and any tensions among states and political actors that affect the peaceful course of

international relations. They construct the GPR index with an algorithm that computes

the share of articles mentioning adverse geopolitical events in leading newspapers pub-

lished in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada. We use the three available

series:

– the aggregate Geopolitical Risk Index (GP Rt);
– the Geopolitical Threats sub-index (GP RTt), which captures references to war threats,

peace threats, military buildup, nuclear threats, and terrorist threats; and

– the Geopolitical Acts sub-index (GP RAt), which captures realised events (beginning

of wars, escalation of wars, or terrorist acts).

10

• Financial conditions: The Goldman Sachs US Financial Conditions Index (GS US
FCI) is a composite indicator developed by Goldman Sachs Research to measure the
overall ease or tightness of financial conditions in the US economy – essentially capturing

how the current state of financial markets either supports or restrains economic growth

in that country. A higher reading signals tighter financial conditions – meaning credit is

more expensive, the dollar is stronger, and equities are weaker – which tends to restrain

economic activity, while a lower reading signals easier conditions that are more supportive

of growth (Goldman Sachs Research, 2017). Given its large historical sample, we will use

this series as a proxy for global financial conditions.

• Uncertainty indicators: The VIX Index, obtained from Bloomberg, measures option-
implied volatility in the US equity market, derived from a weighted average of S&P 500

option prices over a 30-day horizon. Even though the VIX is used in the construction of

the residualised GPR innovations to ensure that our geopolitical shocks are orthogonal to

risk-aversion shocks, it is useful to include this variable as a control. It captures investors’

forward-looking uncertainty about equity market conditions and serves as a broad proxy

for financial market stress.

• Macro-financial controls:

– The logarithm of the Nominal Broad US Dollar Index – a trade-weighted index of
the US dollar against a basket of major currencies – is included because gold is

globally priced in US dollars, creating a mechanical inverse relationship between

dollar strength and gold returns: appreciation of the dollar raises the effective cost

of gold for non-US investors, compressing demand. Controlling for the dollar index

therefore isolates movements in gold prices attributable to shifts in risk sentiment or

geopolitical uncertainty from those driven purely by currency valuation.

– The 10-year US Treasury yield captures the competition of other safe-haven assets.
As nominal yields rise – whether reflecting tighter monetary policy expectations,

improved growth prospects, higher fiscal concerns, or rising inflation compensation

– the relative attractiveness of gold could be affected.

– The logarithm of US industrial production (IP) proxies for the state of the global
business cycle and aggregate economic activity. Beyond its role as a financial asset,

gold retains significant industrial and jewelry demand, both of which are sensitive

to cyclical conditions; moreover, broad economic expansions tend to increase risk

appetite and reduce precautionary demand for safe-havens, while downturns can have

the opposite effect. Controlling for global IP therefore accounts for the demand-side

macroeconomic fundamentals that independently influence gold prices.

– The logarithm of the oil price is included as a joint control for commodity-market
co-movement and inflationary pressures. Gold and oil share exposure to common

commodity factors – including dollar denomination and global supply and demand

cycles – and oil price increases historically signal rising inflation expectations, which

in turn boost gold’s appeal as an inflation hedge. By conditioning on the oil price,

11

the estimates isolate safe-haven and geopolitical channels from broader commodity-

market dynamics and inflation shocks.

It is worth noting that, in all cases presented in Section 5 on results, where the dependent

variable is one of the subindices (GPR threats or events), the other subindex is included as a

control. This approach ensures a more robust identification of each subindex’s distinct effect

on gold prices.

5 Results

5.1 Baseline effects

Figure 3 shows the impact of GPR, GPR threat, and GPR event shocks on gold price returns,

generating overall positive statistical impacts but at different magnitudes and horizons. We find

that the dynamics of the overall impact of GPR on gold price returns is a combination of the

impacts of its two subcomponents: threats and events (first panel). First, there is a short-lived
response of GPR threats of 0.45% (second panel) at month h = 0, with 90% confidence, indi-
cating that the impact is contemporaneous and short-lived. Second, a medium-term response of

0.60% emerges from GPR event shocks (third panel), with 99% confidence, reflecting a longer
and lagged response from this subcomponent. At the contemporaneous horizon (h = 0), our
results are consistent with Baur and Smales (2020): threats have a significant effect on gold re-

turns, whereas geopolitical events do not. However, a lagged effect is observed from this second

factor.

These initial results suggest that, contrary to the existing literature, a dynamic analysis

is needed to better understand the differing effects of geopolitical risks on gold price returns.

While gold price returns respond swiftly to surges in geopolitical threats, they generally take a

few months on average to react to geopolitical events, potentially depending on the escalation

of these events.

The horizon at which each subcomponent operates requires explanations on why we ex-

pect realised events to act with a delay. Geopolitical threats are news about the distribution

of future conflict: they arrive as headlines and are incorporated into prices essentially on impact.

Realised events differ in two key respects. First, a substantial part of their content has typ-
ically been priced already at the threat stage – the invasion follows the mobilisation; the strike
follows the ultimatum – so what moves prices on the day of the event is only its unanticipated
component, which can be small even when the event itself is large. Second, the information that

events do carry is revealed progressively: markets learn about the expected duration, severity

and geographic containment of a conflict, whether the geopolitical event in question will trigger

chain disruptions, the sanctions and policy responses it prompts, and the reallocation of official

reserves it induces, over weeks and months rather than hours. Under this interpretation, the

12

coefficient at h = 0 for geopolitical event shocks on gold returns understates the total effect of
an event. The response is expected to build over the subsequent quarter, as observed in the

third panel of Figure 3, peaking four months after the shock.

6 Which financial channels affect the relationship?

In this section, we examine the mechanisms through which financial conditions influence the

relationship between geopolitical risks and gold prices, separating the relationship into the two

channels most commonly analysed in the literature: (i) the credit and liquidity risk channel,

and (ii) the opportunity cost channel. We analyse each in turn and find that our results operate

18

most clearly through the first channel, while the evidence for the second is less conclusive.

The two channels are:

1. Credit and liquidity risk channel: During periods of heightened market stress, such as
geopolitical shocks, credit and liquidity risks can significantly constrain investors’ ability

to allocate capital to safe-haven assets like gold. When credit risk is high – marked by

tighter credit conditions and elevated borrowing costs – or when liquidity risk is elevated –

characterised by margin calls and forced asset liquidations – investors are often compelled

to prioritise meeting short-term obligations rather than reallocating to gold as a safe

haven. For instance, margin calls during such periods can trigger asset sales to cover

collateral requirements (e.g., Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009), leaving little room for

portfolio adjustments towards gold. This dynamic was evident during the Covid-19 episode

(e.g., Akhtaruzzaman et al., 2021). The same logic applies on the credit side: widening

credit spreads are not merely a symptom of stress but a tightening of financial conditions

in their own right, operating through the risk-bearing capacity of the financial sector

(Gilchrist and Zakrajšek, 2012). When intermediary balance sheets are impaired, risk

premia rise across assets because constrained intermediaries cannot take positions, rather

than because fundamentals have changed (He and Krishnamurthy, 2013).

In contrast,

when credit and liquidity risks are low, agents face fewer balance sheet frictions and are

better positioned to reallocate to gold in response to geopolitical shocks.

• To capture these dynamics, we use as a state-dependent threshold the subcomponent
of the GS US FCI related to corporate credit spreads, which represents the part of
the broad GS US FCI measuring the share related to credit risks associated with

investment-grade US corporate spreads over equivalent-duration government bond

yields. In Figure 8a, we observe the link between gold prices and credit-liquidity risk

conditions, showing that gold prices tend to rise more significantly during geopolitical

shocks when credit and liquidity risks are low, as agents can easily move into gold

under such conditions.

• For robustness checks, we also use as a threshold in a separate exercise the US BBB
corporate spread, as a proxy for global credit and liquidity risks. Wider spreads sig-
nal elevated perceived risks, reflecting both tighter liquidity conditions and increased

credit concerns in the corporate sector. Conversely, narrower spreads indicate im-

proved credit availability and market liquidity, facilitating smoother reallocations to

gold.

2. Opportunity cost channel: Low interest rates can reduce the yield sacrifice of holding
gold relative to interest-bearing alternatives (e.g. Erb and Harvey, 2013; Chen, Miao, and

Tee, 2023). This reduction in opportunity cost makes gold a more attractive investment,

particularly during periods of heightened geopolitical uncertainty. However, it is impor-

tant to note that low interest rates can also introduce opposing effects in this channel.

While low rates reduce the opportunity cost of holding gold, they often coincide with

19

periods of economic weakness or recessions, reflecting the business cycle position. In such

scenarios, agents may face immediate financial pressures, such as the need to consume

or invest in productive or liquid assets to address short-term economic challenges. These

financial constraints can dampen the demand for gold as a safe-haven, as agents priori-

tise meeting immediate consumption needs or maintaining liquidity over reallocating to

gold. Thus, while the opportunity cost mechanism generally supports higher gold demand

during periods of low interest rates, the broader economic context may complicate this

relationship, introducing opposing forces that can limit the extent of gold’s safe-haven

appeal during geopolitical shocks, as stated in Chen, Miao, and Tee, 2023.

Testing the opportunity cost channel. The evidence on the second channel is less
clear-cut. In Figure 11, the response of gold returns to geopolitical shocks is larger in the high-

cumulative impact of geopolitical events peaks at 1.4% four months after the shock. In contrast,

in the low short-term interest rate regime, the impacts are not statistically distinguishable from

zero throughout. Figure 12 re-estimates the specification using alternative long-term interest

rates and yields the same results. These results suggest that the opportunity cost channel, with

thresholds measured in levels, is not an amplifier of the link between geopolitical shocks and

gold returns.

In this section, we present several robustness checks for this paper, addressing potential omitted

variable biases or mediation factors in the analysis. Specifically, we consider four factors that

could influence our baseline results and the paper’s motivation concerning the recent decoupling

observed in the data: the demand for gold by central banks, the trade momentum of gold prices

during major rally events, the US government yield convenience, and fiscal policy uncertainty

in the United States.

First, we examine the role of major participants in the gold market, with a particular empha-

sis on central banks. Figure 14a shows that the share of gold demand accounted for by central

banks rose over 2022–2024, while the investment share rose in 2025, and Figure 14b shows

total gold net purchases, which fell sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic—when economies

faced heightened liquidity constraints—and has recovered strongly since. ECB (2025) notes

that while gold prices reached historical highs in 2024, central bank gold reserves also rose to

their highest levels since the Bretton Woods era. If central banks and private investors were

8 Conclusion

This paper investigates the state-dependent effects of geopolitical risks on gold prices, condi-

tional on the scalation of GPR risks and the tightness of global financial conditions, providing

new insights into the dynamics of gold’s safe-haven properties. The findings indicate that geopo-

litical risks – encompassing both threats and realised events – significantly elevate gold returns,

with the effects being more pronounced during periods of loose financial conditions and rising

GPR risks. The largest impacts are observed when rising geopolitical risk indicators coincide

with loose financial conditions, a scenario that has become increasingly prominent in recent

years. Tests for non-linearities corroborate the asymmetric effects documented in this analysis.

We further ask why financial conditions shape the safe-haven response, separating the rela-
tionship into the two channels most often invoked in the literature: (i) the credit and liquidity

risk channel and (ii) the opportunity cost channel. Our results operate most clearly through

the first channel. When credit and liquidity risks are low, the response of gold returns to geopo-

litical shocks is large and significant; when they are high, it is statistically indistinguishable

from zero. This holds across alternative measures of the channel. The evidence on the second

channel is less conclusive and is sensitive to how the yield sacrifice is measured. We therefore

interpret our findings as suggesting that loose financial conditions amplify gold’s safe-haven role

primarily by relaxing the constraints that limit portfolio reallocation.

Finally, we perform a series of robustness checks to ensure that the link reported in this

paper is not spurious but rather robust to a set of major events potentially affecting gold prices

simultaneously with recent geopolitical shocks. We report that our results remain robust when

considering the role of central bank demand for gold purchases, trade momentum in the gold

market, the evolution of the US government bond convenience yield, and the uncertainty sur-

rounding US fiscal policy.

This study makes three key contributions to the literature. First, it advances prior research

by replacing a contemporaneous analysis with a dynamic and recursive framework, offering a

30

more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between geopolitical risks and gold price

fluctuations. Second, it is the first to examine the non-linear effects of geopolitical shocks on

gold returns, conditional on global financial conditions – a novel approach that addresses a

significant gap in existing research. Third, it distinguishes between geopolitical risks and finan-

cial risks, isolating flight-to-quality effects from safe-haven effects during periods of geopolitical

tension.

Looking ahead, future research could delve deeper into how geopolitical fragmentation and

financial innovation are reshaping the global financial system and influencing patterns in gold

prices. These areas of inquiry are essential for understanding the evolving role of gold as a

safe-haven asset in an increasingly complex and interconnected world.
