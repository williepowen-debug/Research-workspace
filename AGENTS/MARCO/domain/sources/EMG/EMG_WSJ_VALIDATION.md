## VERDICT

**PARTIALLY SUPPORTED — with a critical definitional mismatch.** The WSJ headline ("rates not seen since the 1930s") accurately captures one real phenomenon — U.S. net international migration turned negative in 2025 for the first time since approximately 1935 — but the comparison is structurally misleading for MARCO's purposes. The 1930s "emigration" was overwhelmingly a collapse of inbound immigration plus forced deportations/repatriations of foreign-born residents, not a surge of U.S.-citizen voluntary outmigration. In 2025 the same dynamic is at work: the Brookings-estimated -10K to -295K net migration is driven primarily by a ~55% collapse in inbound flows (from 6M peak in 2023 to ~2.65-2.75M in 2025), not by a documented explosion of Americans leaving. The hard count of voluntary American citizen emigration remains poorly measured, with the best available proxy (IRS covered-expatriate renunciations) at 4,889 for full-year 2025 — elevated but nowhere near the 1930s in absolute terms. The WSJ claim is real on the net-migration-sign metric, partially real on trend-direction for citizen outflows, and hyperbolic if read to imply a 1930s-magnitude surge of Americans voluntarily leaving.

## PART 1 — 1930s Emigration: What Actually Happened

### 1.1 Net Migration Turning Negative

The 1930s are one of only two periods in modern U.S. history (alongside the Civil War era) when net international migration turned negative. The last confirmed year of net negative migration before 2025 was approximately **1935**, based on Census Bureau data. The mechanism was not a surge of American citizens moving abroad; it was:

1. **Collapse of inbound immigration:** Legal immigration fell from 279,678 in 1929 to a low of ~23,068 in 1933. The decade average was ~6,900–69,938/year depending on accounting method (the lower figure covers only non-quota immigrants; the broader measure covers all legal arrivals). [Census Historical Statistics 1789–1945; Immigration to the United States – EH.net]
2. **Forced deportations:** Between 1930 and 1932, ~54,000 aliens were formally deported. Roosevelt reduced the annual deportation rate from ~20,000 in 1933 to under 9,000 by 1934. [Immigration to United States historical archives]
3. **Mexican repatriation campaigns:** Between 400,000 and 1,000,000 Mexicans and Mexican-Americans left the U.S. between 1929 and 1935 — the majority under coercion or "voluntary" pressure from local authorities, not federal deportation orders. Notably, approximately half were U.S.-born citizens. [INS Records for 1930s Mexican Repatriations, USCIS; Migration Policy Institute]
4. **European return migration:** Italian-Americans, Polish-Americans, and other Southern/Eastern European immigrants returned to home countries in significant but unquantified numbers, driven by economic collapse and the pull of New Deal-era U.S. quotas blocking re-entry if they left.

### 1.2 Magnitude: What Are the Actual Numbers?

Precise annual emigration counts for the 1930s are **not available in standard accessible data**. The INS tracked "emigrant aliens" (departing non-citizen residents), but those records are buried in National Archives microfilm. The Census Bureau's working papers confirm foreign-born population fell from 14.2 million (1930) to ~10.3 million (1950), a 3.9M decline over 20 years — attributable to combination of mortality (aging immigrant cohort), very low inbound immigration, and emigration. Separating these three factors precisely is not possible from public data.

**Best available estimates for 1930s net outmigration:**
- Annual negative net migration: Confirmed for at least 2–4 years in the early 1930s (1931–1933 most likely)
- Peak-year net outmigration: Probably on the order of 100,000–200,000 in the worst years (1932–1933), primarily driven by the repatriation campaigns and deportation machinery
- Decade net migration: Probably slightly negative overall for the 1930–1940 period, but this is a rough estimate — no Census source directly states a decadal net negative figure for international migration

**Methodology and data quality:** Emigration in the 1930s was measured via INS departure manifests for departing non-citizens. Counting of native-born Americans emigrating was essentially non-existent — there was no passport surrender requirement, no exit tracking, and no Form 8854 equivalent. This means the 1930s "emigration" figure is essentially a measure of foreign-born persons leaving, not U.S. citizens choosing to relocate. **This is the critical structural difference from what MARCO's EMG-01 vector is designed to track.**

## PART 2 — Current 2025–2026 Data

### 2.1 Net Migration: The Brookings/Census Estimate

**Brookings Institution (January 2026):** Net migration for 2025 estimated at **-10,000 to -295,000** — the first time in at least 50 years it has been negative. The primary driver is collapsed inbound flows (down ~55% from 2023 peak), not a documented surge in outflows. Outflows in their model include: removals (310,000–315,000), voluntary departures under enforcement pressure (210,000–405,000), and normal baseline emigration. [Brookings, "Macroeconomic Implications of Immigration Flows in 2025 and 2026," Jan 2026]

**Census Bureau (Jan 2026):** Net international migration for July 2024–June 2025 was 1.3 million — still positive but down from 2.7M peak. Census projects further decline to ~321,000 in 2026. Census uses ACS data, DHS repatriation records, Mexico's ENOE survey, and CPS monthly foreign-born population trends. [Census Bureau blog, "Historic Decline in Net International Migration," Jan 2026]

Key distinction: The Census 1.3M figure covers the 12-month period ending mid-2025. The Brookings negative range covers the full 2025 calendar year, capturing the late-2025 enforcement surge. The two are not contradictory — they measure different windows.

### 2.2 CBO Voluntary Emigration Estimate (~430K)

CBO's "Update to the Demographic Outlook, 2025 to 2055" (published September 2025; pub. 61390) is the source document. CBO estimates voluntary emigration for FY2025 at approximately **430,000** — a figure that represents 160,000 fewer people than CBO estimated for 2024. The reason: increased enforcement makes voluntary departure less likely because emigrants cannot reenter. **Critical note:** The CBO 430K figure covers voluntary emigration of the foreign-born population (primarily immigrants with uncertain status leaving to avoid enforcement), not U.S. citizens moving abroad. The document does not appear to disaggregate native-born American voluntary emigration as a separate line item. The CBO PDF is accessible at cbo.gov/publication/61735 but direct fetch was blocked; this interpretation is based on available search excerpts and CBO's stated methodology using TPS termination rates. [CBO pub. 61390, Sept 2025]

### 2.3 WSJ Article: What Was the Precise Claim?

**Headline:** "Americans Are Leaving the U.S. in Record Numbers"
**Publication date:** February 27, 2026
**Author:** Not confirmed via public search results (byline behind paywall)
**Primary data cited:**
- Brookings Institution negative net migration estimate (~-150,000 for 2025)
- WSJ proprietary analysis of 15 countries' 2025 residency/immigration data: at least **180,000 Americans** joined those countries in 2025 (described as a floor, likely higher when more countries report)
- Citizenship renunciation data (implied reference to 2024/2025 IRS Federal Register figures)
- Passport application volume at record highs

**The precise claim:** "Last year the U.S. experienced something that hasn't definitively occurred since the Great Depression: More people moved out than moved in." The reference is to **net migration sign** (negative vs. positive), not to a specific emigration rate or volume of citizen outflows. The headline's "Americans Are Leaving in Record Numbers" is the editorialized framing on top of the Brookings net-migration finding.

**Important:** The WSJ article conflates two separate phenomena: (a) net migration turning negative due primarily to enforcement-driven foreign-born departures and collapsed inflows, and (b) an increase in U.S. citizen voluntary emigration. Both are real, but they are different vectors with different magnitudes and different implications for MARCO.

### 2.4 Hard Counts: IRS Expatriation, UK/Ireland Data

**IRS Federal Register Quarterly Publication (covered expatriates):**

| Year | Total | Notes |
|------|-------|-------|
| 2021 | 2,426 | Post-COVID baseline |
| 2022 | 3,816 | |
| 2023 | 3,114 | |
| 2024 | 4,480 (+44% YoY) | Q3 2024 alone: 2,123 — highest single quarter since 2016 |
| 2025 | 4,889 | Q1: 1,285 (102% YoY jump); Q2: 1,057; Q3: 1,593; Q4: 954 |

[Wikipedia, "Quarterly Publication of Individuals Who Have Chosen to Expatriate"]

**Severe undercounting caveat:** The IRS list covers only "covered expatriates" — those with net worth >$2M or average annual tax liability >$206,000 (2025 threshold). State Dept estimates suggest actual renunciants run meaningfully higher; the 2025 list of 4,889 is a lower bound for wealthy renunciants, not a count of all Americans formally leaving. Total renunciants (including non-covered) are not publicly tracked with precision.

**UK Home Office:** Q4 2024 British citizenship applications from U.S. citizens up 40% YoY. Q1 2025 set records for American naturalizations (1,177) and registrations (754). [Connaught Law, citing Home Office data]

**Irish DFA:** MARCO workbook already records 4,700 Irish citizenship applications from U.S. residents in Q1 2025 (decade high).

**Destination-country residency flow (WSJ 15-country analysis):** At least 180,000 Americans joined 15 countries' residency registries in 2025. Primary destinations: Mexico (~800K U.S. citizens resident as of 2024, though this is a stock not flow figure), Portugal, Spain, France, Canada, Costa Rica.

**State Dept passport data:** FY2025 issued 27M+ passport books and cards — record high, driven partly by anticipatory travel and emigration intent. Useful as leading indicator, not as direct emigration count (most passport applicants are not emigrating).

## PART 3 — Rating the WSJ Claim

| Dimension | Finding | Rating |
|-----------|---------|--------|
| Net migration sign in 2025 | Confirmed negative by Brookings (-10K to -295K) and trending to Census-confirmed low | SUPPORTED |
| "First since 1930s/1935" historical anchor | Net migration last confirmed negative ~1935; claim is accurate on this narrow point | SUPPORTED |
| "Americans leaving in record numbers" (citizen outflows) | IRS renunciations at records (4,889 in 2025); WSJ found 180K+ in 15 countries; directionally true but baseline is ambiguous | PARTIALLY SUPPORTED |
| Implied comparison: same *mechanism* as 1930s | 1930s was foreign-born departures + collapsed inflows; 2025 is same mechanism — citizen outflow is secondary story | MISLEADING |
| Rate comparison to 1930s decade average | 1930s annual emigration probably 50K–200K driven by repatriation campaigns; 2025 voluntary citizen emigration likely in 200K–500K range if all destination countries reported — comparable or higher in absolute terms but very different composition | INDETERMINATE |
| "Not seen since the 1930s" applies to *voluntary citizen* emigration | No data exists for 1930s voluntary citizen emigration — that category was not measured | UNVERIFIABLE |

**Overall rating: PARTIALLY SUPPORTED (net-migration metric) / HYPERBOLE (voluntary citizen emigration rate)**

The WSJ is correct that net migration turned negative for the first time since ~1935. It is wrong by implication to suggest a 1930s-comparable exodus of American citizens, because: (a) 1930s "emigration" was not primarily citizen outflow, (b) 1930s citizen emigration was not measured, and (c) 2025 citizen emigration at 180K+ (15-country floor) may actually *exceed* any 1930s voluntary citizen emigration — but that's unknowable and the comparison is not meaningful.

## PART 4 — EMG-01 Threshold Calibration

### Current Thresholds (from VX.tsv)

| Band | Range |
|------|-------|
| NORMAL | <200K annual voluntary |
| ELEVATED | 200K–500K |
| CRITICAL | 500K–1M |
| BREACHED | >1M OR IRS Form 8854 >10K/yr |

### Assessment

**The thresholds are reasonably calibrated for the vector as designed** (voluntary citizen emigration), but need two clarifications:

**1. The CBO 430K figure is not a "voluntary citizen emigration" number.** It covers voluntary foreign-born departures. Actual voluntary U.S. citizen emigration is untracked. If MARCO intends EMG-01 to measure citizen outflows, the reference denominator must be specified clearly. The WSJ 15-country floor of 180K is the best current proxy for citizen flows; annualized that sits squarely in the ELEVATED band (200K–500K).

**2. The IRS Form 8854 threshold (>10K/yr for BREACHED) is miscalibrated.** The 4,889 figure for 2025 is already at record levels, yet only covers wealthy covered expatriates — a fraction of all renunciants. At current trend (102% YoY growth in Q1), annual totals could reach 6,000–8,000 within 12 months. The 10K threshold may fire sooner than expected if growth continues, but it's also a lagging and incomplete indicator. Suggest keeping it but adding a secondary trigger: **IRS covered-expatriate list >7,500/yr** as an intermediate warning level.

**3. Suggested threshold revisions:**

| Band | Current | Recommended | Rationale |
|------|---------|-------------|-----------|
| NORMAL | <200K | <150K | Corrects for the fact that pre-2025 baseline was probably 100K–150K citizen outflows annually |
| ELEVATED | 200K–500K | 150K–400K | WSJ 15-country floor of 180K already in range; centers the band more accurately |
| CRITICAL | 500K–1M | 400K–750K | If full-reporting destination countries show 400K+ citizens/year, structural migration shift is confirmed |
| BREACHED | >1M OR 8854 >10K | >750K OR 8854 >7,500 | Lower absolute trigger; 1M is probably unachievable given U.S. citizen population base and logistics |

Note: These revisions assume EMG-01 is scoped to **U.S. citizen voluntary emigration** specifically. If the vector includes foreign-born voluntary departures as tracked by CBO (430K in FY2025), all thresholds should be revised significantly upward and the ELEVATED band is already firing.

**Current confidence: 65% (PENDING/watch)**

**Recommendation: Hold at 65%, do not downgrade, flag for upgrade to 70% when:**
- WSJ 15-country analysis replicates with Q3/Q4 2025 destination country data showing >200K Americans
- IRS Q1-Q2 2026 Federal Register lists confirm continued >100% YoY growth trajectory
- A second major outlet independently validates the destination-country flow data

**Rationale for holding rather than upgrading now:** The directional signal is real and confirmed. The weakness is in the denominator — we cannot confidently state what "normal" voluntary citizen emigration looks like because the historical baseline is thin and the 1930s comparison is structurally inapt. Until MARCO has a clean 12-month trailing count of Americans registering in destination countries (not just the WSJ's 15-country partial), formalizing EMG-01 at higher confidence risks anchoring on a headline that conflates citizen outflow with net migration mechanics.

**The vector is real. The WSJ headline is partially real. The 1930s comparison is more rhetorical than analytical.**
