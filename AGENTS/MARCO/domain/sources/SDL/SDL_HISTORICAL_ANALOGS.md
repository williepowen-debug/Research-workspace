## SYNTHESIS — What Analogs Imply for SDL-01 (2.2M, 2025-2026)

**Transmission speed ranking from fastest to slowest:** Fear effects and remittance collapse arrive within 1-4 weeks of enforcement escalation — *before* physical departures fully register. Agricultural labor disruption hits within the first planting/harvest season (1-3 months lag from departure shock). Housing vacancy and rent softening in immigrant-heavy neighborhoods follows 3-9 months after population departure, concentrated in rental submarkets (owner-occupied housing shows minimal or negative effect). Food/produce CPI is the slowest confirmed channel: the 2010-2020 farm labor tightening produced an estimated +15% cumulative fruit and vegetable price rise over a decade, but the channel from enforcement shock to retail price is not instantaneous — it requires labor costs to propagate through farm gate prices, distribution, and retail markup, typically a 6-18 month lag. Wage premium for domestic substitution appears in sectors that actually fill (construction, meatpacking more so than fresh produce harvest), with a 2-4 year equilibration window. The single clearest historical finding: **domestic workers do not reliably backfill ag harvest slots at any speed**, while mechanization is available only for commodities where harvest technology exists (processing tomatoes: yes; strawberries, leafy greens: no).

**Scale calibration for SDL-01:** The 2.2M self-deportation figure is plausible relative to precedent and likely conservative. The 1929-1934 episode removed ~500K-1M from a Mexican-origin US population of ~1.7M (33-59% departure rate). The post-2008 wave removed ~1M cumulative from a Mexican-born population of ~12M (roughly 8%). SDL-01's 2.2M implies approximately 11% of the ~20M undocumented population departing in roughly 12-18 months — a faster rate-of-change than 2008-2011 (voluntary, gradual) but slower and more distributed than Operation Wetback (1.1M in a single fiscal year, geographically concentrated). The closest structural analog is a hybrid: the *rate of removal* approximates Operation Wetback but the *mechanism* (fear-driven voluntary exit, not sweeps) more closely matches 2008-2011. This hybrid implies: (a) faster housing effects than 2008-2011 because ICE raid *visibility* accelerates behavior before head-counts move; (b) faster ag labor disruption than 2008-2011 because departure is concentrated in ag-heavy regions where ICE is targeting; (c) slower mechanization offset because the technology development cycle is 5-10 years and was not pre-positioned for this shock.

## EPISODE 1: Mexican Repatriation, 1929-1936

### Numbers
| Metric | Value | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Total departed (1929-1936) | 500K-1M (consensus); 300K-2M (range) | Balderrama & Rodriguez (2006); Pew |
| Formally deported by US gov | ~82K (INS records 1929-1935) | USCIS archives |
| Peak year | 1931 (~138K departures) | INS historical |
| US-born children included | Est. 60K+ (CA alone) | TIME (2019) |
| Share of 1930 Mexican-origin population | 33-59% departed | Wikipedia / Census cross-ref |
| 1930 US Mexican-origin population | ~1.69M | Census 1930 |

### Geographic Concentration
- **Texas:** Largest absolute flows; >50% of total departures; cotton-belt counties hardest hit
- **California:** ~400K estimated (incl. US-born); San Benito -17.8% pop loss, El Paso -14.5%
- **Midwest industrial:** Chicago, Detroit, East Chicago IN — Midwest was 3% of Mexican-US pop but 10% of repatriates; East Chicago lost ~33% of Mexican population by 1932
- **Receiving areas in Mexico:** Jalisco, Guanajuato, Michoacan (same source states as 2025 self-deportees)

### Agricultural Labor Response
- Cotton pickers' wages: $1.21/100 lbs (1928) → $0.44/100 lbs (1931) — a **-64% nominal wage collapse** (TSHAOnline/Texas cotton data)
- Dust Bowl Okies partially substituted for Mexican ag labor in CA by mid-1930s — but **wages continued falling**, not rising, because Okies accepted lower wages than Mexicans had
- By late 1930s, seasonal farm workforce in CA was majority white US citizens for the first time — but this was a *depression-specific* domestic labor surplus that no longer exists in 2025-2026
- Key finding (Peri, Lee, Yasenov, NBER w26399): **No positive substitution effect on native employment** from repatriation. Repatriation-exposed cities performed *similarly or worse* on native employment and wages vs. unexposed cities. Jobs in administrative/supervisory roles that complemented Mexican labor also disappeared.

### Housing Response
- Per Santanna & Xu (working paper, "Send Them Back?"): **8.2 percentage point house value decline per 1% drop in city Mexican population** (Mexican-occupied units, 1930-1940)
- Rents in Mexican-occupied units fell **2+ percentage points per 1% Mexican outflow**
- Repatriation explained ~2.8% of the decade's 43% house value decline and ~3.3% of the 30.6% rent decline
- **Building permits declined 13.3 percentage points** per 1 SD increase in repatriation exposure
- Effect was **localized to Mexican-occupied housing** — no statistically significant spillover to US-born occupied units
- This is the key 2025-2026 implication: expect **rental softening in immigrant-heavy FL, TX, CA, AZ submarkets**, not broad-market appreciation

### Substitution Dynamics
- Domestic workers did partially fill slots — but only because the Great Depression had generated massive domestic labor surplus. **This condition does not hold in 2025-2026** (unemployment 4.4%).
- Wage premium for domestic ag substitution: not a premium — wages *fell* because Dust Bowl migrants underbid
- Peri et al. (NBER w23885, w26399): No net employment benefit to natives; employment losses in complementary occupations exceeded any direct job-opening effect

### Equilibrium Timeline
| Milestone | Timing |
|-----------|--------|
| Peak departure rate | 1931 (2-3 years after shock began) |
| Agricultural labor adjustment | ~3-5 years (Bracero program launched 1942, filling gap) |
| Housing market recovery | Not within decade studied (1930-1940) |
| Policy response | Emergency labor import (Bracero 1942) — 8-13 year lag |

### Political/Legal Context
- No federal legal authority — conducted by county sheriffs, welfare boards, railroad companies
- LA County explicitly organized train shipments; Michigan state government coordinated
- No due process; US citizens deported alongside nationals
- Analog for 2025: ICE raids with no-warrant entries, detention of documented workers — structurally similar coercive environment

## EPISODE 2: Operation Wetback, 1954

### Numbers
| Metric | Value | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Official INS count (FY1955) | 250,464 (17,695 removals + 232,769 "returns") | DHS historical |
| Commonly cited figure | 1.1M in FY1954 (includes prior enforcement year) | INS annual reports |
| Academic consensus | ~300K-500K actual physical removals | Britannica, UCLA faculty analysis |
| Duration of peak enforcement | June-October 1954 (~5 months) | INS operations timeline |
| Enforcement drop-off | >90% decline in apprehensions by 1955-1956 | Migration Dialogue/UC Davis |

### Geographic Concentration
- **Texas Rio Grande Valley:** Primary target — Texas cotton and winter vegetable growers
- **California Imperial Valley, San Joaquin Valley, Salinas:** Secondary sweep zones
- **Chicago, Los Angeles:** Tertiary — operations in urban centers disrupted industrial labor

### Agricultural Labor Response
- **Bracero substitution was simultaneous, not sequential:** INS coordinated with USDA to pre-position bracero contracts. Texas bracero enrollment surged from 168 (July 1953) to **41,766 (July 1954)** — a 248x increase in 12 months (Immigration History)
- Total bracero employment: 309,033 in 1954 → **445,200 at peak 1956** (UC Davis Rural Migration News)
- Braceros constituted **50-75% of labor** in CA asparagus, lemons, lettuce, tomatoes by 1956-1958
- Agricultural wages during bracero substitution: farm wages rose only 41% (1950-1960) vs. 63% for factory workers. DOL concluded braceros **suppressed farm wages below market-clearing levels** (UC Davis/Wilson Center)
- **Crop disruption was minimal** because the substitution was government-orchestrated and pre-planned
- **Critical SDL-01 difference:** No equivalent bracero substitution mechanism exists in 2025-2026. H-2A program is at 415K certified slots but has structural bottlenecks (processing delays, July interview dates for harvest-season workers per MARCO STATUS)

### Housing Response
- No systematic housing market data for this episode at the metro or county level
- Urban operations in LA/Chicago caused localized rental softening in barrio neighborhoods, per oral histories, but no quantified academic study exists
- The brevity of the enforcement campaign (5 months peak) limited housing market transmission

### Substitution Dynamics
| Mechanism | Speed | Scale |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Bracero legal guest workers | Immediate (pre-positioned) | Replaced ~100% of removed ag workers |
| H-2A analog for 2025 | 6-18 month delay (processing) | Partial replacement only |
| Domestic worker substitution | Minimal | Growers continued preferring Mexican labor |

- Post-Operation Wetback, undocumented immigration did not recover to pre-1954 levels until **the late 1970s** — a 25-year suppression period (EBSCO/Immigration History)
- This implies SDL-01 could create a **multi-year agricultural labor deficit with no domestic worker backfill**, a longer disruption window than the 2008-2011 episode

### Equilibrium Timeline
| Milestone | Timing |
|-----------|--------|
| Ag labor stabilized (via bracero) | <12 months (state-managed) |
| Undocumented immigration restored | ~25 years (late 1970s) |
| Bracero program terminated | 1964 — triggered mechanization wave |
| Processing tomato mechanization | 1964-1969 (5 years, 95% mechanized) |

### Political/Legal Context
- Military-style enforcement commanded by Gen. Joseph Swing (Eisenhower appointee)
- Growers were pre-consulted and bracero pipeline was pre-expanded before raids began
- Mexican government cooperated — deportees were transported deep into Mexico to prevent immediate re-crossing
- **Analog for 2025:** Trump administration has pre-expanded H-2A promises to growers, but unlike 1954, the substitution infrastructure is not actually pre-positioned and functional

## EPISODE 3: Post-GFC Self-Deportation Wave, 2008-2012

### Numbers
| Metric | Value | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Net Mexican migration flow reversal | Turned negative 2009; -140K net 2009-2014 | Pew Research Center (2015) |
| Total departed (Mexicans + families incl. US-born) | ~1M (2009-2014 cumulative) | Pew (2015) |
| Undocumented population peak | ~12.2M (2007) | DHS/Pew |
| Undocumented population trough | ~10.5M (2014) | DHS/Pew |
| Net decline | ~1.7M (7 years) | Pew/DHS cross-reference |
| Reason cited by returnees | 61% family reunification; economic displacement implied | Pew (2015) |
| Latino immigrant labor force exit | -1.1M active workers (2008-2011) | Brookings |

### Geographic Concentration
- **Arizona:** >100K departed in 2008 alone (most of any state); undocumented population fell 40% (2007-2012) (DHS/Working Immigrants)
- **Construction belt:** CA Central Valley, AZ, NV, TX, FL — all states with high immigrant share in construction
- **Meatpacking corridor:** IA, KS, NE — secondary concentration
- **Receiving areas in Mexico:** Remittances from US to Mexico fell **13.4% for first 9 months of 2009** (PRB) — measurable economic contraction in Mexican source states

### Agricultural Labor Response
- Unauthorized share of US farm workforce: **55% (2000) → 47% (2014)** — 8 percentage point decline over 14 years (USDA ERS)
- **Pace was slow** (voluntary, diffuse, driven by economic pull not enforcement push) — farms adapted gradually
- H-2A certifications grew from **~75K (FY2010) → ~275K (FY2020)** — a 267% increase over the decade, partially absorbing the gap
- USDA ERS finding: farms lost 200K+ laborers (2010-2020), driving an **estimated +15% rise in fruit and vegetable prices** over the decade (ERS historical data, cited in Civil Eats/UnidosUS)
- Arizona LAWA (2008 E-Verify law): reduced vegetable and melon acreage; **no substantive effect on farmworker wages** in the short run (AEI 2025 report citing UC Irvine study) — consistent with farms reducing production rather than bidding up wages

### Housing Response
- **Arizona vacancy rate:** surged from 9.8% → 16.8% following the 2007 crackdown + LAWA enforcement (National Immigration Forum; American Immigration Council)
- Phoenix: median home price -35% (Jan 2008-Jan 2009) — but this was primarily GFC/subprime driven, not immigration-driven; the immigrant departure was a *compounding* factor in rental softening
- Tucson rental vacancy: 10.5% by 2009 (HUD Comprehensive Housing Market Analysis)
- Key analytical challenge: impossible to separate immigration-departure effects from GFC effects in 2008-2011 housing data — both hit simultaneously. The Santanna/Xu methodology (1930s census) is cleaner because it isolates the repatriation variable from Depression-era housing declines

### Substitution Dynamics
| Channel | What Happened | Wage Effect | Timeline |
|---------|---------------|-------------|----------|
| Construction (domestic) | Some native re-entry but industry shrank 16% (2008-09) | Construction wages +15% (2010-2014, AZ BLS data) | 4-6 years |
| Ag harvest (domestic) | Minimal; farms cut acreage rather than raise wages | Essentially no premium paid | No equilibrium reached |
| Ag harvest (H-2A) | Slow expansion; grew 267% over decade | Wages partly stabilized for those hired | 5-10 years |
| Meatpacking | Partial native substitution | Some wage increase | 2-3 years |

- **Domestic workers did NOT reliably backfill ag harvest.** USDA finding: capital investment (mechanization) was not a perfect substitute in the short run; farms chose acreage reduction over wage competition (AEI 2025 citing multiple studies)
- **Construction is the partial exception:** Arizona farmworker wages rose ~15%, construction wages ~10% (2010-2014) — but this required a 4-6 year window and occurred during an industry downturn (so starting from a low base)

### Equilibrium Timeline
| Milestone | Timing |
|-----------|--------|
| Remittances began declining | Q3 2008 (within months of construction collapse) |
| Peak immigration outflow | 2009-2010 |
| Housing vacancy peaked (AZ) | 2009-2010 |
| Construction wages recovering | 2012-2014 (4-6 year lag from shock) |
| H-2A filled 50% of lost ag labor | ~2015-2017 |
| Full ag labor market equilibration | Not reached by 2020 |

### Political/Legal Context
- Primarily voluntary/economic (Great Recession, collapsed construction jobs)
- Reinforced by Secure Communities (2008-2013, removed 424K individuals)
- Arizona LAWA (2008), SB 1070 (2010) — state-level enforcement creating fear effects before federal enforcement caught up
- **Closest structural parallel to SDL-01:** Self-deportation driven by fear + economic deterioration, not primarily direct enforcement sweeps — but SDL-01 has *higher enforcement intensity* layered on top

## CALIBRATION: MARCO PREDICTIONS

**Prediction #14 (CA produce +15%, H2 2026):** Supported by analogs. The 2010-2020 episode shows +15% cumulative over a decade from a slower departure rate — SDL-01's faster, concentrated shock to CA Central Valley should compress that timeline. However, the 6-18 month lag from departure to retail price suggests H2 2026 is the *early* end of the window; 2027 may capture the larger move. Confidence: maintain or upgrade if H-2A data continues showing delays.

**Prediction #21 (planting-season produce spike):** Consistent with all three episodes. Ag labor disruption is the *fastest* confirmed economic transmission channel (1-3 months). Spring 2026 planting season is already live. WA cherry harvest rotting (2025), CA Central Valley ICE arrests up 58%, MARCO STATUS records. Confidence: high for the H1/H2 2026 window.

**Prediction #26 (housing start delays, border states, Q2 2026):** 1929-1936 analog shows building permits declined 13.3 percentage points per 1 SD increase in repatriation exposure. 2008-2012 analog shows construction industry contracted 16%. Neither episode saw rapid domestic substitution. Q2 2026 is plausible for initial delays; full impact likely Q3-Q4 2026.
