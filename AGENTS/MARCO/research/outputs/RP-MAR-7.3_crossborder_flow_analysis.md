# **Bi-National Economic Corridors: A Strategic Analysis of Transborder Flow and Trade Dynamics Across the Texas and Arizona Land Ports of Entry (2023-2026)**

The geopolitical and economic landscape of the North American continent is fundamentally defined by the terrestrial conduits linking the United States and Mexico. As of 2024 and 2025, the land border between these two nations has evolved from a mere logistical interface into a sophisticated economic ecosystem where high-value industrial supply chains, retail migrations, and national security protocols intersect. Mexico’s emergence as the primary trading partner of the United States, with total bilateral trade reaching approximately $839.6 billion in 2024, underscores the critical importance of the Land Ports of Entry (LPOEs) located in the states of Texas and Arizona.1 This analysis provides a comprehensive examination of the cross-border flows, trade volumes, and administrative shifts that have characterized this corridor through the middle of the current decade.

## **The Architecture of Commercial Flow: Trade Volume and Industrial Integration**

The commercial vitality of the U.S.-Mexico border is anchored by a select group of ports that facilitate the bulk of North American trade. The logistical dominance of Texas and Arizona is not merely a product of geography but of sustained infrastructure investment and the deepening of the "Maquiladora" manufacturing system.

### **Port Laredo: The Global Benchmark in Inland Logistics**

The Port of Laredo, Texas, has solidified its position as the preeminent international gateway in the Western Hemisphere. In 2024, Laredo was ranked the number one port in the nation among more than 450 airports, seaports, and border crossings, handling $339.03 billion in total global trade.3 This represents a 5.98% increase over 2023, driven by the increasing complexity of automotive and electronics supply chains.3

The physical infrastructure of Laredo is divided into specialized crossings. The World Trade Bridge serves as the primary commercial artery, while the Colombia Solidarity Bridge handles a mix of commercial and non-commercial traffic. Together, these bridges accommodate approximately 14,000 commercial trucks daily.3 However, empirical analysis suggests that these facilities are operating at roughly 40% of their theoretical capacity, indicating significant room for growth as nearshoring activities continue to mature in Northern Mexico.3

The commodity composition in Laredo reflects the high-tech nature of modern transborder trade. In late 2025, the primary imports were led by computers ($3.01 billion) and motor vehicle parts ($2.33 billion).3 The export side is dominated by refined petroleum products, aircraft parts, and internal combustion engines, illustrating Laredo's role as a bidirectional hub for the aerospace and energy sectors.3

### **El Paso and the Ysleta-Zaragoza Manufacturing Nexus**

El Paso serves as the second most critical commercial hub in Texas, with its economy inextricably linked to the manufacturing output of Ciudad Juárez. The Ysleta Port of Entry managed $83.38 billion in trade value in 2024, representing a significant 13.6% increase over the previous fiscal year.5 This growth occurred despite significant disruptions in the local logistics chain due to enhanced enforcement measures.

The El Paso Field Office, which oversees operations at the Bridge of the Americas (BOTA), Ysleta, and the surrounding ports, facilitates more than $280 billion in annual cross-border commerce.6 BOTA remains a unique entity as a toll-free bridge, which often leads to higher congestion levels compared to the toll-managed Ysleta facility.6

### **Arizona’s Strategic Gateway: Nogales and the Mariposa Port**

The Nogales Port of Entry, specifically the Mariposa facility, is the primary conduit for Arizona’s trade with Mexico. In 2024, Nogales managed $25.04 billion in total freight value, a 10.2% increase year-over-year.5 While Arizona’s trade volume is smaller than that of Texas, its strategic importance in the agricultural sector is unmatched. Nogales serves as the primary gateway for Mexican winter produce entering the western United States.

In the first four months of 2025, commercial truck crossings in Nogales rose by 3.9%, totaling 241,553 units.7 This growth is part of a broader trend where Arizona ports are capturing a larger share of the automotive and aerospace transit that was previously concentrated in California.7

## **Human Mobility: Pedestrian and Personal Vehicle Dynamics**

While commercial trade value has shown consistent growth, the movement of individual persons—pedestrians and those in personal vehicles—has exhibited a more complex trajectory. As of 2024 and 2025, these volumes have yet to fully return to the pre-2019 baseline, hindered by a combination of administrative costs and logistical friction.

### **Longitudinal Trends in Personal Crossings**

In 2024, the total number of pedestrians entering the United States from Mexico reached 20.7 million, a 6.7% increase from 2023\.9 However, this figure remains 17.4% lower than the volume recorded in 2019\.9 Personal vehicle crossings totaled 99.4 million in 2024, essentially flat relative to 2019 and slightly lower than the 2023 volume.9

In Texas, the Port of El Paso remains the leader in personal vehicle volume, managing 7.54 million cars in 2024, although this represented an 8.2% decrease from 2023\.9 Laredo, conversely, saw a 3.7% increase in personal vehicles, totaling 5.08 million.9

### **The Rise of Bus Transit in Arizona and Eagle Pass**

A notable modal shift has been observed in the increase of bus and bus passenger crossings. In April 2025, bus passenger counts at Eagle Pass, Texas, increased by 38% compared to the previous year.10 Nogales, Arizona, also experienced a 20% increase in bus passengers during the same period.10 This trend suggests that as personal vehicle inspections become more intensive, a segment of the population is opting for commercial bus transit as a more efficient or cost-effective alternative.

## **Geopolitical Shifts and Enforcement Impacts**

The mechanics of the border have been fundamentally altered by state-level and federal-level security initiatives. These operations have introduced new variables into the calculation of border wait times and logistical efficiency.

### **Operation Lone Star and Commercial Friction in Texas**

Operation Lone Star (OLS), a joint mission between the Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS) and the Texas Military Department, has been a central feature of the Texas border since 2021\.11 While its primary targets are illegal immigration and narcotics smuggling, its most visible impact on legal flow has been the implementation of "enhanced safety inspections" for commercial trucks.

In April and May 2024, Texas DPS resumed secondary inspections at the Ysleta-Zaragoza bridge in El Paso and the Bridge of the Americas.12 These state-led inspections occurred after vehicles had already been cleared by federal CBP officers. The results were dramatic: commercial wait times skyrocketed from an average of 45 minutes to over 8 or 10 hours.12 These delays caused a reported 60% drop in commercial traffic at certain ports as trucking companies sought to divert cargo to other states or less congested ports like Santa Teresa, New Mexico.6

The economic cost of these disruptions is substantial. Drayage companies, which rely on multiple daily turns across the border, were often reduced to a single trip, incurring massive productivity losses.15 Furthermore, these inspections were criticized for finding negligible amounts of contraband, leading many analysts to view them as a political tool for pressuring federal policy rather than a strictly security-focused measure.15

### **Federal Policy Under the Trump Administration (2025-2026)**

Following the transition to the Trump administration in January 2025, the federal approach to the southern border intensified. The "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" (OBBBA), signed on July 4, 2025, provided $46.5 billion for the construction of a "Smart Wall," integrating physical barriers with advanced sensors and detection systems.11

Federal reports from late 2025 highlight a policy of "zero releases" for illegal border crossers, which has been in place for seven consecutive months as of November 2025\.17 This environment of heightened deterrence has coincided with record-low encounter numbers, with total nationwide encounters falling to 30,367 in November 2025\.17

## **The Economics of Deterrence: Visa Fees and Retail Shifts**

One of the most significant changes affecting the cross-border flow of people is the imposition of new administrative costs and the changing landscape of the Mexican retail sector. These factors have fundamentally altered the behavior of the traditional "Mexican Shopper" who once sustained the economies of border cities like McAllen, Laredo, and Nogales.

### **The Visa Integrity Fee and Rising Entry Costs**

A major provision of the OBBBA was the introduction of the "Visa Integrity Fee," a $250 charge imposed on most non-immigrant visa applicants beginning October 1, 2025\.20 This fee applies to tourists (B1/B2), students, and temporary workers.22 While the fee is technically refundable if the traveler complies with all visa terms and departs on time, the process for reimbursement remains opaque.21

This fee represents a substantial financial barrier. For a family of four from Mexico City or Monterrey wishing to visit San Antonio or Phoenix, the upfront cost of visas has increased by $1,000.21 Combined with the increase in the I-94 arrival record fee from $6 to $24 and the ESTA fee for visa-waiver travelers increasing to $40, the "price of admission" to the United States has never been higher.21

Economic projections suggest that these costs will deter approximately 5.4% of international visitors, leading to an estimated annual loss of $3.6 billion in spending and tax revenue.24 Border cities, which rely heavily on day-trippers and short-term visitors, are expected to bear the brunt of this decline.

### **The Structural Decline of Exported Retail Sales**

The relationship between Mexican consumers and U.S. border economies has undergone a structural shift. Historically, Mexican shoppers represented up to 45% of retail sales in Laredo and 35% in McAllen.1 However, the share of "exported retail sales" (sales to non-residents) has been in a long-term decline.

In Laredo, this share fell from over 20% in 2002 to roughly 15% by 2023\.1 In Brownsville, the decline was even more dramatic, dropping from a historical average of 25.7% to just 3.3%.1 A critical driver of this shift is that wait times have replaced the exchange rate as the primary deterrent. A survey by the City of El Paso found that 53% of crossers from Ciudad Juárez prioritized wait times in their decision-making, while only 5% were primarily influenced by the peso-dollar exchange rate.1

### **Modern Trade and E-commerce in Mexico**

The expansion of modern retail formats within Mexico has provided domestic consumers with alternatives that were once only available in the United States. Walmart de México grew from 204 stores in 1998 to 3,154 by 2024\.1 Costco and Amazon have similarly expanded, with Amazon opening fulfillment centers in Tijuana and Monterrey to capture the growing Mexican middle class.1

## **Temporal Analysis: Seasonal Patterns and Anomaly Tracking**

Cross-border flow is typically defined by high predictability based on the calendar year, yet 2024 and 2025 have presented deviations from these norms.

### **Holiday Shopping Season: 2024 vs. 2025**

The winter holiday season remains the busiest period for pedestrian and personal vehicle transit. In December 2024, pedestrian crossings on the southern border reached a seasonal peak of 4,052,528, a 12% increase over the October volume.32 However, personal vehicle volume in December (6.66 million) was essentially identical to the October volume (6.67 million), suggesting that the traditional "holiday surge" in car traffic is being mitigated by wait times and administrative hurdles.32

In late 2025, anecdotal evidence from Nogales and Laredo indicated that while the holiday shoppers did arrive, they were not as "strong as they used to be".28 This observation aligns with the implementation of the $250 Visa Integrity Fee on October 1, 2025, which likely curtailed non-essential travel for many middle-class Mexican families.

### **Tariff Front-Running and the 2025 Commercial Anomaly**

In the first half of 2025, commercial truck crossings through the top ten ports rose by 0.3% year-over-year.7 While this sounds modest, it occurred during a period of intense enforcement. Analysts suggest this growth was driven by "front-running" of expected U.S. tariffs. As the Trump administration signaled a return to aggressive trade policies, manufacturers accelerated shipments of motor vehicle parts and electronics to build inventory before the effective tariff rate—which rose from 1.6% in 2024 to 10.6% in 2025—was fully applied.2

### **Real-Time Wait Time Volatility (2025-2026)**

Current wait time data for the transition from 2025 to 2026 shows continued volatility at Arizona ports. At the DeConcini crossing in Nogales, pedestrian delays reached 180 minutes on January 30, 2026\.33 At the Mariposa commercial facility, "lanes closed" were reported during evening hours, forcing a consolidation of traffic into the general lanes.35

In El Paso, the impact of federal policy is visible in the stabilization of wait times at the Ysleta-Zaragoza bridge, which averaged 30-40 minutes in early February 2026\.37 This relative stability, compared to the 10-hour peaks seen in mid-2024, suggests a normalization of enforcement protocols, even as the border remains "hardened" by the ongoing construction of the Smart Wall.37

## **Future Outlook: The Border as a High-Friction Economic Engine**

The research indicates that the U.S.-Mexico border is transitioning into a new phase of existence. The era of the "porous" border, characterized by fluid retail and social migrations, is being replaced by a model that prioritizes the high-security movement of industrial goods.

### **The Maquiladora Resilience**

Despite the frictions of Operation Lone Star and the $250 Visa Integrity Fee, the underlying industrial integration between the two nations remains robust. The reliance of the U.S. automotive and technology sectors on Mexican-made components ensures that commercial volumes will likely continue to grow.3 Laredo’s $339 billion trade figure is a testament to this resilience. However, the future of these corridors depends on whether infrastructure capacity (which is currently only 40% utilized in Laredo) can be unlocked by streamlining inspection technologies, such as the Smart Wall’s high-tech detection systems.3

### **The Border Retail Crisis**

For the retail sectors of Texas and Arizona border cities, the outlook is more challenging. The combination of rising administrative entry costs and the competitive modernization of the Mexican domestic market has fundamentally devalued the U.S. shopping trip. Cities like Nogales and McAllen must pivot away from a reliance on the "Mexican Shopper" toward becoming logistics and service hubs for the binational industrial machine.1

### **Conclusion**

The terrestrial border of 2026 is a study in contradictions: record-high commercial trade value occurring alongside record-low human encounters; high-tech "Smart Wall" infrastructure operating alongside administrative fees that deter middle-class tourism. As Texas and Arizona navigate this landscape, the Land Ports of Entry will remain the most important barometers of North American economic health. The transition to a more controlled, high-friction environment is not a temporary disruption but a permanent restructuring of the binational relationship.

The Port of Laredo will continue to define the standard for inland trade, while El Paso and Nogales will serve as the front lines for the integration of security and commerce. The data from 2024 and 2025 confirms that while the "human" border is narrowing, the "industrial" border is widening, solidifying Mexico's role as the indispensable partner in the American economic future.
