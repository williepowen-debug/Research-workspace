# **The H-2A Temporary Agricultural Worker Visa Program: A Comprehensive Analysis of Systemic Shifts in United States Farm Labor Dynamics**

The United States agricultural sector currently exists in a state of structural transition, where the historical reliance on a localized, often unauthorized workforce is being forcibly replaced by a formalized, high-cost guest worker system. The H-2A temporary agricultural worker visa program serves as the primary leading indicator for this transformation, reflecting the confluence of domestic labor scarcity, intensifying immigration enforcement, and a radical reorganization of agricultural wage-setting methodologies. As of early 2026, the program is no longer a supplemental labor source but the foundational infrastructure for specialty crop production in the United States, representing a significant share of the total labor utilized in labor-intensive sectors like fruits, vegetables, and horticultural specialties.1

## **Volume and Growth Trends in the H-2A Program**

The trajectory of H-2A visa certifications over the past two decades illustrates a nearly exponential increase in demand that persists despite escalating regulatory complexity and significant upward pressure on labor costs. Between 2005 and 2024, the number of H-2A jobs certified increased nearly eightfold, rising from approximately 48,000 to over 380,000.2 This growth reflects an accelerating depletion of the domestic labor pool, as younger generations of U.S. citizens and settled immigrants increasingly shun manual farm labor in favor of urban service and construction roles.5

### **Aggregate Certification and Visa Issuance (FY2020–FY2025)**

The period between fiscal year 2020 and 2025 has been marked by continued expansion, even as the growth rate showed signs of relative cooling compared to the surge seen in the mid-2010s. Nevertheless, fiscal year 2025 marked a historic milestone, with requested positions surpassing 400,000 for the first time in the program's history.7

### **Demographic Origins of the Guest Workforce**

The H-2A program relies almost exclusively on Mexico for its labor supply. While the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) maintains a list of over 80 eligible countries, approximately 93% of all H-2A visas are issued to Mexican nationals.12 This reliance is driven by established recruitment networks, geographic proximity, and a long-standing bilateral labor tradition.14

While Mexico regained its position as the primary origin for all international migrants in 2021 following pandemic-related restrictions, the H-2A program remains the most formalized channel for this migration.17 Efforts to diversify the labor pool, such as adding Belize to the eligibility list for 2025, are partially aimed at addressing regional instability in the Northern Triangle, though logistical costs for growers to recruit outside Mexico remain a significant deterrent.14

## **Processing, Capacity, and Institutional Constraints**

The administration of the H-2A program is a multi-layered bureaucratic process involving the Department of Labor (DOL), the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), and the Department of State (DOS). This system is designed to protect domestic labor markets but is frequently criticized by employers for being slow, rigid, and disconnected from the biological realities of farming.19

### **The 75-Day Regulatory Clock**

Under standard operating procedures, an employer must begin the H-2A process at least 75 days before the intended start date of need.19 This timeline requires high-precision forecasting of weather and crop maturity, which is inherently volatile.

* **75 Days Prior**: Filing the ETA-790 Job Order with the State Workforce Agency (SWA).19  
* **60-75 Days Prior**: Formal application for Temporary Labor Certification (TLC) with the DOL.19  
* **30 Days Prior**: DOL typically issues the TLC, at which point the employer must file the I-129 visa petition with USCIS.19  
* **14-21 Days Prior**: Approval from USCIS leads to consulate appointments in the workers' home countries for visa issuance.19

Despite the OFLC reporting a timely processing rate of 98%, this metric only accounts for applications that are "complete" and contain all necessary documentation (housing inspections, workers' compensation) at least 30 days before the start date.8 For many small-to-midsize farms, the "ideal timeline" rarely occurs, and late arrivals are common due to understaffing at government agencies and the complexity of coordinating international travel.19

### **Denial Rates and Deficiency Notices**

While the overall approval rate for H-2A petitions is exceptionally high—reaching 98.9% in FY2023—denials often stem from procedural errors or failures to establish a "bona fide" labor need.2 In the first half of FY2025, the OFLC processed 16,975 applications, of which only 81 were denied and 845 were withdrawn.8

Common reasons for institutional friction include:

* **Notice of Deficiency (NOD)**: Issued when applications contain errors in job descriptions or inadequate recruitment reports.25  
* **Duplicative Filings**: During peak seasons or government shutdowns, multiple filings for the same job opportunity result in non-acceptance denials.25  
* **State Workforce Agency (SWA) Discontinuation**: SWAs may refuse to post job orders if they believe an employer is non-compliant, effectively barring participation in the program.26

### **The Impact of Federal Funding Lapses**

The H-2A program’s dependency on federal appropriations makes it highly vulnerable to government shutdowns. During the 43-day shutdown in late 2025, the DOL ceased all immigration-related functions because it does not rely on user fees, unlike USCIS and the State Department.27

The 2025 shutdown halted the Foreign Labor Application Gateway (FLAG) system, preventing employers from filing new job orders or labor certifications.24 This created a "dead period" for labor planning, forcing the OFLC to issue emergency procedural changes post-shutdown, including a 33-day automatic extension for all submission deadlines and the temporary acceptance of expired prevailing wage determinations to avoid a total collapse of the 2026 spring planting cycle.29

## **Wage Rates and the AEWR Methodology Shift**

The Adverse Effect Wage Rate (AEWR) is the most contentious aspect of the H-2A program, representing the minimum hourly wage that must be paid to ensure foreign workers do not adversely affect the wages of U.S. workers.30 On October 2, 2025, the DOL issued an Interim Final Rule (IFR) that fundamentally altered how these wages are calculated, shifting the data source from the USDA Farm Labor Survey (FLS) to the BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS).22

### **The Transition from FLS to OEWS**

Historically, the AEWR was based on regional averages from the FLS, which often resulted in large, unforecastable annual spikes.5 Following the termination of the FLS by NASS in 2025, the DOL pivoted to OEWS data, which allows for state-specific and skill-based wage tiering.31

| Methodology Component | Old System (FLS) | New System (OEWS IFR 2025\) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Data Source | USDA Farm Labor Survey | BLS OEWS Survey |
| Occupational Detail | Single rate for most farm jobs | Two skill-based tiers (Level I & II) |
| Geography | 15 Multistate Regions | State or Territory-specific |
| Housing Adjustment | None (Housing provided for free) | Downward "Adverse Compensation Adjustment" |
| Binding Rate | Higher of AEWR, state/federal min | Higher of AEWR, state/federal min |

The new system distinguishes between "Skill Level I" (entry-level/unskilled) and "Skill Level II" (experienced/specialized).33 For Georgia growers, this shift provided immediate relief, as the entry-level rate for 2025 was set at $12.27 per hour, significantly lower than the previous $16.08 AEWR, although state minimum wages often provide a higher floor.36

### **Regional AEWR Trends for 2025–2026**

Despite the methodology change, AEWRs remain significantly higher than federal and most state minimum wages, particularly in the West and Midwest.

The Adverse Compensation Adjustment (ACA) allows employers providing free housing to reduce the hourly wage, provided it stays above the state minimum wage..33

The "Adverse Compensation Adjustment" (ACA) is a landmark inclusion in the 2025 IFR, intended to account for the non-monetary value of employer-provided housing.32 This adjustment ranges from \-$1.32 in Michigan to \-$3.00 in California, reflecting differences in fair market rents.33 Labor unions, led by the United Farm Workers (UFW), have challenged this deduction in federal court, arguing it effectively "cuts the wages of farmworkers twice" and violates the statutory mandate to protect U.S. workers' conditions.32

## **Substitution Signals and Economic Drivers**

The H-2A program serves as a critical barometer for the success of immigration enforcement and its impact on the agricultural labor market. There is significant evidence that H-2A certifications increase in direct response to heightened interior enforcement and the implementation of mandatory E-Verify systems.6

### **The Florida Enforcement Model**

Florida’s SB 1718, signed in 2023, represents the most aggressive state-level attempt to eliminate unauthorized labor from the agricultural workforce.39 The law mandates E-Verify for employers with 25 or more workers and criminalizes the transportation of undocumented individuals.39

This has created a powerful substitution effect:

* **Labor Exodus**: Significant numbers of settled, unauthorized farmworkers have fled Florida, citing fear of detention and family separation.39  
* **Certification Surge**: Florida led the nation in H-2A expansion in FY2025, adding over 9,000 workers despite having an AEWR that increased by 10% during the period.7  
* **Institutional Dependency**: Florida's agriculture industry is now structurally reliant on H-2A as the "only legal option," with high non-wage costs for housing and logistics becoming a permanent feature of the state's production expense profile.41

### **Sectoral Growth and Disaggregation**

While traditional field and livestock occupations (the "Big Six") dominate H-2A, there is substantial growth in disaggregated roles as farmers attempt to separate job duties to manage wage costs.5

* **Trucking and Logistics**: Heavy and tractor-trailer truck drivers now represent 1.5% of certified positions, with wages often $10/hour higher than standard farmworkers.5  
* **Construction and Support**: Positions for construction laborers (0.6%) and shuttle drivers (0.5%) have become more prevalent as farms expand their own infrastructure and housing.5  
* **Mechanization Support**: Farm equipment mechanics (0.2%) entered the top 10 H-2A occupations in FY2025, indicating a shift toward precision agriculture as a long-term labor-replacement strategy.5

Conversely, some regions and small growers are "abandoning" the program. A survey of producers revealed that 52% cited either complexity or expense as the primary reason for non-participation.31 Small family farms, in particular, struggle with the fixed costs of housing and the administrative burden of navigating 3,000 pages of federal regulations introduced in less than two years.2

## **Gaps, Constraints, and Strategic Resilience**

Despite its rapid expansion, the H-2A program currently fills only a fraction of the total demand for hired farm labor in the United States. Analysis of the broader farm workforce reveals significant gaps that H-2A, in its current form, cannot fill.43

### **Theoretical Capacity vs. Actual Demand**

There are approximately 2.5 million individuals employed on U.S. farms annually, of whom 80% were born in Mexico.1 H-2A workers currently account for only 15% to 20% of the total crop workforce and one-seventh of total average employment.1

| Labor Category | Population Estimate | H-2A Share |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Total Farmworkers (Annual) | 2.5 Million | \~14% |
| Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) | 1.5 Million | \~10% |
| Unauthorized Workforce | 680,000 \- 850,000 | N/A |
| H-2A Authorized Workers | \~352,000 (2024) | 100% |

The primary barrier to higher H-2A saturation is the "temporary or seasonal" restriction. Industries with year-round needs, such as dairy, mushrooms, and controlled-environment agriculture (CEA), are effectively excluded from the program, leaving them with few legal labor options as enforcement intensifies.21

### **Structural Barriers: Housing and Transportation**

The statutory requirement to provide free housing for H-2A workers is the most significant financial barrier for growers. USDA reports that housing costs approximately $9,000 to $13,000 per worker, not including the stringent OSHA and local safety inspections.45

* **Housing Quality and Crowding**: Substandard housing remains a major source of labor complaints, with reports of growers converting motels into "barracks" with bunk beds for 4 to 10 people per room.46  
* **Transportation Mandates**: Employers must cover inbound and outbound international travel as well as daily transport to the worksite. New regulations intended to improve safety in transit have added significant operational costs.45  
* **The Power Imbalance**: Because H-2A visas are tied to a single employer, workers have limited recourse against workplace exploitation or poor living conditions, as termination often results in immediate deportation and the loss of future recruitment opportunities.48

### **Perspectives on Reform**

Grower associations, led by the American Farm Bureau Federation (AFBF) and the National Council of Agricultural Employers (NCAE), are advocating for structural reforms to stabilize the system. Their positions focus on:

* **AEWR Stability**: Implementing a wage freeze or "guardrail" concept to prevent double-digit annual increases.26  
* **Year-Round Access**: Expanding H-2A to dairy and other non-seasonal sectors.26  
* **Streamlined Adjudication**: Moving toward an attestation-based system (similar to TSA pre-check) for "A-rated" employers to bypass redundant certification steps.20

In contrast, labor advocates such as the United Farm Workers (UFW) argue that any reform must include a pathway to legal status for the existing workforce and the elimination of the "tied" visa structure that gives employers complete control over a worker's ability to remain in the country.38

## **Strategic Outlook and Future Considerations**

The H-2A program has become the "indispensable backbone" of the American agricultural sector. The evidence from FY2020–FY2025 indicates that the program will continue to grow as the only legal mechanism for securing a manual labor force. However, the program's sustainability is threatened by a cycle of judicial volatility, where every new regulation is met with a preliminary injunction, creating a "puzzle of implementation" across different states.5

In the 2026 harvest season, the industry will likely see:

1. **Accelerated Adoption in the West**: California, which has historically lower dependence on H-2A (5%), is expected to increase its usage as the domestic workforce ages and mechanization proves unable to handle the delicacy of berry and vine harvests.1  
2. **Wage-Housing Friction**: The implementation of the Adverse Compensation Adjustment will continue to be litigated, with the potential for mid-season wage hikes if courts side with labor unions.32  
3. **Institutional Fragility**: The lessons from the 43-day government shutdown suggest that without a transition to a user-fee-funded model for the DOL, the agricultural supply chain will remain vulnerable to federal political gridlock.27

The H-2A program is a definitive indicator that the era of inexpensive, unauthorized labor in U.S. agriculture is coming to a close. The future of the domestic food supply depends on the ability of policymakers to modernize this guest worker system into an affordable, efficient, and ethical framework that can support the high labor costs and rigorous safety standards of the 21st-century farm.21
