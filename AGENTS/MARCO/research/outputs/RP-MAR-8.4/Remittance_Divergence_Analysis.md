# **Structural Divergence in the Western Hemisphere Remittance Corridor: A Comparative Analysis of Mexican and Central American Capital Flows (2024-2025)**

The global economic landscape of 2024 and 2025 has been defined by a historically anomalous and structurally significant divergence in the flow of personal remittances from the United States to Latin America and the Caribbean. For the first time in more than a decade, Mexico, the world’s second-largest recipient of remittances, has entered a phase of sustained contraction in nominal and real terms.1 Simultaneously, the nations of Central America—most notably Guatemala, Honduras, and El Salvador—have experienced an unprecedented surge in inbound capital, driven by what analysts identify as risk-mitigation strategies and asset liquidation by migrant populations facing heightened legal and political uncertainty in their host country.5 This divergence represents more than a simple shift in migration patterns; it reflects a fundamental change in the relationship between immigrant communities and their financial behaviors in response to shifting United States immigration enforcement, a maturing Mexican diaspora, and the volatility of the Mexican peso.8

## **The Mexican Remittance Contraction: A Milestone in Reverse**

Mexico’s remittance inflows have served as a bedrock of its national economy for over a decade, representing between 3.5% and 4% of the country's Gross Domestic Product (GDP).4 Between 2013 and 2024, these flows experienced an 11-year streak of uninterrupted growth, nearly tripling in volume from $23 billion to a record-high $64.7 billion.3 However, this expansionary phase reached a definitive conclusion in 2025\. During the first eight months of 2025, the accumulated flow reached $40.467 billion, a 5.9% decrease compared to the $43.001 billion received during the same period in 2024\.1

The decline became increasingly pronounced as 2025 progressed, with August recording an 8.3% year-over-year drop in nominal dollar terms.1 In real terms—when adjusted for domestic inflation and the appreciation of the Mexican peso—the contraction was significantly more severe, reaching \-13.1% in August alone.1 This trend continued through the end of the year, with November 2025 marking the seventh consecutive month where inflows, while remaining above the $5 billion threshold, demonstrated an annual decrease.8

The internal dynamics of this contraction suggest that the reduction is driven primarily by a decrease in the number of individual transactions rather than a significant drop in the amount sent per transaction. The volume of operations fell by 5.4%, whereas the average individual remittance remained remarkably stable, increasing marginally by only 0.28%.8 This suggests that while existing senders are maintaining their level of support, a growing portion of the migrant population has either stopped remitting or is sending money less frequently.2

## **The Central American Surge: Growth Through Precaution**

In stark contrast to the Mexican experience, Central American countries have emerged as the fastest-growing recipients of remittances in Latin America and the Caribbean during 2025\.5 While Mexico’s results were negative, nine other countries in the region, which originate more than 90% of all flows, saw significant growth, totaling an estimated $158 billion.6

The Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) reported that by the end of 2025, remittance inflows to Central America reached approximately $55.40 billion, a 20.4% increase over 2024\.5 This surge was most visible in Honduras (+26.0%), Guatemala (+20.2%), Nicaragua (+18.2%), and El Salvador (+17.9%).5 During the first five months of 2025 alone, Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras collectively received $18.547 billion, a 17% increase over the previous year.7

The reasoning behind this regional discrepancy is rooted in a fundamental shift in migrant behavior. Unlike previous growth cycles driven by new migration, the 2025 surge in Central America is largely attributed to "risk-mitigation decisions".6 Facing an environment of increased deportation activity and border enforcement, Central American migrants in the United States began sending higher average amounts—increasing by an estimated 27%—as a precaution.6 This strategy, often referred to as precautionary sending or asset liquidation, involves migrants transferring their savings immediately to bolster household economies back home, fearing they may soon be removed and unable to continue sending funds.6

## **The Currency Nexus: "Super Peso" and Real Purchasing Power**

A critical driver of the Mexican decline is the unique monetary environment of the Mexican peso. During 2024 and much of 2025, the peso experienced a sustained period of appreciation against the U.S. dollar, frequently referred to as the "Super Peso".8 Between November 2024 and November 2025, the peso appreciated 10.2% against the dollar.8

For recipient households in Mexico, this currency appreciation, combined with domestic inflation, has led to a catastrophic erosion of purchasing power. The real value of remittances dropped 14.4% annually in November 2025, marking the sixth consecutive month of real-term decline.8 Analysts note that while dollar-denominated income rose by 48.8% between 2020 and late 2025, the actual purchasing power in constant pesos increased by only 4.6% during that same period.8

| :---- | :---- | :---- |

This dynamic creates a disincentive for senders and a crisis for recipients. A migrant who previously sent $500, which converted to over 10,000 pesos, saw that same dollar amount yield only 9,500 pesos or less as the exchange rate fell from 20.90 to 18.73.9 This "loss" of roughly 700 to 1,000 pesos per transaction has forced families to prioritize basic consumption over the savings and investments that remittances historically funded.9

## **Demographic Maturation and the "Transit Migrant" Effect**

The divergence is further explained by the differing demographic profiles of the Mexican and Central American diasporas. The Mexican migrant population in the United States is increasingly categorized as "mature" or "established".9 New migration from Mexico has slowed significantly since 2007, and the Mexican-born population in the U.S. has dropped from 29% of the total immigrant population in 2010 to 22% by 2023\.19 As this population ages or its original ties to the homeland weaken through the death of relatives, the natural flow of remittances begins to diminish.9

In contrast, Central American migration waves have been more recent, particularly between 2018 and 2023\.16 These newer arrivals are more likely to have strong, immediate family ties and an urgent need to remit.6

Furthermore, a significant portion of the remittances historically recorded as "Mexican" actually represented funds sent to transit migrants—Venezuelans, Central Americans, and others—traveling through Mexico to the U.S. border.9 As the U.S.-Mexico border became "virtually sealed" to undocumented migrants in early 2025, with apprehensions falling from 250,000 in December 2023 to just 7,000 in March 2025, this transit migration plummeted.12 The collapse of this transit flow has removed a substantial layer of artificial volume from Mexico’s remittance statistics.9

## **Labor Market Dynamics and the "Fear Factor"**

The economic health of remittance flows is inherently tied to the participation of migrants in the U.S. labor market. In 2025, increased immigration enforcement and the threat of mass deportations created what experts call a "fear of public presence".2 Survey data indicates that roughly 30% of immigrants, and 75% of those who are likely undocumented, have avoided traveling, seeking medical care, or going to work to avoid drawing attention to their status.22

This fear has direct economic consequences. Analysts view the decline in Mexican remittance transactions as a barometer for U.S. employment stability for Mexican workers.8 In the first half of 2025, job growth in industries with high Latino employment, such as construction and transportation, collapsed compared to the same period in 2024\.23

## **The 1% Remittance Tax and Behavioral Shifts**

A new regulatory challenge emerged in 2025 with the passage of a 1% tax on international remittances sent from the United States using cash, effective January 1, 2026\.2 This tax, part of the "One Big Beautiful Bill" Act, is projected to affect at least half of all remittance senders, particularly those who are undocumented and rely on cash-originated transfers.6

The anticipation of this tax has already begun to alter remittance behavior:

* **Front-Loading:** Some migrants in late 2025 accelerated their transfers to move funds before the tax took effect.8  
* **Digitalization:** There has been a surge in the adoption of digital wallets and debit-card-based transfers, which are exempt from the cash-based levy.9  
* **Informal Channels:** Analysts expect a portion of the flow to shift to non-regulated intermediaries, such as "mules" or cryptocurrency wallets, despite the risks.6

In Mexico, the 1% tax is estimated to cost migrants roughly $3 billion between 2026 and 2034\.8 In response, the Mexican government and major retailers like Elektra and Coppel have promoted "remittance in kind" programs, allowing senders to purchase household goods in dollars for delivery to Mexico, effectively bypassing the cash transfer tax.9

## **Regional Vulnerability and Economic Dependence**

The social and economic impact of these shifts is most severe in regions with high remittance dependency. In Central America, remittances have risen to represent 23% of the regional GDP in 2024, sustaining nearly half of all households.28 In El Salvador and Honduras, the probability of planning to migrate decreases as per capita remittances increase, underscoring the role of these funds in stabilizing local populations.30

In Mexico, the decline has been most pronounced in states like Chiapas, Guerrero, Michoacán, Zacatecas, and Oaxaca.8 These states demonstrate a high dependence on remittances as a percentage of their state GDP, making them uniquely vulnerable to the current contraction.

## **Conclusion and Future Outlook**

The divergence between Mexican and Central American remittances in 2024-2025 reflects a complex interplay of demographic maturity, currency volatility, and the psychological impact of immigration policy. While Central America’s surge is driven by a precautionary "liquidation" of assets that may prove unsustainable in the long term, Mexico’s decline signals a more structural shift in the behavior of a mature diaspora and the collapse of the transit migrant corridor.

Looking toward 2026, the remittance environment will be shaped by the 1% tax and the continuation of restrictive migration policies. Projections suggest that Mexico may see a modest 2.6% rebound, though totals are expected to remain below the 2024 record.8 Central American countries, having exhausted much of their precautionary savings capacity in 2025, may face a sharp contraction if deportations intensify.6 For both regions, the era of effortless, double-digit growth has been replaced by a new reality of volatility, formalization through digital channels, and an intensified sensitivity to the political and economic climate of the United States.

The stability of these flows remains essential. As the primary source of external finance for millions of households, remittances continue to outpace Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and Official Development Assistance (ODA) combined.11 Ensuring the continued flow of these resources through formal, affordable channels will be the primary challenge for regional governments and financial institutions in the coming decade.
