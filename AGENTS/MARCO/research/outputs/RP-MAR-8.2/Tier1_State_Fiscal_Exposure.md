# **Fiscal Resilience and Macroeconomic Exposure: A Comparative Analysis of Border and Migration Dynamics in Tier-1 State Budgets**

The intersection of jurisdictional fiscal policy and demographic movement represents one of the most significant variables in contemporary American subnational finance. For the four largest states by population and economic output—Texas, Arizona, Florida, and California—the management of migration patterns and border-driven economic activity has shifted from a secondary policy consideration to a primary driver of budgetary stability and credit risk. This report examines the specific fiscal exposure of these states, examining the mechanisms through which migration flows and border dynamics penetrate state balance sheets, influence revenue structures, and ultimately transmit to state credit ratings.

## **Texas state fiscal exposure**

The fiscal landscape of Texas is uniquely defined by its 1,254-mile border with Mexico, which serves both as a massive engine of commercial activity and a significant source of operational expenditure. The state’s reliance on consumption-based revenue makes it particularly sensitive to changes in border-region economic health, while its aggressive posture on border enforcement has created an unprecedented category of state-level spending that redefines the relationship between state and federal fiscal responsibilities.

### **Operation Lone Star and the militarization of state budgets**

The most visible fiscal impact on the Texas budget is the massive allocation toward Operation Lone Star (OLS), an expansive law enforcement and border security initiative launched in March 2021\.1 As of 2024, the state has allocated or spent an estimated $11.1 billion on this program, representing an unprecedented level of state-funded border security activity.1 The mechanism for this funding has been a complex series of legislative appropriations and executive reallocations that illustrate the state's prioritisation of border security over other traditional government functions. During the 88th Regular Session, the Texas Legislature appropriated $5.1 billion for border security efforts for the 2024–2025 biennium, coordinated through thirteen different state agencies.3

The sheer scale of OLS spending has necessitated the diversion of funds from other critical sectors, creating a "crowding out" effect that has drawn significant scrutiny. In 2022, the state reallocated $360 million originally intended for the Texas Department of Criminal Justice (TDCJ) to support OLS, with the bulk—$339 million—going to the Texas Military Department to fund National Guard deployments.1 This followed an earlier diversion of $426.9 million from the prison system for similar purposes.1 More controversially, approximately $975 million in federal COVID-19 relief funds, intended for public health agencies, was redirected to the Governor’s office to administer OLS.1 This suggests a fiscal strategy where emergency balances and redirected agency funds serve as a flexible pool for border-related expenditures, often bypassing standard biennial planning for specific social services.

The long-term fiscal sustainability of this spending is currently tied to federal reimbursement prospects. Governor Abbott has formally requested that the federal government reimburse Texas for the $11.1 billion in state funds spent on OLS, arguing that the state is performing a role traditionally reserved for the federal government.2 Federal legislative efforts, specifically the "One Big Beautiful Bill" (OBBB), have included provisions to provide up to $13.5 billion in total for states like Texas that have incurred high border security costs during the Biden administration.6 If realized, this reimbursement would represent a massive one-time revenue infusion, potentially allowing the state to restore diverted funds to the Department of Family and Protective Services or Health and Human Services.4

### **Border region revenue dynamics and sales tax sensitivity**

Texas lacks a state income tax, making its budget heavily dependent on sales and use taxes, which constitute the majority of General Revenue. In the border region, this revenue is highly susceptible to the flow of people and goods across the international boundary. The Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission (TABC) operates a ports-of-entry program responsible for collecting excise taxes on imported alcoholic beverages along the Texas–Mexico border. In fiscal year 2025 alone, tax compliance officers at 30 ports of entry collected $5.5 million in taxes and administrative fees.8 This revenue, while small in the context of the total state budget, represents a direct fiscal transmission of cross-border consumer activity.

The state also provides significant direct support to border counties to manage the fiscal fallout of increased migration and enforcement activity. The 88th Legislature provided $330.8 million in General Revenue for fiscal year 2024 to provide counties with additional law enforcement resources.9 Furthermore, the Office of the Attorney General (OAG) has seen increased costs associated with property acquisitions for the construction of the border wall, requiring millions in General Revenue to fund legal assistants and assistant attorneys general.10 These expenditures are compounded by legislative mandates, such as House Bill 90, which provides a $500,000 lump sum payment to survivors of Texas military force members who died in connection with operations initiated to address criminal activity in the border region.8

### **Property tax trends and pension exposure in border municipalities**

The fiscal health of border cities like El Paso, Laredo, and Brownsville is intrinsically linked to the Texas Municipal Retirement System (TMRS). TMRS is a cash-balance plan where retirement benefits are determined by member contributions, city-financed monetary credits, and interest. As of December 31, 2024, the TMRS system-wide funded ratio was 89.4%, a slight decrease from the 89.7% reported in 2023\.7 While specific data on border-city-only exposure is not siloed in system-wide reports, the 942 participating cities include major border hubs whose fiscal stability determines their ability to meet annual contribution rates.

The property tax base in border counties is influenced by both the massive state presence and the logistical bottlenecks inherent in heightened border enforcement. Construction of the border wall, which could cost taxpayers more than $17 billion over its full lifecycle, involves significant land acquisition and legal processing by the OAG.1 While state-owned infrastructure is generally exempt from local property taxes, the economic activity generated by thousands of deployed state troopers and National Guard members creates localized demand for goods and services.1 However, this is offset by the potential for property damage caused by high-speed vehicle pursuits and other OLS-related activities, which some advocates argue require state reparations to maintain local fiscal equity.4

## **Arizona state fiscal exposure**

Arizona’s fiscal exposure is characterized by a structural revenue deficit and a highly formulaic relationship between state income tax collections and municipal funding. The state’s recent transition from a graduated income tax to a flat tax system has created a profound ripple effect across its border cities, many of which are disproportionately reliant on state-shared revenues.

### **The Urban Revenue Sharing crisis and the flat tax transition**

Arizona’s Urban Revenue Sharing (URS) program is a unique fiscal mechanism where a percentage of state income tax revenue is shared with incorporated cities and towns. Traditionally set at 15%, the share was increased to 18% in FY 2024 to mitigate the impact of the state’s move to a 2.5% flat income tax.11 However, because URS distributions are based on collections from two years prior, the full weight of the revenue decline is only now hitting municipal budgets. For FY 2025, URS distributions are projected to decrease from $1.56 billion to $1.27 billion—a nearly 19% decline.11

For border cities like Nogales, Yuma, and Douglas, this decline is particularly damaging. These municipalities rely on URS for general fund operations, including public safety and infrastructure maintenance. Projections for FY 2026 suggest an additional 10% decline as the flat tax regime fully matures and collections from the FY 2024 period (which saw a substantial decline in income tax proceeds) are used as the basis for distribution.12 This reduction in local funding coincides with increased demands for municipal services in border regions, creating a fiscal pincer effect.

### **Causes of the FY 2025 budget deficit and border components**

Arizona faced a projected General Fund shortfall of $(879) million for FY 2025, a situation that ballooned to a total projected shortfall of $1.71 billion when combined with FY 2024 deficits.14 This deficit was driven by several factors, some of which are indirectly tied to migration and regional demographics. A primary driver was the massive growth in the Empowerment Scholarship Account (ESA) program, which provides universal school vouchers. The budget provides $824.5 million for ESA awards in FY 2025, nearly $200 million above previous assumptions.14 This expenditure competes directly with General Fund resources that might otherwise support border-related infrastructure or law enforcement.

The state has had to navigate several upward spending adjustments, including $188 million for prison health care and $140 million for state employee health insurance.14 In border communities, the state's role in funding healthcare and education is magnified by the presence of non-citizen populations who may access certain state-funded services. The Arizona Department of Revenue (ADOR) has also faced challenges with the volatility of sales tax revenues, which grew by 3.3% in April 2025 but showed mixed performance in subcategories like contracting, which fell due to a decline in residential construction activity.16

### **Pension exposure and enforcement costs in border hubs**

The Public Safety Personnel Retirement System (PSPRS) is the primary pension vehicle for Arizona’s law enforcement and fire personnel. As of FY 2025, the PSPRS aggregate funding level reached 70.2%, marking its highest level in nearly 20 years.18 However, contribution rates remain high at 45.6% for PSPRS, placing a significant burden on municipal budgets.18 Border cities face unique pressures here; as they increase law enforcement headcount in response to migration-related security concerns or as part of state-led enforcement initiatives, their long-term pension liabilities grow.

The distribution of the Vehicle License Tax (VLT) and the Highway User Revenue Fund (HURF) also impacts border cities. Yuma, for instance, is estimated to receive $12.35 million in VLT distributions for FY 2025\.19 These funds are critical for maintaining the transportation networks that support both international trade and the movement of migrants and enforcement personnel. Any disruption in these flows or a decline in "county of origin" gasoline sales—which informs HURF allocations—represents a direct threat to regional infrastructure budgets.12

## **Florida state fiscal exposure**

Florida’s fiscal model is built on two pillars: tourism and a burgeoning resident population. Both are highly exposed to migration patterns, whether international or domestic, and broader macroeconomic volatility. Unlike the southwestern border states, Florida’s "border" is its coastline, making insurance markets and tourism tax collections the primary proxies for state fiscal health.

### **The tourism engine and revenue sensitivity**

Tourism is the primary driver of Florida’s General Revenue, largely through sales tax collections. In 2024, tourism delivered a record $133.6 billion statewide economic impact, supporting 1.8 million jobs.20 The study confirms that 143 million visitors came to Florida in 2024, continuing a multiyear streak of growth. The fiscal contribution is immense; out-of-state travelers spent $134.9 billion in 2024, with domestic visitors accounting for $120.1 billion and international visitors contributing $14.8 billion.20

The sensitivity of this revenue was exposed during the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent recovery. The Return on Investment (ROI) for Visit Florida’s marketing spend, which historically sits around 3.27, plummeted to 0.11 in FY 2021-22 as international travel remained depressed.22 While domestic tourism has since surged, with domestic visitor counts growing from 93.28 million in FY 2019-20 to 127.05 million in FY 2021-22, the international segment has been slower to recover.22 International visitor levels dropped to a low of 2.34 million in FY 2020-21 and only recovered to 7.24 million by FY 2021-22.22 This shift makes Florida’s budget more sensitive to the U.S. domestic economic cycle than to global trends, as domestic travelers now make up the overwhelming share of the $33.6 billion in federal, state, and local taxes produced by the sector.20

### **The insurance market backstop: Citizens Property Insurance**

The state’s most significant fiscal risk is arguably the Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, the state’s insurer of last resort. As private insurers have exited the market or raised rates due to hurricane risk and litigation, Citizens' exposure has exploded. In 2024, total exposure reached approximately $678.8 billion, a 23.2% increase over the previous year.23 The number of policies in-force is expected to stay roughly flat at 1.24 million, but premium volume is projected to increase by 36% to $7.1 billion due to higher average premiums and commercial risk growth.23

To manage this risk, Citizens has implemented a "depopulation" strategy to move policyholders back to the private market and has significantly increased its risk transfer budget. For the 2024 wind season, Citizens targeted $5.5 billion in reinsurance and capital markets coverage, with a maximum budget of $750 million.24 The fiscal danger to the state lies in the potential for assessments. If a catastrophic event exhausts Citizens' surplus and reinsurance, the state can levy surcharges on almost all Florida insurance policyholders. Approximately 100% of Citizens' surplus is estimated to be exposed in a 1-in-100 year hurricane event.26 This creates a shadow liability for the state budget, as a major disaster could trigger a de facto tax on residents that would dampen economic growth and population influx.

### **Population growth and service demand**

Florida’s rapid population growth, fueled by domestic migration from northern states, provides a robust tax base but also strains infrastructure and public services. Tourism-driven tax revenue currently saves the average Florida household nearly $2,000 per year in state and local taxes, effectively subsidizing the lack of a state income tax.20 However, the cost of maintaining this "low-tax" environment is a constant need for new schools, roads, and emergency services. The state’s ability to fund these needs is tied directly to the continued health of the tourism sector and the stability of the housing market, both of which are threatened by the escalating cost of property insurance. If the insurance crisis leads to a slowdown in migration, the state's revenue growth could stall while its infrastructure maintenance costs remain fixed or increasing.

## **California state fiscal exposure**

California’s fiscal exposure to migration and border dynamics is defined by its deep integration of immigrant labor into its core industries and the high volatility of its progressive tax structure. The state’s economy, the fourth largest in the world, is heavily dependent on an immigrant workforce that makes up over 27% of the population.28

### **Immigrant labor and the agricultural sector at risk**

California possesses the highest concentration of immigrant labor in its essential industries. Approximately 1.5 million undocumented individuals are active in the labor force, accounting for 8% of all workers in the state.28 These workers generate nearly 5% of California's GDP directly through wages, a figure that rises to 9% (approximately $275 billion in economic output) when accounting for ripple effects.28 They also contribute over $23 billion annually in local, state, and federal taxes, with the average undocumented immigrant paying $7,000 in taxes.28

The agricultural sector is the most acutely exposed to any disruption in migration or a potential labor shortage. Approximately 63% of California's agricultural workforce consists of immigrants, with 26% being undocumented.28 Research suggests that a loss of this undocumented workforce would cause the state’s agricultural GDP to contract by 14%.28 Similarly, the construction sector, where 26% of laborers are undocumented and 61% are immigrants of any status, would see a nearly 16% contraction.28 For the state budget, this translates into a direct loss of corporate and personal income tax revenue, alongside increased food prices and supply chain disruptions that have nationwide implications.

### **State costs for immigrant services and Medi-Cal adjustments**

California has historically offered more comprehensive services to its immigrant population than other states, but fiscal reality and federal policy shifts (specifically H.R. 1\) are forcing a retrenchment. Facing multiyear deficits estimated between $20 billion and $35 billion annually, the state is making discretionary cuts to immigrant-related services.32

The Governor’s 2026-27 budget proposes two significant actions within the Medi-Cal program:

1. **Ending Comprehensive Coverage**: The state would terminate comprehensive coverage for specific immigrant groups who will lose the majority of federal cost-sharing under H.R. 1\.32  
2. **Extending Eligibility Rules**: The budget proposes extending new eligibility rules, such as work requirements, to additional immigrant groups who currently receive state-only-funded coverage.32

These actions are expected to reduce state costs by approximately $900 million in 2026-27, with savings expected to "ramp up" to several billion dollars annually in future years.32 This represents a significant shift in California’s fiscal policy, moving away from universal coverage models toward a more restrictive model necessitated by structural budget gaps and a potential stock market downturn that threatens its primary revenue source: the tech sector.32

### **The tech sector concentration risk and wildfire/insurance crisis**

While the border and agricultural dynamics are critical, California’s budget is overwhelmingly reliant on the fortunes of the tech sector and Silicon Valley. In 2022, the tech sector contributed $623.4 billion directly to the economy, and when factoring in business-to-business interactions and consumption, it accounted for 30% of the state's economy and 30% of total state tax revenue ($55.9 billion).34 This concentration creates extreme revenue volatility; while companies like Nvidia can help erase deficits during market booms, the LAO warns that several "historically reliable signs" suggest the stock market is overheated, putting the state on "precarious footing" if capital gains revenue falls.32

This volatility is compounded by the ongoing wildfire and insurance crisis. While not as centralized as Florida's Citizens, California's insurers are also pulling back, prompting people and companies to move away due to the high cost of living and doing business.33 The state currently owes the federal government $20 billion for unemployment insurance, a debt that stems from the pandemic and which threatens to increase costs for businesses if not addressed through an overhaul of the funding system.33 This fiscal strain limits the state's ability to absorb the costs of providing services to its immigrant population and managing the border region's unique economic needs.

## **Cross-state comparative framework**

The exposure of Texas, Arizona, Florida, and California to migration and border dynamics varies significantly in both mechanism and magnitude. A comparative analysis reveals distinct vulnerabilities in retail, workforce, and federal dependency.

### **Retail decline and cross-border commerce**

Texas exhibits the highest exposure to cross-border retail decline. Its major border cities—El Paso, Laredo, and McAllen—are regional hubs for Mexican nationals who cross the border to shop, contributing significantly to the state’s sales tax revenue. While Arizona also sees significant retail activity in towns like Nogales and Yuma, the total volume in Texas, driven by its 1,254-mile border and large population centers, makes it the leader in this category. The TABC's collection of $5.5 million in port-of-entry taxes is just the tip of the iceberg; the broader sales tax generated by these visitors is a vital component of the state's 6.25% base rate collections.8

### **Workforce displacement and industry contraction**

California possesses the highest exposure to industry-level workforce displacement. The state’s $4.1 trillion economy is deeply integrated with immigrant labor in ways that are systemic. While Texas and Florida have large immigrant populations, California’s reliance on undocumented labor for 8% of its total workforce and its status as the nation’s top agricultural producer create a unique contraction risk.28 A loss of this workforce would cost the state an estimated $275 billion in economic activity, a figure that dwarfs the localized impacts in the other three states.29

### **Migration reversal and population-dependent revenue**

Florida has the highest exposure to migration reversal. Its fiscal model is almost entirely dependent on new residents and visitors. Unlike California or Texas, which have more indigenous industrial bases (Tech and Energy/Agriculture), Florida’s budget relies on the "tax savings" that occurs when residents move to the state, fueled by tourism revenue that covers $2,000 of the average household's tax burden.20 If the insurance crisis or climate-related risks lead to a reversal of this domestic migration, the state’s sales-tax-dependent budget would face a decline that its lack of an income tax makes difficult to recover from.

### **Federal funding dependency for border enforcement**

Texas exhibits the highest dependency on federal funding for border enforcement. With over $11.1 billion spent on Operation Lone Star, the state has moved into a position of "fiscal brinksmanship" with the federal government, requesting full reimbursement for what it deems federal duties.2 Arizona and California spend significant sums on services and localized enforcement, but Texas is the only state that has created a massive, standing military and police presence on the border using General Revenue and diverted emergency funds, making its budget highly sensitive to federal reimbursement legislation like the OBBB.1

## **Transmission to state credit and macro-resiliency**

The transmission of migration and border dynamics to state credit ratings occurs through three primary channels: revenue volatility, expenditure rigidity, and long-term liability management.

### **Credit ratings and rating agency commentary**

As of late 2025, the credit ratings for these four states reflect a range of fiscal health and management philosophies. Texas and Florida maintain "Triple-A" ratings across the board, reflecting their massive rainy day funds and robust, albeit consumption-heavy, revenue bases. Arizona sits slightly lower at AA, while California maintains an AA- rating, reflecting its higher revenue volatility and structural deficits.36

Rating agencies generally monitor migration patterns as a leading indicator of economic growth. For Texas and Florida, the rapid population growth is viewed as a credit positive, though it brings infrastructure strain. For California, the LAO's warning about the "cost of living and doing business" prompting people and companies to move away is a key credit concern.33 Any rating agency commentary on border exposure typically focuses on the state's ability to maintain "roughly balanced" budgets while managing these exogenous shocks.33

### **Rainy day funds and pension funding ratios**

The strength of a state’s "Rainy Day Fund" or Budget Stabilization Fund is the primary defense against migration-driven fiscal shocks. Texas entered the 2024-25 biennium with a historic $32.7 billion surplus, providing the cash flow needed to front-load $11.1 billion in border spending.3 Arizona, by contrast, has a $1.5 billion Budget Stabilization Fund, but this is increasingly taxed by its $(879) million annual deficits.14

Pension funding ratios also play a role in the macro-fiscal outlook. Texas (TMRS at 89.4%) and Arizona (PSPRS at 70.2%) have relatively healthy or improving systems, though Arizona's high contribution rates (45.6%) remain a fiscal drag.7 California’s lower credit rating is partially influenced by its $20 billion debt to the federal government for unemployment insurance and the high costs of maintaining its school facilities building renewal programs.15

The fiscal exposure of these four states to migration and border dynamics is not a uniform phenomenon but a series of distinct challenges. For Texas, it is the cost of enforcement. For Arizona, it is the formulaic decline in shared revenues. For Florida, it is the insurance backstop and tourism sensitivity. For California, it is the systemic integration of immigrant labor. As these states move into the late 2020s, their ability to maintain credit stability will depend on their flexibility in reallocating funds and their success in securing federal support for what have effectively become state-led national security operations.
