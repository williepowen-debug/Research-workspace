# **Fiscal Intelligence Report: Municipal Solvency, Revenue Trajectories, and Credit Risk in the U.S.-Mexico Border Region (2023–2025)**

## **1\. Executive Intelligence Summary**

The fiscal architecture of the United States southern border region, specifically traversing the corridor from the Rio Grande Valley in Texas to the Santa Cruz County interface in Arizona, presents a landscape of stark economic bifurcation in the 2023–2025 reporting period. While macroeconomic narratives often reduce the border region to a monolithic entity defined by immigration flows and federal security policy, a forensic analysis of municipal finance data reveals a distinct divergence in fiscal health, revenue autonomy, and creditworthiness between the eastern and western anchors of this trade corridor. This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level examination of the fiscal conditions governing six critical border municipalities: Laredo, El Paso, McAllen, Brownsville, and Pharr in Texas, and Nogales in Arizona.

The analysis indicates that the post-pandemic economic recovery has evolved into a sustained, structurally reinforced expansion for the lower Rio Grande Valley (RGV) cities of McAllen and Brownsville. These municipalities are currently characterized by historic sales tax remittances, landmark credit rating upgrades to the 'AA+' tier, and manageable long-term liability profiles supported by robust industrial and aerospace investment. Conversely, the western anchor of the Texas border, El Paso, faces a period of acute fiscal distress. The city and its overlapping jurisdictions are grappling with structural deficits, substantial pension underfunding in a single-employer trust, and stagnant revenue growth necessitated by aggressive commercial property tax appeals that have eroded the non-residential tax base.

Pharr, acting as a high-growth logistics hub, exhibits emerging credit risks associated with leverage and fund balance depletion, precipitating a negative outlook from major rating agencies despite strong toll revenues. Laredo, the nation's premier inland port, demonstrates a volatile yet growing revenue profile heavily levered to industrial cycles, while Nogales, Arizona, remains fiscally constrained by state-shared revenue dependence and acute sensitivity to cross-border retail friction.

This report synthesizes data from 2023–2025 municipal annual comprehensive financial reports (ACFRs), actuarial valuations, rating agency rationales (S\&P Global, Moody’s, Fitch), state comptroller allocations, and property tax appraisal datasets to provide a comprehensive risk assessment.

## ---

**2\. Macro-Fiscal Context: The "Two Borders" Economy**

To understand the specific fiscal trajectories of the six target cities, one must first contextualize the macroeconomic environment shaping their revenue streams. The period from 2023 to 2025 has been defined by three dominant themes: the maturation of nearshoring supply chains, the divergence of retail consumption patterns, and the escalating cost of municipal operations due to inflation and wage compression.

### **2.1 The Divergence of Sales Tax Reliance**

Sales tax revenue serves as the primary barometer for the economic vitality of these border cities, often constituting a larger share of the General Fund than property taxes in the retail-heavy hubs of the RGV. This heavy reliance exposes municipal budgets to three external variables:

1. **The Peso-Dollar Exchange Rate:** The purchasing power of Mexican nationals remains a critical driver for McAllen, Laredo, and Nogales.  
2. **Logistics Velocity:** For Laredo and Pharr, sales tax is derived not just from retail, but from the taxable business-to-business (B2B) activity generated by the logistics sector.  
3. **Inflationary Erosion:** While inflation theoretically boosts sales tax collections (as goods cost more), it simultaneously drives up the cost of providing municipal services—fuel, construction materials, and labor—often at a rate faster than revenue growth.

The data suggests a decoupling of these drivers across the region. The RGV is capturing a "consumption boom" fueled by regional population growth and the "SpaceX effect" in Brownsville 1, creating a surplus of revenue. In contrast, El Paso and Nogales are seeing a "consumption plateau," where saturation in the local retail market and friction at ports of entry have stalled revenue growth, leaving budgets exposed to rising fixed costs.2

### **2.2 The Pension Liability Divide**

A critical, often underreported differentiator in the fiscal health of Texas border cities is the structure of their pension obligations. Cities participating in the statewide Texas Municipal Retirement System (TMRS)—specifically McAllen, Brownsville, Pharr, and Laredo—benefit from a cash-balance plan design that includes variable contribution rates and conservative actuarial assumptions, generally leading to higher funded ratios and greater stability.

In sharp contrast, the City of El Paso maintains an independent, single-employer pension trust (CEPERT). This structure has lacked the pooled investment resilience and rigorous oversight of the state system, leading to significant underfunding and a growing liability that threatens to crowd out general service expenditures. Nogales, participating in the Arizona Public Safety Personnel Retirement System (PSPRS), faces its own set of challenges related to legacy liabilities, necessitating austere budget management to meet aggressive funding targets by 2036\.4

**3\. Deep Dive: The Rio Grande Valley Renaissance (McAllen, Brownsville, Pharr)**

The Rio Grande Valley (RGV) has emerged as the fiscal standout of the border region during the 2024-2025 cycle. The confluence of nearshoring industrial investment, population growth, and prudent financial management has bolstered the credit profiles of its major cities.

### **3.1 City of McAllen: The Regional Retail Hegemon**

McAllen continues to demonstrate exceptional fiscal resilience, functioning as the primary retail and entertainment catchment area for both the RGV and Northern Mexico. Its fiscal strategy relies on leveraging this consumption volume to subsidize property tax rates, maintaining one of the lowest tax burdens in the region while funding high-quality services.

#### **3.1.1 Sales Tax Revenue Trends**

McAllen's sales tax performance in the 2023–2025 period has been nothing short of historic. The city has broken consecutive records for monthly remittances, signaling that its retail sector is expanding rather than contracting in the face of e-commerce.

* **Historic Allocations:** In February 2025, the City of McAllen received a record-breaking $11.9 million sales tax allocation for the single month of December sales. This figure represented a substantial 12.3% year-over-year increase compared to the same period in the prior year.5  
* **Sustained Momentum:** The fiscal year-to-date (FYTD) allocations for the 2024–2025 cycle showed a consistent upward trajectory, with total allocations up approximately 6.14% compared to the previous year.5  
* **Sector Diversification:** Detailed sector analysis reveals that while retail trade remains the dominant contributor, the city has successfully diversified into the "experience economy." Significant gains were recorded in food and beverage services, entertainment, and hospitality, validating the city's strategic investments in performing arts and convention facilities.5  
* **Comparative Advantage:** McAllen ranked second in sales tax per capita in the entire state of Texas for cities with populations over 100,000. This metric is crucial: it indicates that McAllen’s tax base is heavily subsidized by non-residents (tourists and regional shoppers), relieving the fiscal pressure on local property owners.5

#### **3.1.2 Budget Health and Credit Standing**

The robustness of the sales tax stream has translated directly into a fortress balance sheet for McAllen.

* **Credit Rating Affirmation:** Fitch Ratings affirmed McAllen's **'AA+'** rating with a Stable Outlook in late 2024\.7 This high-grade rating reflects the city's "strong financial resilience," characterized by ample reserve levels that far exceed regulatory minimums.  
* **Property Tax Relief:** Leveraging its sales tax surplus, the city was able to reduce its property tax rate to approximately **$0.449989 per $100 valuation** for FY 2025\.8 Despite this rate cut, General Fund revenues were still projected to increase by 3%, proving that the expansion of the tax base (via new commercial construction) is outpacing the rate reduction.  
* **Surplus Operations:** The city consistently generates operating surpluses. These funds are frequently used to cash-fund capital improvement projects (CIP), avoiding the interest costs associated with debt issuance. For FY 2025, the budget anticipates General Fund revenues to increase by 3% over the adjusted budget of the prior year.8

#### **3.1.3 Pension Funding Status**

McAllen participates in the Texas Municipal Retirement System (TMRS).

* **Funding Ratio:** The city’s pension plan is robustly funded, benefitting from the TMRS system-wide funded ratio of approximately **89.4%** as of December 2024\.9  
* **Contribution Discipline:** McAllen consistently pays its full Actuarially Determined Contribution (ADC). The city’s prudent management of this liability is a key factor in its strong credit rating, as it presents no looming threat to the General Fund.10

### **3.2 City of Brownsville: Industrial Diversification and Credit Upgrades**

Brownsville has transitioned from a developing border economy to a diversified industrial hub. The "SpaceX effect," combined with the expansion of the Port of Brownsville, has catalyzed a doubling of the tax base over the last decade, leading to significant credit rating upgrades.

#### **3.2.1 Sales Tax and Revenue Growth**

Brownsville’s revenue profile is increasingly driven by industrial and B2B activity alongside traditional retail.

* **Revenue Expansion:** The city generated over **$53 million** in sales tax revenue in the most recent annual reporting period.11 This revenue growth has been pivotal in enabling the city to absorb inflationary cost pressures without structural deficits.  
* **Base Expansion:** The city's total tax base has doubled over the past ten years, a rate of growth that exceeds most peer cities in the region. This expansion is attributed to diverse industries, including the aerospace sector and continued deep-water port activities.1

#### **3.2.2 Credit Rating Milestone**

The most significant fiscal event for Brownsville in the 2024–2025 cycle was its credit rating upgrade.

* **Upgrade to 'AA+':** In January 2025, S\&P Global Ratings raised Brownsville’s long-term issuer credit rating to **'AA+'** from 'AA'. This is the highest rating in the city’s history and places it on par with much wealthier suburban municipalities.12  
* **Rationale:** S\&P cited "vigorous economic growth" and the city's role as a regional trade center. Crucially, the agency noted the city's "well-embedded management practices," highlighting a shift toward sophisticated multi-year financial planning.12  
* **Moody's Alignment:** Moody's also assigned an **'Aa3'** rating (equivalent to AA-) to the city's issuances, praising its "strong financial management" and "very strong reserves".13

#### **3.2.3 Budget and Property Tax Dynamics**

Brownsville’s budget strategy reflects a balance between capital investment and fiscal conservatism.

* **Balanced Budget:** The City Commission adopted a **$253 million** balanced budget for FY 2026\. Notably, this budget maintains the property tax rate at **$0.603504 per $100 valuation**, marking the fourth consecutive year without a tax rate increase despite rising property values.14  
* **Workforce Investment:** The robust revenue environment allowed the city to implement a 3% cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) for employees and raise the minimum wage to $13.00 per hour, addressing labor market competitiveness without creating a deficit.16  
* **Commercial Assessment Growth:** Commercial property values in Cameron County (Brownsville) rose by **4.6%** in 2025, with new commercial construction values surging by **24.5%**.17 This influx of new taxable value is the engine allowing for rate stabilization.

### **3.3 City of Pharr: High Growth with Emerging Leverage Risks**

Pharr presents the most complex fiscal profile in the RGV: a city experiencing rapid logistics-driven growth but showing signs of strain on its governmental fund balances due to heavy capital spending.

#### **3.3.1 Sales Tax and Bridge Revenue**

Pharr’s economy is inextricably linked to the Pharr-Reynosa International Bridge, the number one produce crossing in the United States.

* **Sales Tax Surge:** Pharr reported a **10.08% year-over-year increase** in sales tax collections for December 2025 alone, with year-to-date collections up **4.28%**.18 This growth outperforms the broader state average and highlights the density of logistics-related transactions occurring within city limits.  
* **Bridge Revenues:** The international toll bridge system continues to generate substantial surplus revenues, supporting an 'A' rating on the bridge’s own revenue bonds.19 These revenues are frequently transferred to the General Fund to subsidize city operations.

#### **3.3.2 Budget Health and Warning Signals**

Despite the revenue boom, Pharr faces distinct credit risks related to its spending velocity.

* **Negative Outlook Warning:** In November 2025, S\&P Global Ratings revised the outlook on Pharr’s 'A+' general obligation rating to **Negative**.20  
  * **The Risk:** The negative outlook reflects S\&P’s view that there is a "one in three chance" the city will be unable to stabilize operations within two years, potentially leading to a further depletion of an already reduced fund balance position.20  
  * **Structural Imbalance:** The rating action implies that the city’s expenditure growth—driven by capital projects and operational expansions—has outpaced even its robust revenue growth, leading to a structural reliance on reserves or transfers that may not be sustainable.  
* **Pension Liability:** Among the TMRS cities analyzed, Pharr carries the highest relative pension burden. The city’s Unfunded Actuarial Accrued Liability (UAAL) stood at **51.2%** of covered payroll in 2024\.21 While the city is making required contributions, this high liability ratio reduces its long-term financial flexibility compared to McAllen or Brownsville.

**4\. Deep Dive: The Western Corridor Stagnation (El Paso)**

The fiscal trajectory of El Paso stands in stark contrast to the RGV. The city is grappling with a "hollow boom," where nominal economic activity is insufficient to cover the escalating costs of municipal government, leading to structural deficits and difficult labor market adjustments.

### **4.1 Structural Budget Deficit and Crisis Response**

El Paso is currently navigating its most severe fiscal stress test in years. The convergence of stagnant sales tax growth, rising fixed costs, and an eroding commercial tax base has created a persistent structural deficit.

* **Deficit Magnitude:** The County of El Paso projects a budget deficit ranging from **$55 million to $62 million** for 2025\. This represents a dramatic escalation from mid-year estimates of $20–$25 million, suggesting a rapid deterioration in revenue forecasting or unexpected cost spikes.22  
* **City Deficit Dynamics:** The City of El Paso is similarly strained. For FY 2025, the city reduced its reliance on reserve funds to balance the budget to $4 million (down from $7.2 million), but the continued use of one-time funds for recurring operations indicates a failure to achieve structural balance.23  
* **Stagnant Revenues:** A primary driver of this crisis is the stagnation of sales tax revenue. For FY 2025, the city projected sales tax revenue to increase by only **$3.0 million**. This is a precipitous drop from the **$24.5 million** increase recorded in the prior year.2 This 88% deceleration in growth signals a saturation of the local retail market and a potential weakening of cross-border consumption power relative to inflation.  
* **Austerity Measures:** The depth of the deficit has forced immediate corrective actions:  
  * **Hiring Freeze:** The County implemented a mandatory **16-week hiring delay** for all vacancies to conserve cash.24  
  * **Service Risks:** Local officials have publicly managed expectations downward, stating that "government is not going to be able to do as much" in the coming fiscal cycles.22  
  * **Regional Labor Market Impact:** While the City strives to avoid layoffs, the fiscal contraction is evident in the broader public sector, with the local school district announcing layoffs due to a $38 million shortfall.25

### **4.2 Commercial Tax Base Erosion: The Appeals Crisis**

A critical, under-reported factor driving El Paso's fiscal distress is the erosion of its commercial property tax base through aggressive and successful appeals.

* **Valuation Declines:** For the 2025 tax year, despite a general increase in market value, specific commercial sectors in El Paso saw **decreases** in assessed value after the appeals process concluded.  
  * **Apartments:** Taxable value declined by **4.2%**.26  
  * **Retail Property:** Value declined by **0.2%**.26  
  * **Warehouses:** Value declined by **0.8%**.26  
* **The "Hollow" Residential Boom:** In contrast to the commercial sector, residential values surged by nearly 6%, and medical office values jumped by 24.8%.27 However, the successful reduction of commercial values (often by large corporate entities using sophisticated tax counsel) shifts the tax burden disproportionately onto residential homeowners.  
* **Political Constraint:** This shift creates a political ceiling on tax rates. With homeowners already facing a 6% valuation hike, the City Council faces immense pressure to lower the nominal tax rate, further restricting the revenue available to close the budget deficit.2

### **4.3 Pension Crisis: The Single-Employer Risk**

El Paso’s long-term fiscal stability is threatened by the significant underfunding of its single-employer pension trust, the City of El Paso Employees Retirement Trust (CEPERT).

* **Funding Ratio:** As of the September 1, 2024 actuarial valuation, the plan’s funded ratio stood at a critical **64.0%** on an Actuarial Value of Assets (AVA) basis and an even more concerning **59.5%** on a Market Value of Assets (MVA) basis.28  
* **Liability Growth:** The Unfunded Actuarial Accrued Liability (UAAL) has ballooned to approximately **$325 million**.28  
* **Contribution Shortfall:** Unlike TMRS cities where the contribution rate automatically adjusts to cover the ADC, El Paso’s contributions are often set by ordinance or budget capacity. In 2024, the plan faced a **2.57% contribution shortfall** relative to the actuarially determined requirement.28  
* **Implication:** This structural underfunding acts as a "soft liability" that will eventually require a massive infusion of capital—likely through Pension Obligation Bonds (POBs) or severe benefit cuts—further restricting future budgets.

### **4.4 Credit Rating Resilience**

Despite these headwinds, El Paso retains an **'AA'** rating from S\&P with a stable outlook.29 This apparent contradiction reflects the rating agency's view of the city's *capacity* to pay (a large, diverse tax base) rather than its current *willingness* to structurally align the budget. However, persistent deficits and pension neglect pose a material risk to this rating in the medium term.

**5\. Deep Dive: The Inland Port & The Arizona Outlier (Laredo & Nogales)**

### **5.1 Laredo, Texas: Volatility in the Inland Port**

Laredo’s economy is a direct derivative of international trade volumes, making its revenue streams volatile yet fundamentally robust over the long cycle.

* **Commercial Valuation Boom:** In stark contrast to El Paso, Webb County (Laredo) reported a staggering **28.8% increase** in commercial property values for 2025\.30 This reflects the massive demand for logistics real estate—warehouses, cold storage, and distribution centers—needed to process the record volume of goods crossing the border.  
* **Sales Tax Volatility:** While the property tax base is booming, sales tax shows sector-specific volatility. Remittances from mining and construction sectors saw sharp declines in late 2024, while wholesale trade surged.31 This volatility necessitates larger reserve cushions to manage cash flow swings.  
* **Budget & Credit:** The City maintains an **'AA'** rating. Its FY 2026 budget process is focused on managing the capital infrastructure needs of the inland port, with significant allocations for bridge expansion and roadway improvements to sustain trade velocity.32

### **5.2 Nogales, Arizona: Austerity and Dependence**

Nogales operates in a significantly more constrained fiscal environment than its Texas counterparts, driven by Arizona’s centralized tax collection structure and specific border friction issues.

* **State-Shared Revenue Dependence:** Nogales relies heavily on Urban Revenue Sharing (URS) from the State of Arizona. With the state facing its own deficit in FY 2025 due to income tax cuts and flat revenues, URS distributions to cities like Nogales are under pressure, creating a revenue squeeze that is out of local control.34  
* **Sales Tax Shortfalls:** Local sales tax collections are slowing, mirroring the state-wide trend of 2.7% growth (the lowest since 2011).3 The city explicitly cites "unfavorable exchange rates" and border wait times as factors dampening retail sales.36  
* **Operational Austerity:** The fiscal tightness is visible in the city’s budget adoption process. The City Council adopted the FY 2025-26 budget only after cutting **$130,000** designated for a deputy city manager position, signaling that every dollar of administrative overhead is being scrutinized.37  
* **Pension Reform Goal:** Nogales faces a significant burden from the Arizona Public Safety Personnel Retirement System (PSPRS). The City Council has adopted a policy goal to reach **100% funding by 2036**.4 Achieving this will require sustained supplemental contributions, further crowding out discretionary spending for the next decade.

**7\. Warning Signals and Forward Outlook**

The 2025 fiscal landscape of the border region is characterized by a "K-shaped" divergence.

**The Upper Trajectory (The RGV):**

McAllen and Brownsville are capitalizing on a unique economic moment. The combination of nearshoring manufacturing, the space industry, and robust retail consumption is generating natural revenue growth that outpaces inflation.

* **Outlook:** **Positive.** Expect continued credit stability or upgrades. The primary risk is a national recession dampening retail sales, but high reserve levels provide a substantial buffer.

**The Middle Trajectory (Pharr & Laredo):**

These cities are growing but face specific risks.

* **Pharr Warning:** The **Negative Outlook** from S\&P is a critical signal. Pharr must decelerate its spending or drastically increase revenues to avoid a downgrade. The reliance on bridge transfers is a vulnerability if trade policy shifts.  
* **Laredo Outlook:** **Stable.** Volatility is inherent to its model, but the underlying asset (the port) remains the most critical node in North American trade.

**The Lower Trajectory (El Paso & Nogales):**

El Paso faces the most significant structural challenges.

* **El Paso Warning:** The convergence of a structural deficit, a hiring freeze, a 60% funded pension plan, and an eroding commercial tax base constitutes a **Fiscal Emergency**. Without significant structural reform—likely involving tax increases or deep service cuts—the city risks a credit downgrade and long-term service degradation.  
* **Nogales Warning:** The city is hamstrung by external factors (State revenue, Federal border policy). Its fiscal health is one of "managed austerity," with limited upside potential until cross-border retail friction eases.

**Recommendation for Stakeholders:**

Investors and policymakers should treat the border not as a single credit block but as distinct micro-economies. The "Texas Triangle" of municipal strength has effectively extended south to include the Rio Grande Valley, while the western corridor faces the headwinds of maturity, saturation, and structural imbalance. Monitoring El Paso's FY 2026 budget for structural fixes and Pharr's fund balance restoration efforts will be the critical watchpoints for the coming year.
