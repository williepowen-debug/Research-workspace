The international boundary between the United States and Mexico serves not merely as a jurisdictional divide but as the central nervous system for one of the most productive and complex economic corridors in the world. Within the California-Baja California megaregion, specifically the Cali Baja ecosystem, the structural interdependence of San Diego, Imperial County, Tijuana, and Mexicali has created a "co-production" model that defines the competitive posture of North American manufacturing and services. As of early 2026, this region is navigating a volatile period characterized by shifting federal trade policies, acute housing supply shortages, and a widening divergence in economic performance when compared to the Texas-Mexico border corridor. This report examines the economic structure, infrastructure capacity, and systemic stress signals of the California border region, utilizing the Texas border experience as a critical benchmark for regional competitiveness.

## **I. Economic Structure and Demographic Trajectories**

The Cali Baja region, encompassing San Diego County, Imperial County, and the state of Baja California, possesses a combined Gross Domestic Product (GDP) estimated between $250 billion and $300 billion.1 This economic mass is predicated on a functional division of labor: a "knowledge economy" anchored in San Diego and a high-scale "manufacturing and logistics economy" centered in Tijuana and Mexicali.2

### **1.1 Population Dynamics and Urbanization Trends**

The demographic profile reflects broader shifts in American and Mexican migration patterns. San Diego’s metropolitan population reached 3,345,000 in 2024 and is projected to climb to 3,402,000 by 2026, a steady growth rate of approximately 0.8% annually.3 However, this growth masks a significant underlying stress signal: California has experienced a sustained period of negative net domestic migration, losing over 216,000 residents to other states in the fiscal year ending July 1, 2025\.4

In contrast, the Mexican side of the border remains a magnet for internal migration. Mexicali’s population reached approximately 1,215,000 in 2025, with an annual growth rate nearing 1.6%.5 This creates a unique "transfronterizo" workforce—individuals who live in the relatively more affordable housing markets of Baja California but commute to Southern California for employment.1

### **1.2 Major Employers and Industry Clusters**

San Diego’s economy is defined by its science and technology sectors, including biotechnology (Novartis, Illumina), telecommunications (Qualcomm), and defense. This "innovation ecosystem" ranks second in the United States for the number of patents received.10 In Tijuana and Mexicali, the focus shifts toward advanced manufacturing, particularly in medical devices, aerospace (Boeing, Honeywell), and electronics (Samsung, Foxconn).

### **1.3 Cross-Border Dependency and the Maquiladora Integration**

The economic dependency is absolute. Nearly 97% of the $34.5 billion in goods exported from San Diego and Imperial Counties are destined for Mexico, supporting roughly 95,000 local jobs in sectors such as semiconductors and medical devices.2 Manufacturing remains the backbone of Baja California's expansion, with the Manufacturing, Maquiladora and Export Services Industry Program (IMMEX) revenues rising from 3.38 trillion pesos in January 2024 to 4.55 trillion pesos in January 2025\.11

## **II. Ports of Entry: The Logistics of the Megaregion**

The California-Mexico border facilities are the physical bottlenecks of the binational economy.

### **2.1 Crossing Volumes and Infrastructure Capacity**

San Ysidro remains the largest land border crossing in the Western Hemisphere, processing 14,829,472 personal vehicles in 2024, a 6.4% decline from 2023\.12 In contrast, commercial hubs like Otay Mesa and Calexico East have seen growth or stability. Otay Mesa processed 1,059,759 trucks in 2024, a 2.5% increase, while personal vehicle crossings at the same port surged 13.2%.12

### **2.2 Wait Times and Operational Delays**

Operational issues are severe. At San Ysidro, wait times for standard lanes frequently reach 150 minutes.13 These delays result in an estimated annual loss of $3.4 billion in economic output across the region.14 Approximately 70% of the economic output loss in San Diego County is related specifically to delays in personal trips, which includes retail shoppers.15

## **III. Retail and Tourism: The Binational Consumer**

### **3.1 Mexican Shopper Impact**

Retail is the employment sector most impacted by border delays, accounting for 85% of total job and output losses in San Diego County.15 While specific "shopper share" percentages vary by city, research shows that households in Northern Mexico with a cross-border worker spend 283% more in the United States than those without one.6 The suspension of the $800 "de minimis" exemption in August 2025 has further disrupted this flow, causing significant challenges for small businesses and e-commerce fulfillment centers in the region.16

### **3.2 Medical Tourism**

Tijuana has emerged as the number-one medical destination in Mexico for international patients, attracting more than one million medical and wellness visitors annually. Patients, primarily from the U.S. and Canada, seek care in dental, bariatric, and cosmetic procedures, typically saving 40% to 70% compared to U.S. costs. This sector generates significant revenue for local hotels and specialized transportation services.

## **IV. Labor Market Dynamics**

### **4.1 Imperial County Unemployment and Structure**

Imperial County faces persistent labor market distress. The unemployment rate in El Centro was recorded at 20.0% in November 2025, compared to 5.5% for California as a whole.17 In the city of Calexico, the rate reached a staggering 31.2% in late 2025\.19

* **Agricultural Employment:** Agriculture remains a pillar, directly employing 14,537 people in 2022 and accounting for one in six jobs in the county.  
* **Government Dominance:** Government is the largest employer in the El Centro MSA, providing approximately 21,400 jobs, or over 35% of the total nonfarm workforce.18

### **4.2 Cross-Border Workforce**

The number of transborder commuters has faced recent downward pressure. Baja California residents who commute regularly to work in Southern California fell by 20% between early 2024 and early 2025, dropping to 70,642 in Q1 2025\.20 This decline is attributed to a tougher immigration environment and rising commute times.20

## **V. Fiscal Health and Stress Signals**

### **5.1 Imperial County Credit and Budget**

In June 2025, S\&P Global Ratings downgraded Imperial County's credit rating from A- to BBB, citing weak financial performance, negative available reserves, and management risks related to audit delays and leadership turnover. The county’s 2025-2026 budget of $755.8 million represents a marginal 1% increase, reflecting high fiscal pressure and a reliance on general fund support for enterprise funds like the local landfill.21

### **5.2 Calexico and San Diego Municipal Health**

* **Calexico:** The City Council initially failed to pass its 2025-2026 budget in June due to concerns over water rate hikes, before finally approving a $160 million plan on June 30, 2025\.22 The budget projects flat sales tax revenue, prompting a deferral of cost-of-living adjustments for staff.22  
* **San Diego:** While the city benefits from rising household incomes, it faces significant exposure to state-level budget deficits, which are projected to reach $18 billion in 2026-2027.24

## **VI. Real Estate: Affordability vs. Industrial Expansion**

### **6.1 Housing Market Divergence**

California possesses some of the most expensive housing in the U.S., with San Diego's home price-to-income ratio at 9.6.25

* **San Diego:** Median home prices crossed the $1 million threshold in 2025\.10  
* **Imperial County:** While more affordable than San Diego, the median home price of $457,000 still represents a significant burden, at 7.32 times the median household income.

### **6.2 Industrial and Logistics Growth**

The industrial market in Otay Mesa has expanded by over 50% since 2019, reaching 24.4 million square feet in 2024 to support nearshoring demand.26 Similarly, Mexicali is emerging as a hub for advanced manufacturing, with 14 new company arrivals or expansions in the first half of 2025 alone.27

## **VII. Systemic Stress Signals**

### **7.1 Retail Closures and Business Stress**

California leads the nation in big-chain retail exits and layoffs. San Diego has been identified as a hotspot for shutdown activity, with national chains like GameStop, Walgreens, and Foot Locker announcing significant store closures in 2025\. San Ysidro’s retail sector remains particularly fragile, having lost over 2,000 jobs and 200 businesses since the pandemic began.

### **7.2 Wildfire Insurance Crisis**

San Diego is one of the highest-risk metropolitan areas for wildfires in the Western U.S.. As private insurers retreat, the California FAIR Plan has seen its policy count more than double since 2020 to over 452,000. For San Diego homeowners, this has led to steep premium hikes—sometimes quadrupling—and decreased property values in high-risk zones.

## **VIII. Comparative Analysis: California vs. Texas**

### **8.1 Scale and Diversification**

San Diego’s economy is significantly more diversified into high-tech and "knowledge" sectors (biotech, aerospace, semiconductors) than traditional Texas border hubs.1 While Port Laredo handles a superior volume of trade (35% of all U.S.-Mexico trade) and processes 20,000 trucks daily, its economy is more acutely vulnerable to trade policy shocks.

### **8.2 Policy and Regulatory Environments**

Texas utilizes a "low regulation/low tax" model that has fostered 6.8% GDP growth in 2025, compared to 1.2% in California.17

* **Development Timelines:** A typical multifamily development takes 49 months in California versus 27 months in Texas.28  
* **Infrastructure Investment:** The Texas Department of Transportation has allocated over $1.5 billion for highway expansions in the Laredo region alone, a level of per-capita investment that exceeds most California border initiatives.29

## **IX. Conclusion: The 2026 Horizon**

The California border region enters 2026 in a state of "cautious optimism" tempered by severe structural stressors. While San Diego and Tijuana continue to lead in high-value co-production, the region's fiscal health—particularly in Imperial County—is under threat from credit downgrades and high unemployment. The wildfire insurance crisis and the ongoing housing supply shortage represent systemic risks that could accelerate the outmigration of the workforce to more affordable regions like the Texas-Mexico border. Strategic focus on municipal fiscal stabilization and the modernization of ports like Otay Mesa East remains essential for the region to maintain its competitive advantage in the North American supply chain.
