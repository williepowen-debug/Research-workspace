# **Strategic Reorientation of the Canadian Tourism Economy: An Analysis of Domestic Travel Substitution and the 2025–2026 Structural Pivot**

The Canadian tourism sector in 2025 has reached a definitive inflection point, characterized by a fundamental restructuring of consumer demand and capital flow. As the global travel landscape grapples with shifting geopolitical alliances and economic volatility, Canada has emerged as a primary beneficiary of its own internal market. The phenomenon of domestic travel substitution—the deliberate redirection of discretionary travel spending from international destinations, predominantly the United States, toward the domestic market—has evolved from a emergent trend into a foundational pillar of national economic resilience.1 This analysis examines the quantitative and qualitative dimensions of this shift, utilizing the latest data from Destination Canada, Statistics Canada, and various survey instruments to chart the sector’s performance through the third and fourth quarters of 2025 and into the 2026 horizon.

## **Macroeconomic Performance and Quarterly Domestic Consumption**

The fiscal year 2025 was marked by the tourism sector consistently outperforming the broader Canadian economy. In the third quarter of 2025, tourism gross domestic product (GDP) grew by 0.9% in real terms, matching the pace established in the second quarter. This performance is particularly significant when contrasted with the economy-wide real GDP, which grew by 0.5% in the third quarter following a decline of 0.2% in the preceding period.1 By the end of September 2025, the tourism sector’s share of nominal GDP stood at 1.70%, reflecting its status as a critical engine of economic activity during a period of cross-border uncertainty.4

Domestic tourism revenue has been the primary driver of this growth. Year-to-date figures for the third quarter of 2025 indicate that domestic tourism revenue reached $80.1 billion, representing 129% of the levels recorded in the pre-pandemic baseline of 2019\.6 This volume indicates a 3% year-over-year increase, sustained despite a tightening of general household discretionary spending.6 The quarterly progression of domestic spending highlights the seasonal strength of the substitution effect.

| Q1 2025 | $13.8 | \+4.4% | Withdrawal from US travel begins; surge in domestic winter recreation.7 |

In the second quarter of 2025, Canadian residents took approximately 99.3 million total trips, with 90.6 million of those involving a domestic visit—an 10.9% increase from the previous year.10 Expenditures for these domestic visits reached $20.3 billion in Q2 alone, marking the highest spending recorded for any second quarter since the inception of the National Travel Survey in 2018\.9 By the third quarter, while the rate of growth moderated slightly to 0.5% on a quarterly basis, the absolute volume remained historically high at $20.8 billion, fueled by a record-breaking summer season where total tourism revenue between May and August approached $60 billion.1

The fourth quarter of 2025 is projected to maintain this momentum, albeit with a seasonal shift toward urban centers and holiday-related travel. Preliminary estimates suggest that domestic spending will continue to hover above the $20 billion mark per quarter, as the "Elbows Up" sentiment and favorable domestic infrastructure continue to discourage outbound travel to the United States.13

## **The Mechanics of Substitution: Sentiment, Sovereignty, and Spending**

The redirection of Canadian travel dollars is not merely an economic coincidence but a calculated behavioral shift driven by a combination of political backlash, currency fluctuations, and targeted domestic incentives. The "rupture" in Canada-U.S. travel relations, as evidenced by the "free fall" of Canadian visits to the U.S. in mid-to-late 2025, serves as the primary catalyst for domestic substitution.2

### **Geopolitical Friction and the Boycott Movement**

The catalyst for the 2025 travel shift was the reinflammation of trade tensions and provocative rhetoric from the U.S. administration. Comments regarding the potential annexation of Canada or its characterization as a "51st state" struck a significant nerve across the Canadian populace.16 This resulted in a nationwide movement to "vote with the wallet," manifesting in organized boycotts of American goods and destinations.

Survey data throughout 2025 confirms the depth of this sentiment. In February, an Angus Reid survey indicated that 48% of Canadians were "seriously likely" to cancel or delay plans to travel to the U.S..18 By the third quarter of 2025, the Canadian Survey of Consumer Expectations reported that 53.1% of Canadians planned to spend less while vacationing in the United States, while 33.6% specifically planned to increase their spending within Canada.1

| Survey Instrument | Date | Finding | Behavioral Outcome |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Angus Reid | Feb 2025 | 48% likely to cancel US travel | Immediate drop in cross-border air bookings (-40%).18 |
| Longwoods International | April 2025 | 60% less likely to travel to US due to policy | 36% had already canceled 2025 plans.16 |
| Leger Survey | Late 2025 | 66% reduced US product purchases | Spillover effect into tourism and duty-free retail.16 |
| StatCan Consumer Expectations | Q3 2025 | 53.1% plan to spend less in US | Corresponding 0.5% quarterly domestic growth.1 |

The tangible impact of this sentiment was most visible in border crossings and airline capacity. Canadian return trips from the U.S. by automobile fell by 23% in February and by a staggering 34% in August 2025 compared to the previous year.2 Major carriers, including Air Canada, WestJet, and Flair, were compelled to cut U.S. routes or curb capacity as demand plummeted.2

### **Economic and Infrastructure Drivers**

While political sentiment provided the motivation, economic realities and government policy provided the means for domestic substitution. The depreciation of the Canadian dollar against the U.S. dollar served as a natural barrier to international travel, making domestic options significantly more cost-effective.17 Furthermore, the "Canada Strong" pass, which was valid from June 20 to September 2, 2025, acted as a powerful incentive for regional exploration. This initiative contributed to a 3.9% increase in passenger rail transport in the third quarter, as Canadians took advantage of subsidized access to national parks and museums.1

The result was a "national economic contribution" where the summer of 2025 saw 89% of Canadian regions posting year-over-year growth.12 This successful dispersion of tourism suggests that the domestic market is increasingly capable of absorbing demand that was previously exported to sun-belt states or American metropolitan hubs.

## **Regional Performance: Provincial Leaders in the Substitution Surge**

The benefits of domestic substitution were felt most acutely in provinces that offered strong "backyard" alternatives to traditional American vacation spots. Atlantic Canada and Western Canada emerged as the dominant performers in 2025, while border-adjacent regions in Central Canada faced a more complex transition.

### **The Atlantic Performance**

Atlantic Canada posted some of the highest growth rates in the country during the 2025 season.12 Prince Edward Island (PEI) was a standout leader, recording a 97.2% year-over-year increase in spending by travelers originating from the province in the second quarter of 2025\.9 PEI also maintained the highest expenditure per night in the country at $184, significantly outperforming the national average of $140.9 This suggests that the substitution effect is not just moving volume but is attracting high-yield travelers who were previously spending their "luxury" budgets abroad.

### **Central and Western Dynamics**

British Columbia saw a 35.2% increase in spending in Q2 2025, as residents chose local resort properties over destinations like California and Hawaii.9 In contrast, provinces with high reliance on cross-border automobile traffic, such as Ontario, faced localized challenges. While Ontario remained the most visited province, many of its border communities suffered from the 80% drop in duty-free sales and the collapse of same-day cross-border excursions.2

However, the domestic pivot eventually stabilized these regions as well. By the end of the summer, 59% of rural and smaller metro regions across Canada had outperformed the average growth of major metro areas, signaling a successful geographic dispersion of tourism spending.12

## **Sectoral Shifts and Workforce Resilience**

The structural shift in travel patterns has necessitated a corresponding adjustment in the hospitality and transportation sectors. The accommodation sector, in particular, demonstrated remarkable strength throughout the 2025 season.

### **Record Accommodation Strength**

National hotel occupancy reached 80.7% in August 2025, the highest level recorded since 2014\.12 This surge in demand occurred despite an expanded supply of both traditional hotels and short-term rentals, indicating that the volume of domestic travelers was more than sufficient to absorb new capacity. Revenue Per Available Room (RevPAR) for Canadian hotels rose by 7.6% overall during the summer, reflecting higher yields from domestic travelers who were staying longer and spending more per trip.12

In the third quarter of 2025, growth in tourism GDP was primarily driven by accommodation (+1.2%) and transportation (+1.2%).1 The transportation sector saw a notable divergence: while rail travel surged, passenger air transport declined by 1.6% due to the aforementioned route cancellations and industrial action in August.1

### **Labor Market Stability**

The tourism industry has maintained its role as a vital employer, supporting approximately 2.26 million people in the labor force as of October 2025\.21 Tourism-related employment increased by 0.6% in the third quarter, a period during which the broader Canadian economy saw a 0.3% decline in total employment.4 This resilience highlights the sector's importance as a counter-cyclical stabilizer.

| Tourism Industry Group | YoY Workforce Growth (Oct 2025\) | Performance Relative to 2019 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Accommodations | \+22.9% | Strong summer gains; seasonal autumn cooling.21 |
| Recreation & Entertainment | \+2.7% | Surpassed pre-pandemic baseline levels.21 |
| Transportation | \+4.6% | Driven by rail and regional vehicle rentals.21 |
| Food & Beverage | \-1.4% | Slightly below 2024; labor losses in September reversed.21 |

The decline in the travel services workforce (-14.7%) is a direct reflection of the domestic substitution trend. As Canadians move away from complex international itineraries—which often require travel agents and specialized services—and toward simpler, self-managed domestic road and rail trips, the demand for traditional travel intermediaries has softened.21

## **The 2026 Horizon: Projections and Strategic Transformation**

The outlook for 2026 is exceptionally positive, with experts anticipating that Canada is "set to break tourism records".23 The transition from 2025 to 2026 is expected to be defined by the stabilization of domestic habits and the gradual recovery of high-value international markets outside of the United States.

### **Catalysts for 2026 Growth**

A major driver for the 2026 season will be the expansion of airline services and international connectivity. Air Canada has announced plans to launch new direct routes from major international markets and expand U.S.-Canada connectivity in the spring of 2026, assuming a moderation of current trade tensions.23 This expansion is expected to boost demand from Europe and Asia, complementing the robust domestic base established in 2025\.

Furthermore, infrastructure projects and digital advancements, such as the deployment of Machine Learning-powered forecasts and the "Aurora AI" search tool, are expected to improve the efficiency and appeal of the Canadian tourism product.6 Destination Canada’s "Transformation" scenario, which aims to realize a $160-billion revenue potential by 2030, identifies seven key levers for growth, including workforce development and the promotion of travel outside of peak seasons.25

### **Future Risks and Strategic Imperatives**

While the domestic substitution trend has provided a significant buffer, the industry must remain vigilant against emerging risks. The WTTC has warned that "inbound growth from key markets remains delicate" and that the deep reliance on the U.S. market remains a vulnerability.27 To protect the momentum of 2025, the sector must continue to invest in "smart marketing, frictionless access, and visitor experience".27

The "Elbows Up" campaign and the resulting domestic pivot have demonstrated the power of the Canadian internal market. However, for this to be a permanent structural gain, the industry must address capacity constraints—such as limited seasonal accommodation and labor availability—that could otherwise cap the sector’s growth potential at $140 billion.25

## **Conclusion: Synthesis of the Domestic Substitution Era**

The data from the third and fourth quarters of 2025 confirms that domestic travel substitution has transitioned from a reactive consumer movement into a structural economic reality. With domestic tourism revenue surpassing $80 billion YTD in Q3 and the summer of 2025 breaking all-time records, the sector has proven its resilience in the face of unprecedented geopolitical pressure.6 The movement of capital from outbound U.S. travel to domestic destinations has revitalized Atlantic Canada, supported a robust accommodation sector, and maintained employment levels even as the broader economy wavered.

As Canada enters 2026, the strategic imperative is to convert the "political tourists" of 2025 into "loyal domestic advocates." By addressing the capacity constraints identified by Destination Canada and capitalizing on expanded international connectivity, the sector is well-positioned to reach its $160-billion potential. The 2025 pivot was driven by discontent, but the 2026 trajectory will be defined by the quality and diversity of the Canadian tourism experience.23 The "rupture" with the traditional cross-border travel model has ultimately catalyzed a more self-reliant, geographically dispersed, and economically significant national tourism industry.2
