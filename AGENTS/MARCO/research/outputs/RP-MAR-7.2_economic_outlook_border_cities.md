# **US-Mexico Border Economic Outlook 2025: Structural Divergence in the North American Trade Corridor**

## **Executive Summary**

The economic architecture of the United States-Mexico border region is currently navigating a period of profound structural transformation, characterized in the 2024-2025 fiscal cycle by a phenomenon of "Great Divergence." While the region remains the indispensable artery of North American commerce—facilitating over $800 billion in annual trade—the localized economic benefits of this integration are shifting dramatically. The historic reliance on the "Mexican Shopper" to sustain municipal tax bases and downtown retail districts is collapsing across most of the Texas frontier, driven by the maturation of Mexico’s domestic retail sector, a hardened security posture, and volatile currency dynamics. In its place, a new economic order is emerging: one anchored in advanced logistics, aerospace, energy exports, and integrated industrial supply chains.

This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the economic structures and cross-border dependencies of six critical border municipalities: the primary Texas hubs of Laredo, El Paso, McAllen, and Brownsville, and the secondary Arizona nodes of Nogales and Yuma. The analysis reveals that while industrial real estate and transportation sectors are overheating with demand—driven by nearshoring and the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA)—the commercial retail sectors are facing an existential crisis. In 2025, cities like Laredo and Brownsville have seen their cross-border retail share plummet to historic lows of 13% and 3.3% respectively, necessitating painful urban restructuring. Conversely, McAllen remains a unique outlier, retaining a high dependency on cross-border consumption that exposes it to acute risks from tariff uncertainties and peso volatility.

Current stress signals observed in late 2024 and throughout 2025 include a wave of national retail bankruptcies (Big Lots, JoAnn Fabrics) that have disproportionately impacted border strip malls, a crackdown on regulatory compliance in entertainment districts, and the looming specter of tariff renegotiations in 2026\. This report synthesizes these trends to offer a detailed portrait of a region in transition, moving from a marketplace of goods to a corridor of industrial velocity.

## ---

**1\. Macroeconomic Context: The 2025 Border Environment**

To understand the specific dynamics of the six target cities, one must first contextualize the broader macroeconomic and geopolitical environment governing the US-Mexico border in 2025\. The region is currently operating under a unique set of pressures that are decoupling trade volumes from local prosperity in unprecedented ways.

### **1.1 The Nearshoring Industrial Boom**

The most significant positive driver for the border economy is the acceleration of "nearshoring." As global supply chains decouple from Asia due to geopolitical friction and tariff regimes, manufacturing capacity has flooded into Northern Mexico. This has created a "manufacturing symbiosis" where the Mexican side (Ciudad Juárez, Reynosa, Nuevo Laredo) handles assembly, while the U.S. side (El Paso, McAllen, Laredo) manages logistics, warehousing, financing, and high-value component supply.1

In 2025, this trend is visible in the industrial real estate markets of border cities, which are reporting vacancy rates significantly below the national average. For instance, Laredo’s industrial vacancy rate tightened to 3.8% in Q1 2025, driven by the need for distribution centers that can process goods flowing north from Mexican factories.3 This industrial boom is insulating border labor markets from the broader cooling of the U.S. economy, with Texas border cities often outperforming the state's job growth metrics.4

### **1.2 The Collapse of the Retail Export Model**

Historically, U.S. border cities functioned as the "shopping malls of Mexico." In the late 20th century, Mexican nationals accounted for 40-50% of retail sales in cities like Laredo and Brownsville. In 2025, this model is effectively dead for most of the border. The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas reports a structural decline in exported retail sales share across the board, attributed to three primary factors:

1. **Domestic Competition in Mexico:** The aggressive expansion of U.S. retailers (Walmart, Costco, Amazon) within Mexico has reduced the necessity for cross-border shopping trips.5  
2. **Border Friction:** Increased wait times and security protocols have turned casual shopping trips into logistical ordeals, deterring discretionary travel.6  
3. **Economic Parity:** Rising wages in Northern Mexico have fueled domestic consumption *within* Mexico, rather than leakage across the border.7

### **1.3 Policy Volatility: Tariffs and Immigration**

The 2025 economic landscape is clouded by uncertainty regarding U.S. trade policy. The threat of new tariffs—specifically a potential universal tariff or targeted enforcement on Mexican imports—has frozen some capital investments and created anxiety in the agricultural sectors of Nogales and Yuma.8 Furthermore, intensified immigration enforcement, including workplace raids reported in the Rio Grande Valley in 2025, has introduced a labor supply shock, causing shortages in construction and service industries while dampening local consumer confidence.10

**2\. Primary City Analysis: Texas**

The Texas border region, stretching from the high desert of El Paso to the tropical delta of Brownsville, represents the demographic and economic core of the borderlands.

### **2.1 Laredo, Texas: The Logistics Sovereign**

Laredo stands as the singular most important inland port in the Western Hemisphere. Its economy is a monoculture of trade, defined by the relentless flow of trucks across the World Trade Bridge. In 2025, Laredo is a city of stark contrasts: a booming industrial periphery surrounding a hollowing historic downtown.

#### **2.1.1 Economic Profile**

**Population Dynamics:** Laredo’s metropolitan demographics reflect steady, albeit moderate, growth. Estimates for 2025 place the population between 263,619 and 272,823, with an annual growth rate hovering around 0.9% to 1.45%.11 The population is exceptionally young, with a median age of 29.7 years, significantly lower than the national average.13 This youth bulge provides a steady stream of entry-level labor for the logistics sector, though retention of skilled professionals remains a challenge.

**Labor Market & Employment:** The labor market in Laredo is tight and resilient. In April 2025, the civilian labor force expanded to over 122,000, with total employment rising to 116,693.14 The unemployment rate was recorded at 4.5%, slightly above the Texas average of 3.7% but historically low for the region. This 4.5% figure represents "frictional unemployment" as workers churn between logistics contracts rather than structural joblessness.14

* **Key Employers:** The "Trade, Transportation, and Utilities" sector is the dominant employer. Major corporate entities include H-E-B (retail/grocery), Laredo Medical Center (healthcare), and Concentrix (business services). Crucially, the logistics sector is anchored by FedEx Freight and a vast network of customs brokers and freight forwarders. The financial sector is robust, led by the International Bank of Commerce (IBC), which is headquartered in Laredo and serves as a primary financier of cross-border trade.15

**Income & Wealth:** Despite high employment, income levels lag. The median household income in 2023 was approximately $63,264. While this represents growth, nearly 18.4% of families live in poverty.13 The divergence between the wealth generated by trade (billions in value) and local wage capture remains a central economic tension.

#### **2.1.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**The Logistics Monoculture:** Laredo’s dependency on cross-border flows is absolute, but it has shifted from *people* to *freight*. In 2024, Port Laredo handled $339 billion in trade, ranking it as the number one port in the United States, surpassing the maritime giants of Los Angeles and Chicago.17 The city handles an estimated 17.6 to 19.2 million TEUs annually. The local economy is synchronized with the World Trade Bridge; when traffic flows, Laredo earns.

**The Retail Collapse:**

The most critical structural change in Laredo is the collapse of retail dependency.

* **Historic Share (1978-2001):** Mexican shoppers accounted for **51.1%** of all retail sales in Laredo. The city was effectively a duty-free shop for Nuevo León and Tamaulipas.5  
* **Current Share (2023-2025):** This share has plummeted to **13.0%**. The implications are profound: Laredo can no longer rely on sales tax revenue from Mexican nationals to subsidize its municipal budget. The "retail export" industry has evaporated due to the rise of big-box retail in Mexico and border delays.5

**Sister City Dynamics:** Nuevo Laredo, Tamaulipas, with a population exceeding 438,000, serves as the industrial counterpart. The combined binational metro area exceeds 636,000 people.18 The relationship is functional and industrial; Nuevo Laredo manufactures and processes, while Laredo distributes. Recent coordination has focused on the expansion of the World Trade Bridge (International Bridge III) to handle increasing capacity, a project that saw renewed binational meetings in late 2025\.19

#### **2.1.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Downtown Vacancy Crisis:** The evaporation of the Mexican shopper has devastated Laredo’s downtown. In 2025, the vacancy rate for downtown buildings hit a staggering **32%**, with commercial unit vacancy reaching **42.85%**.20 Historic buildings, once generating premium rents from perfume and electronics merchants catering to cross-border foot traffic, now sit empty. This has forced the city to launch a "Downtown Business Improvement District" and draft new "vacant building ordinances" in late 2025 to compel property owners to rehabilitate or sell.21

**Retail Insolvencies:** Laredo is bearing the brunt of the 2025 national retail contraction. The bankruptcy of **Big Lots** resulted in the closure of two Laredo locations, adding to the inventory of vacant commercial space.22 This follows the closure of **JoAnn Fabrics** and **Party City** earlier in the cycle.23 These closures are not merely cyclical; they represent a permanent rightsizing of a retail footprint that was built for a cross-border consumer base that no longer exists.

**Industrial Overheating:** Conversely, the stress signal in the industrial sector is a lack of supply. With vacancy rates at 3.8% and a pipeline of 3.2 million square feet under construction, developers are racing to catch up with demand.3 The stress here is infrastructural: roads, bridges, and utilities are straining under the volume of truck traffic, creating bottlenecks that threaten the efficiency of the port.

**2.2 El Paso, Texas: The Borderplex Titan**

El Paso is the most mature and diversified economy on the Texas border. Unlike Laredo’s singular focus on trade, El Paso has developed a robust internal economy anchored by military, medical, and educational institutions, shielding it from some—but not all—of the volatility of 2025\.

#### **2.2.1 Economic Profile**

**Population & Demographics:** El Paso is a true metropolis. The metro area population crossed the **1 million** threshold in 2025 projections (approx. 1,006,000 to 1,017,000).24 This scale provides a critical mass for services and amenities that smaller border cities lack.

**Employment Landscape:**

The labor market is robust, reaching record employment levels in 2025\.

* **Unemployment:** The rate stood at 4.5% in November 2025, consistent with the border region's tighter labor conditions.25  
* **Key Employers:** The economy is buttressed by **Fort Bliss**, one of the largest military installations in the U.S., which pumps billions into the local service economy. **The University of Texas at El Paso (UTEP)** and **Texas Tech Health Sciences Center** drive a growing education and healthcare sector. The call center and business service industry remains a major employer, with companies like **ADP** and **Datamark** leveraging the bilingual workforce.26

#### **2.2.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**The Manufacturing Symbiosis:**

El Paso’s dependency is inextricably linked to the *maquiladora* industry of Ciudad Juárez. While El Paso provides the white-collar support (legal, financial, logistics), Juárez provides the blue-collar assembly.

* **Job Divergence (2025):** A notable trend in 2025 is the divergence in employment growth between the sister cities. While El Paso gained **7,200 jobs** (+2.1% YoY) in early 2025, Ciudad Juárez *lost* **10,600 jobs** (-2.1% YoY), marking 19 consecutive months of decline in the formal manufacturing sector.1 This signal suggests a potential softening or restructuring in the Juárez factory sector, possibly due to automation or labor cost shifts, which poses a long-term risk to El Paso’s service providers.

**Retail Independence:**

El Paso has successfully weaned itself off reliance on the Mexican shopper.

* **Retail Share:** In the historic period (1978-2001), Mexican nationals accounted for **11.3%** of retail sales. By 2023-2025, this fell to **7.4%**.5 This indicates that El Paso’s retail sector is primarily fueled by local residents and the Fort Bliss population, making it more resilient to border closures than McAllen or Laredo.

#### **2.2.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Downtown Retail Distress:** Despite overall resilience, the downtown retail core—which specifically targets pedestrian crossers from Juárez—is suffering. In 2025, merchants reported plummeting sales due to reduced bridge traffic and long wait times. Iconic establishments like **Chihuahua Charlie’s** and **Matteo’s Mexican Food** announced closures in early 2025, citing "rising expenses and falling sales".28 This micro-economy of downtown El Paso is decoupling from the broader, booming city economy.

**Political & Social Friction:** El Paso remains the epicenter of the political debate over immigration. In 2025, a "National General Strike" protesting immigration enforcement saw significant participation from local businesses, some of which closed in solidarity. This event highlights the social precarity of the region; business continuity is often subject to the political climate regarding the border.30

**Chain Contraction:** El Paso was hit heavily by the **Big Lots** restructuring, with **four** locations identified for closure in 2025\.22 This reflects the broader weakness of the discount retail segment in the face of inflation, rather than a specifically border-related failure, but it adds to the inventory of vacant commercial space.

**2.3 McAllen (Hidalgo/Pharr), Texas: The Retail Outlier**

McAllen presents the most unique economic profile among the border cities. It acts as the "shopping mall" for Monterrey, Mexico’s industrial and financial capital. This relationship has made McAllen wealthy but exceptionally vulnerable.

#### **2.3.1 Economic Profile**

**Population Growth:** The McAllen-Edinburg-Mission metro area is the demographic powerhouse of the Rio Grande Valley. The 2025 population estimate is approximately **1.13 million**, growing at a robust rate of 1.34%.31 The region is urbanizing rapidly, transforming from agricultural towns into a continuous metropolitan corridor.

**Labor Market:**

* **Unemployment:** McAllen consistently runs a higher unemployment rate than the state average, hovering around **6.1% \- 6.2%** in 2025\.32 This structural unemployment persists despite job growth, largely due to a rapidly expanding labor force that outpaces job creation.  
* **Key Employers:** The economy is a dual engine of Healthcare and Retail. **DHR Health** and the **McAllen Medical Center** are massive employers, serving not just locals but "medical tourists" from across the region. The **retail sector** employs a disproportionate share of the workforce compared to national averages.33

#### **2.3.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**The Monterrey Lifeline:**

McAllen is the anomaly in the "retail collapse" narrative.

* **Retail Export Share:** While other cities have seen their Mexican retail share evaporate, McAllen retains a massive **28.2%** share (down from a historic 35.6%, but still dominant). In 2023, McAllen "exported" **$4.9 billion** in retail sales to Mexican citizens.5  
* **Mechanism:** This resilience is due to geography. McAllen is the closest U.S. metro to Monterrey (approx. 2.5 hours away). Wealthy *Regiomontanos* (residents of Monterrey) continue to utilize McAllen for high-end shopping, entertainment, and banking, insulating the city from the decline seen in Laredo.5

**Industrial Growth:** While retail dominates, McAllen is diversifying. The McAllen Economic Development Corporation reported **1,000 new industrial jobs** in the pipeline for 2025, focused on the produce sector and light manufacturing, supported by the Pharr Bridge’s dominance in produce importation.35

#### **2.3.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Regulatory Crackdown on Nightlife:** In a significant stress signal for the local service economy, the City of McAllen launched an aggressive crackdown on downtown entertainment venues in 2025\. The city **revoked Special Use Permits** and **cut power** to over a dozen businesses operating without proper certificates of occupancy or violating codes. This enforcement action disrupted the "17th Street" entertainment district, a key draw for visitors, forcing closures and creating friction between business owners and the municipality.36

**Retail Exposure Risks:** Because McAllen is so dependent on retail, national closures hit harder here. **Big Lots** announced closures in McAllen, Edinburg, and Weslaco in 2025\.22 Furthermore, any fluctuation in the Mexican Peso (which devalued in early 2025 due to tariff threats) has an immediate, multiplier effect on McAllen’s sales tax receipts.

**Immigration Enforcement Impact:** Local mayors in the RGV have publicly cited **ICE workplace raids** in 2025 as a major economic disruptor. These raids have caused labor shortages in construction and hospitality and have created a "climate of fear" that keeps undocumented residents (a significant consumer base) at home, further depressing local retail activity.10

**2.4 Brownsville, Texas: The Space & Energy Frontier**

Brownsville is undergoing the most radical identity shift of any border city. Historically one of the poorest cities in the U.S., it is pivoting aggressively toward high-tech aerospace and energy exports.

#### **2.4.1 Economic Profile**

**Population:** The city population is estimated at **190,000 to 193,000** in 2025, with steady growth driven by industrial migration.38

**Employment & Wages:**

* **Unemployment:** Brownsville historically struggles with high unemployment, recording rates between **6.4% and 6.9%** in 2025\.40  
* **Wage Growth:** However, a remarkable trend is visible: **Average hourly earnings grew by 12.0%** year-over-year in early 2025\.32 This wage spike is attributed to the entry of high-paying employers like SpaceX and LNG terminals, which are forcing up wages across the local labor market to compete for talent.  
* **Key Employers:** **SpaceX (Starbase)** is the transformative entity. Other major employers include the **Port of Brownsville**, **Keppel AmFELS** (offshore rig manufacturing), and the **Brownsville Independent School District**.41

#### **2.4.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**The End of the Retail Era:**

Brownsville has effectively ceased to be a retail destination for Mexico.

* **Retail Share:** The share of retail sales to Mexican nationals collapsed from a historic **25.7%** to a negligible **3.3%** in 2023-2025.5 The city of Matamoros no longer shops in Brownsville; instead, the economic relationship is now purely industrial.

**Port-Based Integration:**

The dependency has shifted to the **Port of Brownsville**, which serves as a crucial hub for steel, fuel, and wind energy components moving into Northern Mexico. The integration is logistical rather than commercial.

#### **2.4.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Infrastructure Growing Pains:** The rapid industrialization has strained local infrastructure. In 2025, extensive downtown **water and wastewater upgrades** caused significant road closures and detours, disrupting local businesses. While necessary for the "Downtown Renaissance Master Plan," the short-term impact has been a reduction in foot traffic and business accessibility.42

**Retail Contraction:** Like its neighbors, Brownsville lost a **Big Lots** location in 2025\.22 The retail sector is shrinking to fit the local population size, shedding the excess capacity that was built for the now-absent Mexican shopper.

**3\. Secondary City Analysis: Arizona**

The Arizona border presents a different economic morphology: smaller, specialized, and heavily focused on agriculture and produce logistics.

### **3.1 Nogales, Arizona: The Produce Gateway**

Nogales is less a city and more a specialized logistics machine. It is the primary entry point for winter vegetables entering the United States.

#### **3.1.1 Economic Profile**

* **Population:** The population is stagnant, hovering around **19,700 to 19,900** in 2025\.44 It lacks the critical mass to develop a self-sustaining internal economy.  
* **Key Industries:** The economy is dominated by the **Mariposa Port of Entry**. Logistics, warehousing, and customs brokerage firms employ a significant portion of the workforce. The "Produce" cluster is the economic engine.

#### **3.1.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**The Produce Monoculture:** Nogales handles approximately **$30-$35 billion** in trade annually. The dependency on the winter harvest in Mexico is absolute; without the flow of tomatoes, squash, and berries, Nogales, AZ would cease to function economically.45

**Sales Tax Vulnerability:** Unlike the Texas cities that are diversifying, Nogales remains dangerously dependent on cross-border shoppers for municipal revenue. Estimates suggest that up to **60% of the city’s sales tax revenue** is generated by Mexican nationals.46 This makes the city budget extremely sensitive to border wait times and exchange rates.

#### **3.1.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Tariff Anxiety:** The primary stressor in 2025 is the threat of tariffs. Local trade leaders traveled to Washington D.C. in late 2025 to lobby against tariffs on Mexican produce, arguing that such costs would devastate the local logistics industry and raise prices for U.S. consumers.8 The uncertainty alone has dampened investment in new cold-storage facilities.

**Main Street Struggles:** Merchants on **Morley Avenue**, the historic retail district within walking distance of the border, report declining sales. They cite the "unwelcoming" atmosphere of hardened border enforcement and the "Trump Tariffs" rhetoric as key deterrents to their customer base.47

### ---

**3.2 Yuma, Arizona: Agriculture & Defense**

Yuma operates as a distinct economic island, driven by the dual engines of large-scale agriculture and military defense.

#### **3.2.1 Economic Profile**

* **Population:** The Yuma metro area population is robust, estimated at over **220,000** in 2025\.48  
* **Employment:** Yuma is famous for its high unemployment rate, which reached **11.3%** in late 2025\.49 However, this figure is often misleading due to the extreme seasonality of the agricultural workforce.  
* **Key Employers:** **Marine Corps Air Station (MCAS) Yuma** and the **Yuma Proving Ground** provide stability. The agricultural sector produces **$3.4 billion** in annual output, supplying 90% of the U.S. winter leafy greens.50

#### **3.2.2 Cross-Border Dependency**

**Labor Force Integration:** Yuma’s dependency is on *labor*, not shoppers. The **San Luis Port of Entry** processes over 40,000 crossings daily, the vast majority being farmworkers who live in San Luis Río Colorado (Sonora) and work in Yuma’s fields.51 The agricultural economy cannot function without this daily migration.

#### **3.2.3 Recent Stress Signals (2024-2025)**

**Federal Service Cuts:** A specific stress signal in 2025 was the **closure of a local USDA office**, which disrupted services for local farmers. This administrative contraction threatens to slow down the processing of grants and inspections necessary for the ag sector.52

**Retail Resilience:** Interestingly, Yuma’s retail sector showed resilience, with sales up **2.4%** in 2024\. Unlike the Texas border, Yuma’s retail sector was never as dependent on cross-border shoppers, relying instead on the steady incomes of the military and the seasonal "Winter Visitor" (snowbird) population.53

**4\. Synthesis: The Great Divergence**

### **4.1 Table: The Retail vs. Industrial Shift (2025 Status)**

| City | Primary Economic Driver (2025) | Retail Dependency on Mexico (Current) | Key Stress Signal (2025) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Laredo** | Logistics / Transportation | Low (13.0%) | Downtown Vacancy (32%) |
| **El Paso** | Diversified / Industrial Services | Low (7.4%) | Downtown Merchant Closures |
| **McAllen** | Retail / Healthcare | **High (28.2%)** | Regulatory Crackdown on Nightlife |
| **Brownsville** | Aerospace / Energy | Very Low (3.3%) | Infrastructure Construction Disruption |
| **Nogales** | Produce Logistics | **Very High (\~60% of Tax)** | Tariff Threats to Ag Supply Chain |
| **Yuma** | Agriculture / Defense | Low (Labor Dependent) | USDA Office Closures |

### **4.2 The Transformation of "Sister Cities"**

The relationship between U.S. and Mexican border cities has fundamentally changed.

* **From Consumerism to Production:** The era of the "Sister City" as a shared consumer market is over for most pairs (except McAllen/Reynosa). The relationship is now defined by **Production Sharing**. Laredo does not need Nuevo Laredo's shoppers, but it desperately needs Nuevo Laredo's trucks. Brownsville does not need Matamoros's pedestrians, but it relies on the steel arriving at its port for export to Matamoros's factories.  
* **The Decoupling:** This shift has decoupled local prosperity from cross-border foot traffic. A hardened border with long wait times destroys the retail economy (as seen in downtown Laredo/El Paso) but has a manageable impact on the industrial economy, provided that commercial lanes (FAST lanes, World Trade Bridge) remain fluid.

## ---

**Conclusion**

The 2025 outlook for the US-Mexico border is one of **asymmetric evolution**. The industrial and logistics sectors are thriving, insulated by the geostrategic necessity of nearshoring and the shear volume of North American trade. Laredo and Brownsville are successfully pivoting their economies to align with this reality, investing in bridges, ports, and aerospace infrastructure.

However, the legacy commercial structures of the border—the downtown retail districts, the duty-free perfume shops, the pedestrian malls—are in terminal decline. The "Great Divergence" means that cities like McAllen and Nogales, which still rely on the old model of cross-border consumption, are facing the highest risks from the policy volatility of 2025\. For the rest of the region, the economic future lies not in the *register*, but in the *warehouse*. The transition is painful, visible in the shuttered storefronts of downtown El Paso and Laredo, but it is forging a border economy that is more integrated into the global industrial machine than ever before.
