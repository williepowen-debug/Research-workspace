## 1. ⚠️ The headline correction: you pointed me at the wrong dam

Your packet framed the risk on **Powell/Glen Canyon at minimum power pool 3,490 ft, "~32 ft" of margin.** That is correct as stated and it is *not* the binding constraint. Two corrections, both from TA-15:

**(a) Glen Canyon does NOT stop generating at 3,490 ft.** Your line — *"below that, Glen Canyon Dam stops generating"* — is off by a step. TA-15: max physical capacity **1,320 MW at elev 3,700 ft**; **at minimum power pool (3,490 ft) the plant still has an estimated physical capacity of 630 MW.** 3,490 ft is where **vortex formation begins** (it sits 20 ft above the penstock intake centerline of 3,140 ft) — generation ceases *below* it, not at it. So Powell's near-term path is a **~52% derate, not a cliff to zero.**

**(b) The real cliff is Hoover, and it is much closer.** TA-15, verbatim: *"At Lake Mead elevation **1,035 feet**, Hoover Dam's generating capacity is **drastically reduced from 1,274 MW to 382 MW**. At elevations above 1,035 feet or greater in current market conditions, hydropower can be produced at or above market value. **With current turbines, if the elevation drops below 1,035 feet, it is estimated that operating costs will exceed the value of the hydropower produced.**"

**Mead is at 1,041 ft. That is ~6 ft of margin, and USBR projects 1,037 ft by 12/31 — landing 2 ft above the threshold.** Against that, Powell's 32 ft is the comfortable one.

**This matters because 1,035 ft is an ECONOMIC threshold, not a physical one** — it is the elevation at which Hoover stops being worth running. The 950 ft "minimum power pool" you'd naturally anchor on (physical capacity there: **117 MW**) is **91 ft away** and is the wrong number for a market read.

**Hoover elevation ladder [TA-15 Fig 15-7]:** ~1,469 / 1,428 / 1,304 MW at upper elevations → **382 MW at 1,035** → 374 → **117 MW at 950**. Below **895 ft** = dead pool, releases no longer possible. 895–950 ft = inactive pool (water moves, no power). Max combined nameplate **2,074 MW**, 17 commercial generators.

## 3. The number you asked for — and why it is smaller than the framing implies

Replacement priced at **Palo Verde Peak** [EIA ICE, 2026 YTD n=127, deliv 2025-12-29 → 2026-07-22]: **YTD mean $24.00/MWh**, median $21.71, **Jun–Jul mean $33.24**, max $83.65.

| Scenario | Energy | @ $24.00 YTD | @ $33.24 summer | @ $75 stress |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Already incurred** (−6.29 TWh/yr vs 2016) | 6.29 TWh | **$151M/yr** | $209M/yr | $471M/yr |
| **Forward tail:** Glen Canyon → 0 | 8.26 TWh | $198M/yr | $274M/yr | $619M/yr |

⚠️ **Read the top row before the bottom one.** A −27.6% generation collapse has *already* occurred and Palo Verde on-peak is averaging **$24/MWh** — the Western market is not pricing scarcity at all. **If losing 6.3 TWh/yr didn't move the hub, the marginal 8.3 TWh won't either, absent a coincident stress event.** Solar has substituted for the energy. As an **energy** channel this is a few hundred million dollars a year in a multi-billion-dollar market: real, sourced, and **too small to reprice anything on its own.**

**The honest steelman — and it is a good one:** the loss is **capacity and ancillary services**, not energy. Hoover falling 1,274 → 382 MW removes **~892 MW of dispatchable, fast-ramping, black-start-capable capacity** from WECC. My table above prices **only the energy** and therefore **understates the reliability value**. WECC has no PJM-style capacity market to mark it against, which is precisely why this loss will show up as *reliability events*, not as a price line — and why nobody has sized it.

**Cross-confirmation worth noting:** USBR says below 1,035 ft *"operating costs will exceed the value of the hydropower produced"* **in current market conditions**. My independent price pull says current conditions are **$24/MWh on-peak**. A low-price environment makes USBR's economic threshold *more* likely to bind, not less. Two sources, same direction, arrived at separately.

## 4. The structural finding — TA-15's own robustness table

**TA 15-14 (Lake Mead high-head turbines, ≥1,035 ft):** across **2027–2039**, *all* alternatives hold the threshold in only **18–36% of futures**; **No Action is worst at 7%.** It improves in 2040–2049 (Supply Driven LB Pro Rata 80%, LB Priority 73%, Enhanced Coordination 73%, Max Operational Flexibility 72%).

**⇒ On USBR's own modelling, Hoover spends the next ~13 years mostly BELOW the elevation at which it is worth running, under every alternative on the table.** That is the durable finding here — far more than any single summer's elevation print.
