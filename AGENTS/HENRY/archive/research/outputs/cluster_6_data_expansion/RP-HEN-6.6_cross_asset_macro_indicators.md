## **1\. Copper/Gold Ratio**

### **Strategic Evolution of the Growth Barometer**

The copper/gold ratio serves as the quintessential measure of economic optimism versus defensive posture. Traditionally, copper represents industrial demand—the "Dr. Copper" thesis—while gold serves as a safe-haven proxy for fear and monetary debasement. In early 2026, the ratio is being redefined by two competing structural forces: a supply-side "copper crunch" and a central bank-driven gold bull market.9

The calculation of the ratio involves dividing the spot price of copper per pound (or ounce in specialized models) by the price of gold per ounce. As of late January 2026, the ratio stands at approximately 0.000072 based on a gold price of $5,138.20 and a copper price equivalent.11 This historically high absolute level for both metals indicates a regime where "everything is expensive," yet the relative strength of copper reflects a belief in a durable industrial expansion tied to the rollout of renewables, electric vehicles (EVs), and AI-driven digital infrastructure.6

### **Historical Relationship with Equity Returns and the Yield Curve**

The "Gundlach Ratio"—the specific correlation between the copper/gold ratio and the U.S. 10-Year Treasury Yield—remains a core pillar of cross-asset analysis. Historically, when copper outperforms gold, it signals rising real-world demand that typically leads to higher sovereign yields.13 In 2025 and early 2026, this relationship has held firm, with rising copper prices forecasting the climb in the 10-year yield toward 4.30%.13

For the S\&P 500, a rising copper/gold ratio is generally bullish, as it correlates with improving industrial profits and corporate operating leverage. However, the current divergence is meaningful: while copper hit historical highs of $5.85/lb in January 2026, the recent "limit down" move on February 2nd suggests that speculative bubbles in the commodity complex may be vulnerable to liquidity shifts.6 A falling ratio in the presence of stable equity prices often acts as a leading indicator of an impending "growth scare" or a transition to a recessionary regime.

### **Meaningful Divergences and 2026 Outlook**

In the 2026 context, the primary divergence to monitor is the "strategic rotation." Institutional investors are increasingly allocating to copper as a "green metal" hedge, which may decouple the ratio from traditional cyclical patterns. J.P. Morgan forecasts a 330,000-ton copper deficit for 2026, which could drive prices toward $12,500/ton ($5.67/lb) even if broad economic growth moderates.6

If the ratio rises while the yield curve flattens, it suggests that industrial demand is strong but is being met by aggressive central bank tightening—a "hawkish expansion" scenario. Conversely, a falling ratio paired with an un-inverting yield curve is a classic "late-cycle" signal that has historically preceded equity market corrections of 10-15%. Current ETF tracking should focus on CPER (Copper) and GLD (Gold) to monitor these intraday shifts.

In the U.S., these are most accurately measured through Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), where the yield is quoted on an inflation-indexed basis. ### **Historical Impact on Growth Stock Valuations**

Real yields are the primary denominator in Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) models. When real yields rise, the present value of future earnings—especially for high-duration "growth" stocks—diminishes. In 2025, growth stock valuations, anchored by the "Magnificent 7," showed remarkable resilience to rising rates.16 However, analysts at J.P. Morgan and Morgan Stanley note that as 10-year real yields approach the 2.0% "restrictive" threshold, the risk of P/E compression increases significantly.16

The impact of real yields on the Equity Risk Premium (ERP) is equally profound. A higher real yield increases the "hurdle rate" for equities. If an investor can achieve a 2.0% real return in a risk-free government bond, the required return for holding volatile equities must rise, leading to a downward repricing of the broad market index if earnings growth does not accelerate proportionately.

### **Relationship Between Real Yields and P/E Compression**

Current 2026 EPS growth estimates for the S\&P 500 are approximately 13.7%, providing a fundamental floor for valuations.16 However, the "restrictive" real yield level of 1.90% implies that for the bull market to continue, operating leverage must improve through productivity gains—specifically from AI adoption—rather than through further multiple expansion.18

There is a clear historical precedent for "P/E Mean Reversion" when real yields stay above 1.5% for extended periods. The 2026 outlook suggests that while the Federal Reserve may cut nominal rates 2-3 times, real yields will remain "range bound" due to persistent inflation expectations and a steeper yield curve driven by fiscal deficits.16 This environment creates a "valuation ceiling" for the S\&P 500, estimated at roughly 10% total return for the year.18

## **3\. Breakeven Inflation**

### **Defining Market-Based Inflation Expectations**

Breakeven inflation is the difference between nominal Treasury yields and TIPS yields of the same maturity. It represents the "break-even" point where an investor would be indifferent between holding a nominal bond and an inflation-indexed bond. As of early February 2026, breakeven rates are signaling that while inflation has cooled from its 2022-2023 peaks, it remains "sticky" above the Fed's 2.0% target.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |

| 10-Year (T10YIE) | 2.35% | \+35 bps | Anchored but elevated |
| 5Y/5Y Forward | \~2.40% | Elevated | Structural shift |

### **Divergence from Realized Inflation**

Breakevens often diverge from "realized" inflation (CPI/PCE) during periods of economic shock or shifting supply chains. In February 2026, Nowcasting data from the Cleveland Fed suggests Year-over-Year (YoY) CPI is at 2.50% and Core PCE at 2.53%.20 The fact that 5-year breakevens are currently aligned with realized core inflation suggests that the market has fully priced in the "higher for longer" inflation narrative.

The divergence to watch is when breakevens fall while realized inflation remains high—a signal of impending demand destruction. Conversely, if breakevens rise while realized inflation falls, it indicates that the market anticipates a "reflationary" policy mistake or an external supply shock (e.g., renewed energy pressures).

### **Impact on Equity Valuations**

Inflation breakevens have a dual impact on equities. On one hand, moderate inflation allows for nominal revenue growth and price increases. On the other hand, if breakevens rise too sharply, it forces nominal interest rates higher, which compresses multiples. The current 2.35% level on the 10-year breakeven is considered a "sweet spot" for 2026—high enough to support nominal earnings but low enough to prevent a hawkish Fed panic.18

Efforts to anchor these expectations are complicated by the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" (OBBBA), which is expected to boost household spending through tax refunds, potentially adding 0.2-0.3% to headline inflation in the first half of 2026\.19 Equity investors should prioritize sectors with high pricing power to hedge against these lingering breakeven pressures.

## **5\. Yield Curve Signals**

### **Current Regime: The Bear Steepener**

The yield curve is the most watched indicator for the economic cycle. After a historical 700+ day inversion of the 2s10s spread, the curve has finally "un-inverted" and moved into a "bear steepening" phase in early 2026\.8 A bear steepener occurs when long-term rates rise faster than short-term rates, reflecting a combination of stronger growth expectations and higher inflation/term premiums.8

| 30Y/10Y Spread | 0.64% | Positive | Fiscal Deficit Concern |

### **Bull Steepening vs. Bear Steepening**

The distinction between "bull" and "bear" steepening is vital for equity positioning. A "bull steepening" is led by short-term rates falling faster than long-term rates (typical of a Fed pivot to fight recession). A "bear steepening" is led by long-term rates rising (typical of a market demanding higher compensation for holding long-term debt).

The current bear steepening regime is "atypical" because it is happening as the economy is decelerating.8 This suggests that the "term premium"—the extra compensation for holding long bonds—is returning to positive territory after years of being deeply negative. For equities, this is a "challenging optimistic" signal: growth remains, but the cost of long-term financing is structurally higher, which may cap the upside for capital-intensive industries.28

### **Historical Lead Time and Recession Signals**

Historically, the lead time from yield curve inversion to the onset of a recession is 12-18 months. However, the 10Y/3M spread—the Fed's preferred indicator—is now at 0.60%, suggesting that the immediate recession risk has faded.29 Morningstar analysts suggest that the yield curve "normalization" tells us the Fed is ready to provide accommodation, which historically supports equities in the medium term, even if initial volatility is high.27

Investors should classify the current regime as "Late Expansion / Normalization." In this phase, diversified investors often find that fixed income offers better "diversification benefits" than it did during the period of deep inversion.27
