# **Passive Investing Dominance \- Research Output**

## **Executive Summary**

The transformation of the United States equity market from a system of active price discovery to one dominated by passive index-tracking vehicles represents the most significant structural shift in modern financial history. As of year-end 2025, passive investment vehicles—comprising index mutual funds and Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs)—control over **55% of US equity assets under management (AUM)**.1 This threshold, long theorized by academics as a potential "tipping point" for market efficiency, has now been crossed, creating a market structure with no historical precedent.2

The implications of this shift are profound and systemic. The rise of the "Big Three" asset managers (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) has centralized voting power to an extent that rivals the trusts of the early 20th century, with these firms now constituting the largest shareholder in **88% of S\&P 500 companies**.4 More critically, the nature of equity demand has shifted from elastic (price-sensitive) to inelastic (flow-driven). Empirical evidence from the 2024-2025 period validates the "Inelastic Markets Hypothesis," suggesting that passive dominance significantly amplifies the price impact of capital flows, detaching valuations from fundamental earnings power.5

This report identifies a mechanical "concentration feedback loop" where capitalization-weighted allocations force capital into the largest companies regardless of valuation, driving top-10 S\&P 500 concentration to historic highs of **38.22%**. While this "Structural Bid" from 401(k) inflows creates a persistent floor for equity prices, it masks a latent liquidity mismatch. The arbitrage mechanisms that align ETF prices with underlying assets are fragile; stress tests modeled on the March 2020 dislocation suggest that a sustained redemption cycle could trigger a "doom loop" of mechanical selling that the diminished active management ecosystem is ill-equipped to absorb.7

The following analysis details the scale of this transformation, the degradation of price discovery mechanisms, the risks of liquidity failure, and the altered nature of mean reversion in a passive-dominated world.

**1\. Scale & Market Share**

### **1.1 The Crossing of the Rubicon: AUM Dynamics (2000–2025)**

The trajectory of US equity ownership has followed a relentless, non-linear path toward passive dominance. The "crossover point"—the moment passive US equity assets exceeded active assets—occurred in late 2023, a milestone that signaled the end of the active management era.1 Since that event, the gap has widened aggressively, driven by a self-reinforcing cycle of active underperformance and cost-conscious capital migration.

As of December 31, 2025, passively managed funds account for **55% of net assets** in the US fund universe.1 This dominance is the result of massive capital reallocation over the last decade. In 2024 alone, passive strategies absorbed **$885.94 billion** in net inflows, while active funds suffered **$165.36 billion** in net outflows.10 This divergence indicates that active management is not merely losing market share but is undergoing an absolute liquidation of its investor base. The growth rate of passive funds from 2015 to 2024 was **286%**, outpacing active fund growth by a ratio of nearly 6:1.11

### **1.2 The "Big Three" Oligopoly**

The shift to passive investing has not democratized market power; it has concentrated it in the hands of a triopoly. The market is now effectively overseen by the "Big Three"—BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street Global Advisors. As of 2025, these three firms control nearly **60% of global ETF assets** 13 and constitute the largest shareholder in **88% of S\&P 500 companies**.4

This concentration creates a homogenization of shareholder behavior. Unlike dispersed active managers who might have divergent views on a company's valuation or strategy, the Big Three are legally mandated to hold these stocks regardless of price, management quality, or earnings outlook, provided the company remains in the index.

### **1.3 Closet Indexing: The "Real" Passive Share**

The headline figure of 55% passive market share significantly understates the true extent of passive behavior in the market. A substantial portion of the remaining "active" management industry engages in "closet indexing"—hugging the benchmark to minimize career risk (tracking error).

Academic research and industry analysis suggest that active fund portfolios often exhibit **70-90% overlap** with their benchmarks.12 When closet indexers are aggregated with explicit index funds, the "effective" passive share of the market—capital that exerts no discretionary price discovery pressure—may be as high as **75-80%** of institutional capital.12

This distinction is critical for risk modeling. If 80% of the money is merely following the price rather than setting it, the burden of price discovery falls on a rapidly shrinking minority of active traders, hedge funds, and high-frequency trading (HFT) firms. The "active share" of the market—the portion of capital truly willing to bet against consensus—is at a historic low, implying that prices are increasingly set by flow mechanics rather than fundamental analysis.

**2\. Price Discovery Impact**

### **2.1 The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis (IMH)**

The most significant theoretical development relevant to this investigation is the **Inelastic Markets Hypothesis (IMH)** proposed by Gabaix and Koijen. Traditional finance theory assumes demand for stocks is highly elastic—if a stock's price deviates slightly from its fundamental value, arbitragers will aggressively buy or sell to restore equilibrium.

However, in a market dominated by passive funds (which have a demand elasticity of effectively zero) and constrained active mandates (which rarely deviate massively from benchmarks), the aggregate market becomes inelastic. Gabaix and Koijen estimate that investing **$1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5**.5

This "multiplier effect" occurs because there are fewer sellers willing to part with shares when prices rise. Passive funds *must* hold, and active funds are constrained by tracking error limits. Therefore, a small inflow of cash chases a limited supply of shares, driving prices up disproportionately. This hypothesis provides a robust explanation for the decoupling of valuations from aggregate earnings growth observed in 2024-2025. The structural flows into passive vehicles (via 401ks) act as a leveraged force on valuations, creating a "melt-up" dynamic that is mathematically distinct from speculative bubbles driven by euphoria.16

### **2.2 The "Index Inclusion Effect" Resurgence**

If markets were informationally efficient, the inclusion of a stock in an index like the S\&P 500 should not significantly change its price, as inclusion contains no new fundamental information about the company's future cash flows. Historically, this "inclusion effect" (a price pop upon announcement) existed but waned in the 2010s as liquidity improved and hedge funds anticipated additions.

However, recent data confirms a sharp resurgence of this inefficiency in the 2024-2025 era. In 2025, index additions (e.g., companies like Block, Coinbase, DoorDash) outperformed the equal-weight S\&P 500 by an average of **7.4 percentage points** on the announcement day.17

This data suggests that the sheer volume of capital that *must* buy the stock upon inclusion (index funds rebalancing) has overwhelmed the capacity of hedge funds and market makers to front-run or arbitrage the trade. The passive demand shock is now so large it permanently alters the equilibrium price, confirming that non-fundamental flows are a primary driver of single-stock valuations.18

### **2.3 The Grossman-Stiglitz Paradox & Analyst Coverage**

As passive dominance grows, the economic incentive to perform fundamental research declines, a realization of the **Grossman-Stiglitz Paradox**. If the majority of assets are managed by fees of \<10bps, there is less revenue to pay for equity research.

While coverage of mega-cap stocks remains high, coverage of mid- and small-cap stocks has deteriorated. This has led to greater pricing inefficiencies in the lower tiers of the market. Furthermore, passive flows drive up correlations between stocks in the same index. When money flows into the SPY ETF, it buys both high-quality and low-quality companies indiscriminately, causing their prices to move in tandem regardless of divergent fundamentals. This reduces the benefits of stock-picking and diversification, as the "beta" component of returns overwhelms the "alpha" component.19

Academic research by Greenwood and Thesmar (2011) on "Stock Price Fragility" further supports this. They found that assets held by flow-driven investors (like mutual funds and ETFs) are more fragile and susceptible to non-fundamental volatility. As ownership concentrates in these vehicles, the potential for volatility *unrelated to news* increases.20

**3\. Concentration Mechanics**

### **3.1 The Feedback Loop: "Momentum on Steroids"**

One of the most dangerous dynamics identified is the **mechanical concentration feedback loop**. In a capitalization-weighted index (like the S\&P 500), a company's weight is determined by its market value. Passive funds allocate capital strictly according to these weights.

The mechanism operates as follows:

1. **Initial Outperformance:** A company (e.g., Nvidia) reports strong earnings, and its stock price rises.  
2. **Weight Increase:** Its weight in the index increases proportionally.  
3. **Mechanical Buying:** As fresh capital flows into passive funds (via 401ks), a larger portion of every new dollar is automatically allocated to Nvidia *because* it went up.  
4. **Price Inflation:** This buying pressure pushes the price up further, independent of valuation.  
5. **Repeat:** The cycle continues, reinforced by momentum traders and "closet indexers" who cannot afford to be underweight the winner.22

This creates a "momentum on steroids" effect where the largest companies detach from reasonable valuation anchors. Research Affiliates notes that passively owned stocks exhibit positive momentum exposure, while actively held stocks display negative loadings (contrarian positioning). The dominance of passive flows essentially creates a "momentum machine," extending the duration and magnitude of trends beyond what fundamental analysis would justify.23

### **3.2 Quantifying Concentration**

The result of this loop is historic levels of market concentration. The top 10 companies in the S\&P 500 now account for approximately **38.22%** of the index's total market capitalization. This exceeds the peak concentration seen during the Dot-Com bubble (\~25%).24

For the three-year period ending 2025, a small cluster of mega-cap tech stocks (often termed the "Magnificent Seven") contributed **55% of the total return** of the S\&P 500\.26 Without these stocks, the annualized return of the index would have been halved. This creates a vulnerability: the entire market's performance is now contingent on the continued momentum of fewer than a dozen firms.

### **3.3 Factor Tilts and Distortions**

Passive flows do not just distort individual stock prices; they distort factor returns. The mechanical buying of large-cap growth stocks (which dominate indices) suppresses the "Value" and "Size" factors.

* **Small Cap Discount:** The Russell 2000 (Small Caps) trades at a significant valuation discount to Large Caps (\~26% discount in 2025).27 This is partly because passive flows are weighted heavily toward the S\&P 500, starving smaller companies of capital.  
* **Value Factor Suppression:** Because "Value" investing often involves buying falling stocks (which have lower index weights) and selling rising stocks (which have higher weights), passive flows—which do the opposite—act as a headwind to value strategies.28

**4\. Redemption Risk Assessment**

### **4.1 The ETF Liquidity Mismatch**

The most acute systemic risk associated with passive dominance is the **liquidity mismatch** inherent in the ETF structure. ETFs offer **daily liquidity** to investors—essentially promising that they can exit positions instantly at the screen price. However, they often hold assets (corporate bonds, bank loans, small-cap equities) that cannot be liquidated instantly without massive price concessions.

In normal markets, Authorized Participants (APs) arbitrage any difference between the ETF price and the underlying Net Asset Value (NAV). They buy the underlying bonds/stocks, exchange them for ETF shares, and sell the shares (or vice versa). This keeps the ETF price tethered to reality. However, in stressed markets, if the underlying bond market freezes, APs cannot effectively price or trade the underlying basket. They withdraw from the arbitrage mechanism, breaking the tether.8

### **4.2 Case Study: March 2020 Fixed Income Dislocation**

The COVID-19 crash provides a critical "live fire" stress test for the passive ecosystem. During the peak volatility of March 2020, the liquidity mechanism for fixed-income ETFs fractured.

* **LQD Discount:** The iShares iBoxx $ Investment Grade Corporate Bond ETF (LQD), one of the most liquid credit instruments in the world, traded at a discount to its NAV of roughly **5%**.7 This is an enormous dislocation for an asset class deemed "safe."  
* **Mechanism of Failure:** The underlying corporate bond market became illiquid. Dealers (banks) were unwilling to hold inventory due to balance sheet constraints and volatility. APs stopped creating/redeeming shares because they could not hedge their exposure or source the underlying bonds at predictable prices.  
* **The "Price Discovery" Argument:** Proponents argue that the ETF price (trading at a discount) was the "real" price, while the NAV (based on stale bond quotes) was artificial. While true, this highlights the risk: the ETF price collapsed *faster* than the underlying assets, effectively transferring the liquidity cost to the ETF holder.29  
* **Resolution:** The crisis was only resolved when the Federal Reserve stepped in to buy corporate bond ETFs explicitly, essentially becoming the "Market Maker of Last Resort".7

### **4.3 The "Doom Loop" Simulation**

Academics have modeled a "Doom Loop" scenario where passive redemptions trigger a cascade. Applying 2008-level redemption rates to the 2026 passive AUM structure yields a frightening projection.

**Scenario: A 10% Aggregate Redemption in Passive Equities**

1. **Trigger:** An exogenous shock (e.g., geopolitical event, inflation spike) causes retail and institutional investors to redeem 10% of their passive holdings (\~$2 trillion).  
2. **Mechanical Selling:** ETFs and Index Funds must sell $2 trillion of stock pro-rata. They cannot "wait for a better price" or "sell the liquid stuff first." They sell everything, immediately.  
3. **Inelasticity Strike:** Per the Gabaix-Koijen multiplier (5x), $2 trillion in selling pressure could theoretically suppress market capitalization by up to **$10 trillion**.  
4. **Feedback:** Lower asset prices hurt ETF performance (-20% to \-30%), triggering *more* redemptions from reactive investors (momentum chasers).  
5. **Liquidity Spiral:** As prices fall, liquidity providers (HFTs/APs) widen spreads or withdraw, exacerbating the drop. Active managers, having been decimated for a decade, lack the "dry powder" to step in and arrest the fall.5

**Bond vs. Equity Risk:** While equity markets are generally more liquid, the "Doom Loop" risk is higher in Bond ETFs due to the opacity and fragmentation of the underlying bond market. However, the concentration in equities means that a "run" on the S\&P 500 would be a run on the entire US economy.31

**5\. Historical Context**

### **5.1 The Japanese Experiment: A Window into the Future**

Has a market ever survived \>50% passive ownership? We are in uncharted waters, but the Japanese market offers the closest proxy. The Bank of Japan (BOJ) has been a massive passive investor in its own equity market since 2010, effectively nationalizing a portion of the market via ETFs.

* **Scale:** By 2025, the BOJ held ETFs equivalent to \~7% of the total Tokyo Stock Exchange market capitalization.33 In specific float-adjusted terms for companies like Fast Retailing, the BOJ was effectively the top shareholder.  
* **Distortions:** Research indicates the BOJ's buying artificially suppressed volatility and inflated valuations of the Nikkei 225 constituents.34 It created a "governance void" where a major shareholder never voted or sold, insulating management from discipline.  
* **The Unwinding Problem:** In late 2025, the BOJ announced plans to dispose of its ETF holdings. The challenge is immense: simply announcing the sale creates a supply overhang. The BOJ has had to commit to a **100-year disposal timeline** to avoid crashing the market.33 This illustrates the "Hotel California" nature of massive passive intervention—you can check in, but exiting without destroying price stability is nearly impossible.

### **5.2 Theoretical Limits**

Is there a theoretical limit to passive share?

* **The Grossman-Stiglitz Limit:** If passive share reaches 100%, no price discovery occurs, and prices become random walks detached from fundamentals.  
* **The 60-70% Threshold:** Simulations suggest that market efficiency degrades non-linearly. We are currently in the danger zone (55% to 60%). Studies indicate that beyond this threshold, "chaotic" price dynamics become more frequent because there are simply not enough active eyes to correct mispricing before it becomes systemic.36  
* **Volatility Paradox:** High passive share suppresses volatility in normal times (due to lack of reactive trading) but *amplifies* volatility in stress times (due to lack of liquidity providers). This creates a market characterized by long periods of eerie calm punctuated by violent, unexplained crashes.37

**6\. Structural Bid Analysis**

### **6.1 The 401(k) Machine: The Permanent Bid**

A counter-argument to the "Doom Loop" is the "Structural Bid" hypothesis. This suggests that passive flows create a permanent floor for equity prices, altering mean reversion. The US retirement system has shifted entirely to Defined Contribution (DC) plans, specifically 401(k)s, creating a relentless flow of monthly capital.

* **Inelastic Demand:** Every two weeks, millions of Americans automatically buy equities via payroll deductions. This buying happens regardless of whether the S\&P 500 P/E is 15x or 30x.2  
* **Target Date Dominance:** The default option for most plans is the Target Date Fund (TDF), which now holds over **$4 trillion** in assets.38 TDFs mechanically rebalance. If stocks fall, TDFs *buy more* to maintain their target allocation. This creates a built-in "stabilizer" or mean-reversion force that dampens dips.39  
* **Magnitude:** With $10 trillion in 401(k) assets 40, the flow is large enough to absorb moderate selling pressure, explaining why recent market corrections have been shorter and shallower (e.g., the rapid recoveries in 2020 and 2022).

### **6.2 The New Equilibrium**

The Structural Bid suggests that "mean reversion" to historical valuation averages (e.g., Shiller CAPE of 17\) may never happen.

* **Valuation Floor:** If 60% of the market is price-insensitive buying, the equilibrium P/E ratio may have permanently shifted higher (e.g., to 20-25x).  
* **Risk of Reversal:** This floor depends entirely on employment. The only thing that stops the 401(k) bid is mass unemployment. Therefore, the market has become hyper-correlated to the labor market. A recession that causes layoffs would simultaneously shut off the liquidity tap, potentially making the subsequent crash deeper than historical norms.2

**7\. Active Ecosystem Health**

### **7.1 The Dying Canary**

Active management is the immune system of the market—it detects and removes inefficiencies. This immune system is compromised.

* **Performance Crisis:** Approximately **90%** of active equity fund managers have underperformed their index over the last 10 years.41 This has led to a crisis of confidence and a "cash burn" of talent.  
* **Brain Drain:** The "smart money" has migrated from mutual funds to multi-manager hedge funds and private equity, leaving the public mutual fund space hollowed out.

### **7.2 Is There Enough "Smart Money" Left?**

The burden of price discovery has shifted to Hedge Funds and High-Frequency Traders (HFT).

* **Hedge Fund Capacity:** While hedge fund AUM is at record highs ($5.7 trillion) 42, their capacity is insufficient to absorb the liquidity needs of the $20 trillion passive complex. If the passive complex tries to sell 10%, hedge funds cannot buy it without a massive price collapse. They are also constrained by leverage limits and risk management rules (VaR shocks).  
* **HFT Limitations:** HFTs provide liquidity in calm markets but are notoriously fickle; they vanish during volatility (the "Hot Potato" effect). They trade *volatility*, they do not provide *valuation support*.43
