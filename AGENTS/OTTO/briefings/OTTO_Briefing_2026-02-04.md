# OTTO Briefing: The Subprime Auto Fraud Pattern
**Date:** February 4, 2026  
**Format:** Audio briefing (~20 minutes)  
**Purpose:** Comprehensive situation overview

---

## The Briefing

Let me tell you about something that's been quietly unfolding in the American financial system over the past six months. It's a story about fraud, about systemic risk, and about how the plumbing of the credit markets can become corrupted in ways that don't show up until it's too late. This is the situation we're tracking with OTTO, and I think once you understand the full picture, you'll see why it matters not just for auto lending, but for the entire consumer credit chain we've been mapping.

### Part One: What Happened at Tricolor

Let's start with Tricolor Holdings, because that's where this story begins. Tricolor was a Texas-based auto lender that specialized in what's called the "buy here, pay here" market. These are the people who can't get a loan anywhere else — immigrants, people with no credit history, people who've had financial setbacks. Tricolor would sell them a used car and finance it themselves, usually at very high interest rates, around eighteen to twenty-one percent.

On the surface, this seemed like a legitimate business serving an underbanked population. They even had Community Development Financial Institution status, which gave them a kind of social respectability. They grew aggressively — quadrupling in size over five years to operate sixty-five dealerships across seven states. By the time they collapsed, they had originated over two billion dollars in loans.

Here's what was actually happening behind the scenes, according to the Department of Justice. Starting around 2018, the founder Daniel Chu and his team began running a systematic fraud scheme. The core mechanism was something called double-pledging. 

Let me explain how warehouse lending works, because this is critical to understanding the fraud. When Tricolor originated a car loan, they didn't keep it on their books. They would take those loans and pledge them as collateral to get credit from warehouse lenders — big banks like JPMorgan Chase and Fifth Third. The banks would give Tricolor cash, secured against those auto loans. Then Tricolor would eventually package those loans into asset-backed securities and sell them to investors, using the proceeds to pay back the warehouse lenders.

The fraud was elegant in its simplicity. Tricolor pledged the same loans to multiple warehouse lenders simultaneously. They would take a pool of auto loans, tell JPMorgan those loans secured their credit line, and then tell Fifth Third that those exact same loans also secured a different credit line. They were essentially getting paid twice for the same collateral.

But it gets worse. They also fabricated entire loans that didn't exist. They created synthetic receivables — fake car loans that existed only on paper. They manipulated the data they reported to lenders, making delinquent loans appear current. When a borrower stopped paying, Tricolor would sometimes manually enter fake payments into their system to hide the deterioration.

By August 2025, the company had pledged approximately two point two billion dollars in collateral to various lenders. The problem? They only actually had about one point four billion in real assets. That's an eight hundred million dollar hole. Eight hundred million dollars of collateral that simply didn't exist.

When this finally unraveled — and it unraveled fast once it started — Tricolor filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy in September 2025. Not Chapter 11, where you try to reorganize. Chapter 7, where you just liquidate everything. The executives were charged with federal crimes in December. Daniel Chu, the CEO, faces potential life in prison. He and his COO David Goodgame pled not guilty in January. Two other executives have already pled guilty and are cooperating with prosecutors.

Here's a detail that really captures the character of this fraud: in the weeks before the bankruptcy filing, even as Chu knew the company was, in his own recorded words, "definitely insolvent," he directed the payout of a six and a quarter million dollar bonus to himself. He then used some of that money to buy property in Beverly Hills. The prosecutors have recordings of him comparing their situation to Enron and strategizing about how to blame the banks for not catching the fraud sooner.

JPMorgan took a hundred seventy million dollar loss. Fifth Third took between a hundred seventy and two hundred million. Barclays lost about a hundred forty-six million. Several regional banks — Origin, Renasant, Triumph — are dealing with their own exposures, collectively around seventy-five million in non-accrual loans.

### Part Two: The Pattern Emerges

If Tricolor were an isolated case, we could write it off as one bad actor. But it's not isolated.

Within weeks of the Tricolor collapse, questions emerged about First Brands Group. First Brands is a completely different kind of company — they're an automotive aftermarket parts supplier, the kind of company that makes oil filters and brake pads under brands you'd recognize: Fram, Autolite, Trico. But they had pursued an aggressive acquisition strategy, rolling up over twenty companies for about four billion dollars, funded largely through debt.

The First Brands situation is in some ways even more disturbing than Tricolor. When they filed for bankruptcy in September 2025, they had nine point three billion dollars in debt and only twelve million dollars in cash. That's not a typo — nine billion in debt, twelve million in cash. 

The forensic investigation revealed what prosecutors are now calling a "Ponzi scheme." The company's founder, Patrick James, and his brother Edward allegedly fabricated invoices to secure billions in financing. They would take an invoice for a hundred seventy-nine dollars and alter it to show nine thousand two hundred seventy-one dollars — a fifty-fold increase — and then use these inflated invoices as collateral for borrowing.

They also double-pledged, just like Tricolor. The same receivables were factored multiple times to different lenders. Jefferies has seven hundred fifteen million dollars of exposure to First Brands. UBS has over five hundred million. The investigation has identified approximately two point three billion dollars in factored invoices that can't be matched to actual inventory sales. Two point three billion dollars of fake collateral.

The James brothers were arrested in late January 2026 in Ohio. They were indicted on January 29th for running what the government calls a "continuing financial crimes enterprise." Like Chu at Tricolor, they face potential life sentences.

Now, you might notice something interesting here. Both frauds used the same basic mechanism: double-pledging collateral and fabricating data. Both ran for years without detection. Both involved executives extracting massive personal payments before the collapse. 

This is what we call the "cockroach" thesis. When you find one cockroach in your kitchen, you don't have one cockroach — you have an infestation. The question we're asking is: are there more?

The third company in our fraud trilogy is PrimaLend Capital Partners, though this one is different. PrimaLend filed for Chapter 11 in October 2025 with about two hundred eighty-six million in debt. They provided credit lines to buy-here-pay-here dealerships — essentially, they were a lender to the lenders. Unlike Tricolor and First Brands, the PrimaLend collapse appears to be driven by macroeconomic stress rather than fraud. Their borrowers — the small dealerships — were getting crushed by rising delinquencies among their own customers. This is still important because it shows us that even honest players in this space are under severe pressure.

### Part Three: The Systemic Picture

Now let's zoom out and look at the bigger picture, because the individual fraud cases are really just symptoms of something larger.

Over the past decade, there's been a massive structural shift in how credit flows through the American financial system. Banks, facing tighter capital requirements after the 2008 crisis, pulled back from certain types of lending. Non-bank financial institutions — what regulators call NBFIs or NDFIs — stepped into the gap. These include specialty finance companies, private credit funds, and business development companies.

The numbers here are staggering. As of November 2025, banks had loaned one point seven trillion dollars to these non-bank financial institutions. That's trillion with a T. And this number grew forty-seven percent in just one year. At the top twenty-five banks in the country, lending to non-bank financials now represents thirty-four percent of all commercial and industrial loans. In 2018, that figure was fourteen percent. The banking model has fundamentally changed.

This matters because of concentration risk. Regional banks that you might think are conservative, boring institutions — banks like U.S. Bancorp, Truist, Fifth Third — now have eight to twelve percent of their loan books tied up in lending to these non-bank intermediaries. When Tricolor collapsed, it wasn't just Tricolor that got hurt. The pain flowed upward to the banks that provided their funding.

Let's talk about the warehouse lending ecosystem specifically. Wells Fargo is estimated to have eight to twelve billion in subprime auto warehouse exposure. JPMorgan has five to ten billion. Citi has four to seven billion. Barclays and Deutsche Bank each have three to five billion. When you add it all up, the major warehouse lenders — we call them the "Warehouse Titans" — control the bulk of subprime auto funding.

The industry's response to the fraud discovery has been dramatic. They're shifting from what used to be called "trust but verify" to what they're now calling "verify and control." Advance rates — the percentage of collateral value that lenders will actually lend against — have dropped from eighty to eighty-five percent down to sixty to seventy percent for deep subprime collateral. Lenders are now requiring third-party custodians to hold all loan documents, ending the era where originators could self-custody their own paper. Daily cash sweeps, real-time data feeds, quarterly audits — all of this is being mandated.

There's even a pilot program launching in April to create a distributed ledger — essentially a blockchain-based registry — where lenders can verify that a loan hasn't already been pledged to someone else. The fact that this didn't exist before tells you something about how the industry operated on trust.

### Part Four: The Consumer Credit Picture

Let's talk about what's happening on the ground with actual borrowers, because the fraud cases are interacting with a genuinely stressed consumer credit environment.

The subprime auto market was already under pressure before Tricolor collapsed. As of late 2025, delinquency rates for subprime auto loans have reached levels we haven't seen since 1993. The sixty-plus day delinquency rate for monoline lenders — the specialized auto finance companies — hit sixteen point six percent. For context, anything above fifteen percent is considered highly stressed.

What's driving this? Several factors. Vehicle prices spiked during the pandemic, and many borrowers are now underwater on their loans. About fifty-three percent of borrowers have negative equity — they owe more than their car is worth. This creates a trap: you can't refinance because you'd have to bring money to the table, and you can't sell the car because you'd still owe money after the sale. So you're stuck paying eighteen to twenty-one percent interest rates on a depreciating asset.

The geographic pattern is striking. The top fourteen states for auto loan delinquency are all in the South. Mississippi is worst at nine point eight percent. Louisiana is at eight point four. Texas is at seven point nine percent, but Texas is actually the biggest dollar risk because average loan balances there are fifteen percent above the national average. If you're looking for where the stress is concentrated, draw a line from Texas across to Georgia and color everything south of it red.

There's another concerning development that we've identified: credit washing. This is a form of fraud where consumers or credit repair companies file false identity theft claims with the FTC or credit bureaus to get legitimate negative marks removed from credit reports. A borrower with a 500 FICO score can suddenly appear to have a 700 FICO score. This has grown a hundred sixty-two percent year over year and now affects one point seven percent of all auto loan applications.

Think about what this means for lenders. Their underwriting models are built on credit scores. If those scores are being systematically corrupted, the models can't accurately predict who will default. You're flying blind. Industry experts estimate total auto lending fraud exposure at nine point two billion dollars in 2024 alone, and they expect ten billion-plus in 2026.

### Part Five: The Asset-Backed Securities Market

Now let's talk about what's happening in the securitization market, because this is where the rubber meets the road for investors.

Auto loans get packaged into asset-backed securities — ABS — and sold to investors. These deals are structured in tranches, from AAA-rated senior notes down to BB-rated subordinate notes. The riskier tranches absorb losses first, protecting the senior bondholders.

Performance varies dramatically by vintage — meaning when the loans were originated. The 2020 vintage is performing relatively well, benefiting from pandemic stimulus payments. The 2021 vintage is weakening. But the 2022 vintage is a disaster. These are loans originated at the peak of vehicle prices, when lenders were still relatively loose with credit. At thirty-one months of seasoning, the 2022 vintage has a cumulative net loss rate of twenty-two point four percent. For comparison, the 2021 vintage at the same age was around nineteen percent.

If you're looking for where the downgrade wave might start, it's the 2022 vintage, particularly deals from originators that focus on deep subprime borrowers.

Spreads have widened significantly. Triple-B rated subprime auto ABS now trades at about a hundred eighty basis points over the benchmark — that's fifty basis points wider than before the Tricolor collapse. Double-B rated tranches are trading at three hundred fifty basis points or more. This is the market pricing in risk.

The rating agencies have already started taking action. Thirty-four classes of Tricolor-backed deals were downgraded to D — meaning default — after the company missed interest payments. S&P has raised loss expectations on eight Exeter Finance deals. First Help Financial has been downgraded. The question is whether this stays concentrated or spreads.

Our assessment is that a broad downgrade wave is moderate probability but would be concentrated, not systemic. The prime auto market — which is seventy-five percent of the total — remains stable. The structural protections in modern ABS deals are stronger than they were in 2008. But the 2022 vintage, the deep subprime specialists, and the subordinate tranches are all at meaningful risk.

### Part Six: Private Credit and the BDCs

There's another layer to this story involving private credit — the massive pools of capital managed by firms like Blackstone, Apollo, and Ares. Private credit has grown from about four hundred sixty billion dollars in 2013 to over three trillion today. A significant portion of this capital has flowed into asset-based finance, including auto lending.

Business Development Companies — BDCs — are publicly traded vehicles that make loans to middle-market companies. Several BDCs had exposure to First Brands. Great Elm Capital had between twenty-five and twenty-nine million dollars of exposure, representing twenty-one percent of their net asset value. That's an enormous concentration in a single credit. Prospect Capital had fifty-nine million. Total direct BDC exposure to First Brands was about two hundred thirty-seven million dollars.

But there's also indirect exposure through CLOs — collateralized loan obligations. First Brands loans are distributed across two point seven billion dollars in five hundred seventeen different CLO vehicles. Many of these CLOs are held in the portfolios of larger BDCs. This creates a transmission mechanism: even if you didn't directly lend to First Brands, you might own a piece of a CLO that did.

In the fourth quarter of 2025, BDC redemption requests spiked by two hundred percent. Oaktree Strategic Credit saw redemption requests hit almost five percent of net asset value. Blackstone's BCRED fund hit three and a half percent. This matters because redemptions can create a feedback loop: investors withdraw, the fund has to sell assets to meet redemptions, selling assets at distressed prices marks down the portfolio, which causes more investors to withdraw.

The largest U.S. banks have committed approximately ninety-five billion dollars to private credit vehicles through what's called back-leverage — subscription facilities and warehouse lines that the funds themselves use to juice their returns. So when private credit has problems, banks feel it too.

### Part Seven: The Regulatory Landscape

The regulatory response to all of this is complicated by politics. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — the CFPB — was created after 2008 specifically to protect consumers from predatory lending. But the Bureau is facing an existential threat.

A Department of Justice opinion concluded that because the Federal Reserve has been running at a loss since 2022, there are no "combined earnings" from which the CFPB can legally draw its funding. This creates a potential funding lapse as early as March 2026. If Congress doesn't act, the Bureau could enter a shutdown posture — pausing supervision, rulemaking, and litigation.

The current administration has also deprioritized certain enforcement theories. The Bureau announced it would no longer pursue cases based on "disparate impact" — the idea that practices can be discriminatory even without intentional discrimination if they affect protected groups disproportionately.

In response, state attorneys general are stepping in. New York is pushing the FAIR Business Practices Act, which would codify the CFPB's definitions of "unfair and abusive" practices into state law. Massachusetts has legislation targeting subprime lending practices. California is working on regulations around home improvement financing. We're seeing what regulators call "substituted enforcement" — states filling the gap left by federal retreat.

This creates a fragmented landscape. National lenders now have to navigate a patchwork of state requirements that vary widely. Some may simply pull back from subprime lending in states with stricter rules, further reducing credit access for vulnerable consumers.

### Part Eight: Historical Parallels

People have been asking whether this is another 2008. It's a fair question, and the answer is: not quite, but there are important parallels.

In 2008, the problem was subprime mortgages. Lenders made loans to people who couldn't afford them, packaged those loans into securities, and sold them to investors worldwide. When housing prices fell, the whole system cascaded. Lehman Brothers went bankrupt. The government had to bail out the banks. Nine million people lost their jobs. Ten million lost their homes.

The similarities to today: Both involve subprime lending to vulnerable populations. Both involve securitization and complex financial engineering. Both involve off-balance-sheet entities and fabricated data. Both involve executives extracting money before the collapse.

The differences: Scale is the big one. The subprime mortgage market in 2008 was embedded in the global banking system. Today's subprime auto market is large — but it's mostly in the non-bank sector, which is somewhat ring-fenced from the core banking system. The one point seven trillion in NDFI exposure is concerning, but it's not the same as having toxic mortgages on the balance sheets of every major bank.

Structural protections are also stronger. Modern auto ABS deals have higher subordination levels — more cushion to absorb losses before senior bondholders get hurt. The "bankruptcy remote" structure means that even if an originator fails, a backup servicer can continue managing the loans for investors.

The Fed's intervention probability for subprime auto is very low. If more lenders fail, they'll be allowed to fail. This isn't too big to fail. The government will prosecute the fraudsters, but they won't bail out the industry.

### Part Nine: What We're Watching

Let me close by telling you what we expect to happen and what would change our view.

We have ten formal predictions for OTTO. The highest confidence one is that the 2022 vintage cumulative net loss will exceed twenty-five percent — we put that at seventy-five percent probability. We think there's a seventy percent chance a fourth fraud case gets discovered, and Flagship Credit Acceptance is our primary candidate. That company was sold in a fire sale to a private equity firm last November. There have been mass layoffs. Employee reviews describe a "toxic" and "downward spiral" environment. If the new owners' forensic review finds a collateral gap similar to Tricolor's, we'll have our fourth cockroach.

We're also watching the CFPB funding situation closely. A March funding lapse would create a regulatory vacuum that could accelerate both fraud and aggressive lending practices.

The scenario we're most concerned about isn't the individual fraud cases — those are bad, but they're idiosyncratic. What concerns us is the transmission chain. If employment breaks — and LABOR is telling us the danger window is the second and third quarters of this year — then consumer distress accelerates. When consumers can't pay their car loans, delinquencies spike. When delinquencies spike, ABS deals underperform. When ABS deals underperform, warehouse lenders get nervous. When warehouse lenders get nervous, credit tightens. When credit tightens, consumers who might have been able to refinance can't, and they default. It's a feedback loop.

This is why OTTO matters beyond just auto lending. It's a leading indicator. Auto loans are often the first thing consumers prioritize — you need your car to get to work. If people are falling behind on their car payments, they're in serious trouble. And if the lenders financing those loans are cooking the books, we won't see the stress until it's too late to do anything about it.

The fraud cases have already cost banks close to a billion dollars. They've already triggered a complete rethinking of how warehouse lending works. They've already shown us that the trust-based systems we relied on can be systematically exploited. The question now is whether this is the end of the bad news or the beginning.

Our working model says it's closer to the beginning. But that's a model, not truth. We'll know more as the year unfolds. The Daniel Chu trial in August, the 2022 vintage performance over the next two quarters, the CFPB funding situation in March — these are the key milestones that will tell us whether our thesis is right or wrong.

That's the OTTO situation as we understand it today. The system is under stress. The fraud has been discovered. The question is how deep it goes.
