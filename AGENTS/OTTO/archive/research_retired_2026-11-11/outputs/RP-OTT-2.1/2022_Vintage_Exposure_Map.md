# **Systematic Deterioration and Exposure Mapping of the 2022 Vintage Subprime Auto ABS Sector**

The collateral performance of the 2022 vintage of subprime automobile asset-backed securities (ABS) has emerged as the most significant pocket of distress within the broader consumer credit landscape. Characterized by a confluence of peak used-vehicle valuations at the time of origination, the abrupt withdrawal of pandemic-era fiscal stimulus, and a rapid transition to a higher interest rate environment, this specific cohort has demonstrated performance metrics that significantly lag behind historical benchmarks.1 By the first quarter of 2026, cumulative net loss (CNL) figures for many 2022 transactions have breached initial projections, necessitating substantial upward revisions in expected cumulative net loss (ECNL) by major rating agencies and triggering a series of downgrades across mezzanine and subordinate tranches.3 The structural vulnerability of these deals is exacerbated by the fact that many loans were underwritten with expanded loan-to-value (LTV) ratios on vehicles that have since experienced rapid depreciation, leaving a substantial portion of the underlying borrower base in an "underwater" equity position.5 This analysis maps the resulting exposure across originators, institutional holders, and the banking sector, identifying the primary nodes of systemic risk and the tradeable implications of this vintage-specific malaise.

## **Part A: Originator Exposure and Portfolio Degradation**

The 2022 vintage was shaped by an aggressive expansion in subprime lending volumes as specialty finance companies sought to capitalize on robust consumer demand and high vehicle turnover. However, the loosening of underwriting standards during this period, particularly regarding debt-to-income (DTI) and LTV limits, has created a legacy of underperforming paper that now dominates the loss profiles of several major issuers.6 The divergence in performance between the 2021 and 2022 cohorts is stark; while the 2021 vintage benefited from residual stimulus cushions and an undersupply of vehicles that bolstered recovery rates, the 2022 vintage was originated at the absolute zenith of the Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index, making it uniquely susceptible to the subsequent normalization of used car prices.1

### **Issuer Ranking and Issuance Landscape**

The issuance of 2022 subprime auto ABS was highly concentrated among a group of large, specialized lenders. Santander Consumer USA, through its Santander Drive Auto Receivables Trust (SDART) and its "DRIVE" shelf—the latter of which specifically targets the deepest subprime segments—remains the most significant originator of 2022 paper.5 Other high-volume issuers include Exeter Finance (EART), GM Financial’s AmeriCredit (AMCAR), and Credit Acceptance Corporation (CAALT). The dominance of Santander is further evidenced by repossession data from early 2025, where SDART and DRIVE together accounted for approximately 43% of all ABS-related vehicle repossessions.10 This volume is a direct consequence of the aggressive 2022 underwriting; for example, the SDART 2022-7 transaction featured a weighted average LTV of 106.58% at origination.5 As used vehicle values corrected throughout 2024 and 2025, these high LTVs translated into higher loss severities, as the proceeds from auctioning repossessed vehicles were insufficient to cover the outstanding principal balances.6

### **Deal-Level Performance vs. Projections**

The degradation of the 2022 vintage is most visible at the individual deal level, where ECNL revisions have become commonplace. Analysts have observed that many 2022 deals hit their "peak loss" period approximately 18 to 24 months after origination, a timeframe that coincided with the highest inflationary pressure on subprime households.1 A notable example is United Auto Credit Securitization Trust (UACST) 2022-2, which saw its lifetime CNL expectation revised from an initial 20.25% to a critical 32.50% by late 2024\.3 This level of loss represents a terminal threat to the subordinate tranches of the transaction, as the available credit support is systematically eroded by charge-offs.4

Furthermore, the structural integrity of these transactions is being tested by "turbo" amortization triggers. When cumulative losses exceed certain thresholds, or when overcollateralization (OC) is depleted, the deal structure typically mandates that all available excess spread be used to pay down senior noteholders, effectively "starving" the junior tranches of interest payments and making a principal loss on Class E and F notes almost certain.11 The Westlake 2022-1 transaction provides a case study in this phenomenon; KBRA placed its Class F ratings on "Watch Downgrade" in 2025 specifically because the overcollateralization had been completely depleted due to underperformance.11

The failure of the Tricolor (TAST) shelf in 2025 was a watershed moment for the sector. KBRA downgraded dozens of ratings across seven TAST transactions to 'D' (Default) following missed interest payments.13 While the Tricolor failure involved specific allegations of fraud and structural opacity, it also underscored the extreme vulnerability of "deep subprime" pools to shifts in the macroeconomic and regulatory environment, such as changes in immigration policy that affected Tricolor's core obligor base.2

### **Originator Financial Health and Guidance**

The public originators most exposed to the 2022 vintage have spent much of 2025 attempting to "ring-fence" these portfolios while highlighting the better performance of their 2024 and 2025 originations.1 Ally Financial (ALLY) reported in its Q4 2025 earnings call that it is finally beginning to benefit from "vintage rollover," as the underperforming 2022 and early 2023 loans are charged off and replaced by more conservatively underwritten assets.16 Despite this, Ally's retail auto net charge-off rate reached 2.14% in Q4 2025, reflecting the persistent tail of the 2022 vintage's distress.18

Credit Acceptance Corporation (CACC) has also provided transparent evidence of the 2022 vintage's weakness. In its year-end 2022 filings, the company already noted that its 2022 consumer loan assignments were yielding collection results "significantly worse" than initial estimates.19 By Q4 2025, CACC announced a further reduction in forecasted net cash flows from its loan portfolio by approximately $34.2 million, largely driven by the underperformance of the 2023 and 2024 cohorts that followed the initial 2022 downturn.20 The company's unique "dealer holdback" structure provides a buffer against these losses, but the erosion of the "forecast accuracy rate" remains a significant concern for analysts.12

## **Part B: Institutional Holder Mapping**

The distribution of 2022 subprime auto ABS risk is broad, spanning mutual funds, insurance company general accounts, and specialized private credit vehicles. Many institutional investors were drawn to the high coupons of mezzanine and subordinate subprime tranches during the 2022 issuance boom, often as a hedge against rising interest rates or as a means of generating yield in a tightening credit environment.21

### **Mutual Funds and ETFs**

Several major asset managers have identified concentrations in subprime auto paper. PIMCO, through its ABS and Short-Term Investments Portfolio, manages over $15 billion in asset-backed securities, including significant positions in auto-related tranches.23 Similarly, the Natixis LS Intermediate Duration Bond Fund reported holdings in various 2022-2023 subprime series, including Ally Auto Receivables Trust (AART) and American Credit Acceptance (ACA).24

The risk for mutual fund holders is twofold: credit risk and liquidity risk. As spreads for BBB-rated subprime auto tranches widened by 50 basis points in late 2025, the market value of these holdings has declined significantly.2 For funds that are benchmarked against the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index, the "up-in-quality" rotation observed in early 2026 has led many managers to divest from BBB and BB subprime tranches in favor of A-rated prime paper or corporate bonds, further depressing prices for 2022 mezzanine paper.25

### **Insurance Company Concentration**

Insurance companies are among the most stable but also most heavily exposed buyers of subprime auto ABS. U.S. insurers' investment in structured finance, particularly collateralized loan obligations (CLOs) and ABS, totaled approximately $276.8 billion at year-end 2024\.26 Life insurers account for the vast majority of this exposure, holding roughly 82% of the industry's total.26

A significant trend within the insurance sector is the dominance of private equity-owned (PE-owned) insurers. These entities tend to have significantly higher allocations to asset-backed securities than traditional carriers. Research indicates that PE-owned insurers may hold as much as 50% of their investment portfolios in ABS, compared to just 25%-33% for the broader industry.27 This concentration creates a potential systemic link between the performance of the 2022 subprime vintage and the solvency or capital adequacy of mid-sized life insurance platforms.27

The regulatory environment for insurers is also shifting. The NAIC's "Principles-Based Bond Project," which became effective for Q1 2025 reporting, has introduced more stringent definitions of what qualifies as a "bond" for Schedule D reporting.29 Under these new rules (SSAP No. 26R and 43R), asset-backed securities that fail to provide sufficient structural protection or that exhibit a high probability of principal loss may be reclassified to Schedule BA, which carries significantly higher Risk-Based Capital (RBC) charges.29 This reclassification threat could force insurance companies to sell their 2022 subordinate tranches into a dislocated market, creating a "forced seller" dynamic that would further widen spreads.29

### **CLOs, BDCs, and Private Credit**

The "Asset-Backed CLO" market has also served as a repository for 2022 subprime auto tranches. Managers like Blackstone, Carlyle, and CIFC Asset Management, which oversee tens of billions in CLO assets, must navigate the credit quality of the auto ABS paper often used as "bucket" investments within multi-asset CLOs.32 Furthermore, the collapse of First Brands, an auto parts manufacturer, in late 2025 highlighted the exposure of Business Development Companies (BDCs) to the automotive ecosystem.34 BDCs such as Monroe Capital Income Plus and Great Elm Capital Corp held significant positions in First Brands, illustrating the "second-order" effects of the subprime malaise; as consumers fall behind on car payments, the secondary market for parts and services also faces a liquidity squeeze.34

## **Part C: Banking Sector and Warehouse Exposure**

The banking sector’s exposure to the 2022 subprime vintage is primarily indirect, channeled through warehouse lending facilities and trustee roles. While major banks like JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, and Citibank have largely exited direct subprime origination, they remain the primary providers of the infrastructure that sustains the specialty finance market.36

### **Warehouse Lender Concentration**

Banks provide revolving credit facilities, or "warehouses," to originators like Santander, Exeter, and Credit Acceptance, allowing them to fund new loans before they are permanently securitized.36 The risk for the warehouse lender is that the value of the underlying collateral—the 2022 vintage loans—may decline faster than the originator can pay down the facility. In the event of an originator's insolvency, as seen with Tricolor, the warehouse lender is left to manage a portfolio of underperforming subprime loans.2

Customers Bank, identified as a top warehouse lender, offers credit facilities ranging from $5 million to $300 million to various originators.38 JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo also face litigation and operational risks related to their roles as indenture trustees for subprime trusts, where they are often accused of failing to enforce repurchase obligations when the underlying loans breach representations and warranties.36

### **Bank Trading Books and Systemic Links**

Major banks also hold subprime auto ABS on their trading books to support market-making activities. Trading volumes in the ABS market surged to an average daily volume (ADV) of $2.15 billion in late 2025, a 17% increase year-over-year.2 However, the widening of subprime spreads suggests that banks may be carrying inventory that is subject to "mark-to-market" losses.2 While third-quarter earnings reports from banks in 2025 highlighted improved credit performance in their own direct auto and credit card portfolios, the volatility in the subprime ABS segment remains a concern for systemic transparency.2

## **Part D: Tradeable Implications and actionable Strategies**

The systematic underperformance of the 2022 vintage has created a set of tradeable opportunities for market participants, ranging from shorting public equities to expressing views on the relative value of structured tranches. The 2026 outlook for the sector is defined by "structural differentiation," where the quality of the servicer and the robustness of the deal's payment waterfall determine the ultimate recovery for investors.39

### **Short Candidate Analysis**

Publicly traded companies with outsized exposure to the 2022 vintage—either as originators or as holders of residual interest—face significant earnings headwinds as charge-offs peak.

### **ABS Relative Value and Spreads**

The pricing of 2022 subordinate tranches (BBB, BB, and B) appears mispriced relative to their loss trajectories. In early 2026, BBB-rated subprime auto tranches saw the "sharpest movement" in the market, widening by 50 basis points in a single month as investors reacted to the collapse of Tricolor and weak retail auto data.2

For fixed-income investors, the strategy of "up-in-quality" remains paramount. There is little evidence that the relative spread between BB (high yield) and BBB (investment grade) tranches will tighten further, given the backdrop of impending recessionary fears and the structural fragility of 2022 pools.25 The ICE BofA BBB US Corporate Index Option-Adjusted Spread stood at 0.94% in late January 2026, reflecting a broader tightening of credit conditions that will continue to pressure the most subordinate subprime tranches.44

### **Actionable Trade Ideas**

Based on the mapping of 2022 vintage exposure, the following trades represent ways to express a view on the ongoing deterioration of subprime credit:

1. **Short CACC Equity**: Given the sequential growth in "contingent losses" and the admission of significantly worse-than-forecasted collections for the 2022 vintage, Credit Acceptance is vulnerable to further downward revisions in its adjusted net income.19  
2. **Long Prime / Short Subprime Auto Spread**: This trade capitalizes on the "differentiation" seen in the market, where prime auto delinquencies remain steady at 1.9% while subprime delinquencies have spiked to 16%.2  
3. **Hedge via Insurance Sector Put Options**: Using options on major life insurers or PE-owned platforms with high ABS concentrations (Schedule D reclassification risk) provides a way to play the systemic risk of a subprime "fire sale".27  
4. **Short the BBB Mezzanine Tranches of SDART 2022-7**: This specific deal’s high LTV and seasoning profile make it a prime candidate for a downgrade to "non-investment grade" as losses breach initial ECNL caps.2  
5. **Volatility Plays on ALLY**: Ally's 2026 guidance anticipates NIM migration toward the "upper 3% range," but sequential margin softness and seasonal credit spikes make it a candidate for high volatility around earnings dates.17

## **Synthesis of Findings and Future Outlook**

The 2022 vintage of subprime auto ABS has established itself as a terminal outlier in consumer credit performance. The mapping of exposure reveals a sector where risk is deeply embedded in the "K-shaped" economy, with lower-income borrowers disproportionately affected by the resumption of federal student loan payments and the enforcement of immigration policies.1 The systemic risks are most acute for originators with high leverage and for institutional holders, particularly PE-owned insurers, that are currently facing a "regulatory cliff" due to NAIC bond definition changes.27

As we move through 2026, the primary focus for market participants will be the "vintage rollover" rate. While originators like Ally and Santander are pivoting to higher-quality customers, the "tail risk" of the 2022 vintage remains substantial. The collapse of Tricolor served as a warning that the "opaque financing structures" of the subprime market can still lead to catastrophic failures.2 For investors, the takeaway is clear: structural protections and transaction design matter more than ever, and those who failed to distinguish between the "stimulus-aided" 2021 performance and the "inflation-burdened" 2022 reality are now facing a period of unavoidable principal loss.2

The ability of the financial system to absorb these losses will depend on the continued resilience of the labor market and the willingness of institutional investors to maintain liquidity in a sector that has clearly breached its credit stability limits.1
