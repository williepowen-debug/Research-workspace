# GM Financial Bank & Ford Credit Bank: FDIC ILC Approval Analysis

## Approval Overview

On **January 22, 2026**, the FDIC Board of Directors approved deposit insurance applications for both Ford Credit Bank and GM Financial Bank, permitting the establishment of two new Utah-chartered industrial banks. Both approvals were signed by Executive Secretary **Debra A. Decker** in Washington, D.C.. The same day, Utah Commissioner of Financial Institutions **Shaun Berrett** conditionally approved the state industrial bank charters.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

No specific FDIC order numbers or docket numbers appear in the public orders — the documents are styled simply as "ORDER" under "Application for Federal Deposit Insurance" for each institution. The approvals were issued under the statutory framework of **Section 6 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act (12 U.S.C. § 1816)**, evaluating seven factors: financial history and condition, capital adequacy, future earnings prospects, management character and fitness, risk to the Deposit Insurance Fund, convenience and needs of the community, and consistency of corporate powers.[^3][^4][^1]

FDIC Chairman **Travis Hill** oversaw the resumption of ILC application processing after years of regulatory uncertainty under the Biden administration. The Biden-era FDIC had proposed rules to tighten industrial bank applications but never finalized them.[^5][^6]

### Application Timeline

GM Financial's path was notably longer. It first applied for an ILC charter in **December 2020**, gained conditional approval from the Utah regulator in **June 2024**, then pulled its FDIC application to "address feedback." The company reapplied in **February 2025**. Ford submitted its application in **2022** and faced pushback from trade groups.[^8]

## Regulatory Supervision

Both banks will be **dual-supervised** by:

1. **FDIC** — as the federal deposit insurer and primary federal regulator for state-chartered nonmember banks[^4][^1][^3]
2. **Utah Department of Financial Institutions (UDFI)** — as the state chartering authority[^2]

Critically, the **Federal Reserve has no supervisory role** over the parent companies. This is the core of the ILC "loophole" — because industrial banks are excepted from the Bank Holding Company Act's definition of "bank," parent companies are not required to register as bank holding companies and therefore avoid consolidated Federal Reserve supervision. The FDIC has addressed this gap through **Part 354 of its Rules and Regulations**, which imposes written commitment and reporting requirements on ILC parent companies not subject to consolidated federal supervision.[^9][^10][^11][^12]

Both orders explicitly require compliance with Part 354 at all times. Both orders also require the parent companies to enter into:[^3][^4]

- A **Capital and Liquidity Maintenance Agreement** — requiring the parent companies to provide financial resources to support the bank's capital adequacy and liquidity[^4][^3]
- A **Parent Company Agreement** — with conditions related to reporting, examination of parent companies, and monitoring compliance with affiliate transaction laws (Sections 23A and 23B of the Federal Reserve Act)[^3][^4]

Both banks must maintain **a majority of independent directors** on their boards (independent of the parent companies).[^4][^3]

Utah's UDFI charters and regulates approximately **87% of the nation's industrial bank assets** and has supervised these FDIC-insured institutions for over 35 years. As of September 30, 2025, Utah-chartered depository institutions had combined total assets of $433 billion. Ford Credit Bank will join sixteen other industrial banks supervised by UDFI.[^2]

## Capital Requirements

Both orders impose **materially elevated capital requirements** relative to standard de novo banks:

| Requirement | Ford Credit Bank | GM Financial Bank |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial paid-in capital** | $1.5 billion[^4] | $667 million[^3] |
| **Minimum Tier 1 leverage ratio** | 15%[^4] | 15%[^3] |
| **"Well capitalized" status** | Must meet/exceed at all times (12 CFR § 324.403(b))[^4] | Must meet/exceed at all times (12 CFR § 324.403(b))[^3] |
| **Adequate allowance for credit losses** | Required in addition to above[^4] | Required in addition to above[^3] |
| **Dividend restriction** | No dividends in first 3 years without FDIC Regional Director approval[^4] | No dividends in first 3 years without FDIC Regional Director approval[^3] |
| **Parent support obligation** | Capital and Liquidity Maintenance Agreement required[^4] | Capital and Liquidity Maintenance Agreement required[^3] |

The **15% Tier 1 leverage ratio** is notably elevated. For context, the standard "well-capitalized" threshold under FDIC rules is a 5% leverage ratio. The 15% requirement is **three times the normal threshold** — a clear reflection of the FDIC imposing enhanced prudential guardrails on commercial-parent ILCs. Klaros Group's Valerie Song characterized the capital requirements as evidence that "the FDIC is clearly committed to maintaining appropriately high standards for safety and soundness".[^13][^8]

The significant difference in initial capitalization ($1.5B for Ford vs. $667M for GM) likely reflects differing projected asset sizes and business plan scope in the approved business plans.

## Conditions and Restrictions — Full Detail

Both FDIC orders contain **25 conditions each**. The conditions are substantially parallel but with entity-specific variations. Key conditions include:

### Operational Constraints

- Must operate within the parameters of the approved **Business Plan**; material deviations require 60-day prior written notice and non-objection from the FDIC Regional Director[^3][^4]
- Must submit **updated Business Plans annually** with current and three-year pro forma financial statements[^4][^3]
- Must maintain **separate accounting and business records** (including customer data) from parent companies and affiliates[^3][^4]
- Books and records must be under the control of **authorized Bank officials** and available for FDIC review at the main office[^4][^3]

### Governance and Management Controls

- **Board/senior executive changes**: During the first three years, any proposed additions or changes to the board or senior executive officer positions require 30-day prior written notice and FDIC Regional Director non-objection, including Interagency Biographical and Financial Reports[^3][^4]
- **Compensation**: Employment agreements and compensation arrangements for senior officers/directors require FDIC non-objection[^4]
- **Long-term incentive plans (LTIP)**: Must be determined independently from the parent; FDIC may force exercise or forfeiture of stock rights if capital falls below minimums[^3][^4]
- **Ford Credit Bank** specifically required to appoint a **Chief Credit Officer** and **Chief Audit Executive** with FDIC non-objection prior to opening, and add at least one additional outside director and one additional inside director[^4]
- **GM Financial Bank** specifically required to appoint a **Chief Compliance Officer** with FDIC non-objection prior to opening[^3]

### Affiliate Transaction Controls

- Neither bank may enter into contracts with parent companies or affiliates for services **material to the bank's operations** without prior FDIC Regional Director approval[^4][^3]
- Ford Credit Bank has a carve-out for transactions already covered by a General Services Agreement[^4]
- The Ford order specifically addresses **Section 23A attribution rule** analysis regarding indirect auto financing through independent dealers not receiving floorplan financing from FMCC[^4]

### Pre-Opening Requirements

- FDIC must conduct a **pre-opening visitation** with satisfactory findings[^3][^4]
- Must obtain all necessary final approvals from federal, state, and other authorities[^3][^4]
- GM Financial Bank must develop and test a **credit decisioning engine** per its business plan with FDIC non-objection before deployment[^3]
- Ford Credit Bank must finalize and implement a **CRA Strategic Plan** approved by the Regional Director[^4]
- Both must submit comprehensive policies on capital planning, liquidity/funds management, and credit underwriting for FDIC review[^3]

### Audit and Reporting

- **Independent audits** annually for at least the first three years[^4][^3]
- Audited financial statements due to the Regional Director within 90 days of fiscal year end[^3][^4]
- Management letters and other auditor reports due within 15 days of receipt[^4][^3]

### Expiration

- Approval expires if the banks are **not established within 12 months** (by January 22, 2027), unless the FDIC grants a written extension[^1][^3][^4]

## Lending Activities — Product and Geographic Scope

### Ford Credit Bank

- **Primary lending**: Purchase of retail installment sales contracts and lease contracts from **independent Ford dealers**[^1][^4]
- **Additional lending**: Consumer direct financing for parts and accessories, electric vehicle infrastructure loans, and working capital loans to independent dealers[^4]
- **Geographic scope**: **Nationwide** — automotive financing products offered across the country[^2][^1]
- Ford Credit CEO Cathy O'Callaghan indicated the bank will initially start small, adding products like **certificates of deposit and indirect auto financing** through dealers over time[^7]

### GM Financial Bank

- **Primary lending**: Purchase of new and used automobile loans originated by GMF, secured by the automobile[^3]
- **Geographic scope**: **Nationwide** — automotive financing products[^1]
- The FDIC Statement notes GMF also provides retail loan and lease lending, floorplan lending, construction and real estate loans, and insurance to network dealerships at the parent level[^3]

Neither order imposes explicit geographic lending restrictions — both banks are approved for **nationwide** operations. The product scope is effectively constrained by the approved business plans, which focus on automotive financing. Any material deviation requires FDIC non-objection.[^5][^1][^4][^3]

## Deposit Insurance and Funding

### Deposit Insurance

Both banks will carry standard **FDIC deposit insurance** with the standard coverage limit of **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category**. This is the same coverage that applies to all FDIC-insured institutions — no special limitations or enhanced coverage were noted in the orders.[^14]

### Deposit Products and Funding Strategy

| Feature | Ford Credit Bank | GM Financial Bank |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary funding** | Retail savings accounts and time deposits[^1][^4] | Savings accounts and time deposits[^1][^3] |
| **Distribution channels** | Bank website and mobile application[^1][^4] | Bank website and mobile application[^1][^3] |
| **Target depositors** | Consumers nationwide[^4] | GM employees/retirees; GM dealer principals, employees, and customers; GM customers; GMF loan/lease customers; and individuals with no GM relationship[^3] |
| **Demand deposits** | Not mentioned in business plan[^4] | Not mentioned in business plan[^3] |

Neither order explicitly restricts brokered deposits by name, but the business plans describe direct-to-consumer online deposit gathering rather than broker-intermediated channels. The absence of demand deposit (checking) accounts is notable — ILCs historically avoid offering demand deposits because doing so would eliminate the BHC Act exemption that allows commercial parent ownership without Federal Reserve oversight.[^15][^9]

GM Financial Bank will additionally use "many marketing channels and have arrangements with listing services" for deposit gathering, suggesting the bank may utilize deposit listing/aggregator platforms (such as those used by other online banks) to attract depositors beyond GM's existing customer base.[^3]

IBAT President Christopher Williston characterized the strategy bluntly: "There's only one way for these banks to succeed — that's to offer high yield savings rates to flighty depositors to fund risky loans".[^16]

## Industry Context and ILC Landscape

### Current ILC Population

There are currently **24 industrial banks** operating in the United States. Notable existing auto-industry ILCs include **Toyota** and **BMW**, which already have industrial banks functioning in the U.S..[^17]

### Pending ILC Applications (as of February 2026)

At least three additional ILC applications await FDIC decisions:[^8]

- **Nissan** — applied in June 2025 to establish Nissan Bank U.S.[^18]
- **PayPal** — filed in December 2025 with FDIC and UDFI for PayPal Bank[^15]
- **Affirm** — filed in January 2026 with the Nevada Financial Institutions Division and FDIC for Affirm Bank (Nevada-chartered ILC, notably not Utah)[^19]

**Stellantis** also submitted a request for an ILC charter in 2025.[^17]

### Industry Opposition

The Independent Community Bankers of America (ICBA) remains strongly opposed, calling ILCs a "regulatory loophole" that injects "unnecessary systemic risk into the banking system". The Bank Policy Institute (BPI) and ICBA jointly oppose the ILC model, arguing that parent companies escape the robust supervision and regulation that bank holding companies face.[^11][^20][^8]

### Snell & Wilmer Analysis

Snell & Wilmer's February 2026 analysis briefly noted the approvals, characterizing them as coming "after years of preparation" and highlighting that both banks will accept FDIC-insured deposits from customers into savings accounts. The Snell & Wilmer piece primarily contextualized the approvals within broader 2026 auto finance compliance trends, including collections practices, data accuracy, and fair lending/AI use.[^21]

### Regulatory Framework — Part 354

The FDIC's Part 354 (12 C.F.R. Part 354), finalized in December 2020, governs parent companies of industrial banks not subject to consolidated Federal Reserve supervision. It requires written agreements covering:[^10][^12]

- Annual listing of parent company subsidiaries
- Consent to examination of parent and subsidiaries
- Annual operational reports
- Record maintenance for FDIC risk assessment
- Independent annual audits
- Board independence (parent representation below 50%)
- Capital and liquidity maintenance commitments[^12]

In July 2024, the FDIC proposed amendments to Part 354 that would expand its scope and add additional criteria, including assessing whether an ILC is a "shell" or "captive" entity. It is unclear whether those proposed amendments have been finalized under the current FDIC leadership.[^22][^10]

***

## Strategic Implications

The core strategic rationale for both automakers is **funding cost reduction**. By accessing FDIC-insured deposits (which carry lower interest cost than capital markets debt), Ford and GM can lower their blended cost of funds, enabling more competitive loan and lease rates at a time when average new vehicle transaction prices approach $50,000.[^23][^14]

Karen Petrou of Federal Financial Analytics summarized the dynamic: "The lower you can drive your funding costs, the lower you can drive your lending costs, and the more competitive you get".[^14]

Ford Credit CEO O'Callaghan described it as "a long-term strategic initiative that will expand our capabilities, enabling us to offer additional savings options to customers, which will over time help lower our cost of funding as well as broaden our financing offerings". The bank will "start small and grow over time" with approximately one year to put people, systems, and processes in place before taking deposits.[^7]

This marks a significant shift in the regulatory posture toward ILCs after what was effectively a **de facto moratorium** that lasted from the mid-2000s (triggered by Walmart's failed ILC attempt in 2006) through much of the Biden era. The approvals under FDIC Chairman Travis Hill signal that ILC formation is now a viable path for well-capitalized commercial firms willing to accept elevated capital requirements and extensive prudential conditions.[^6][^13][^17]
