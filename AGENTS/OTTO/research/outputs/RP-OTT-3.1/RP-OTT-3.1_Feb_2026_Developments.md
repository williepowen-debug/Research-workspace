## Executive Summary

The subprime auto lending sector continues to deteriorate rapidly in early 2026. First Brands Group dominates the landscape with criminal indictments, unit wind-downs, and potential Chapter 7 conversions. PrimaLend's sale process is underway but without a confirmed buyer. Flagship Credit's InterVest acquisition remains pending with limited public disclosure. The Fed's 2026 stress test scenarios published February 4 model a severe recession with 10% unemployment, but contain no auto-specific scenarios. Rating agencies have been relatively quiet on subprime auto ABS in early 2026 after significant action in late 2025.

## 1. PrimaLend Capital Partners — Sale Process Update

### Background
PrimaLend filed Chapter 11 on October 22, 2025 in the Northern District of Texas, listing assets and liabilities each under $500 million, with funded debt of approximately $286.1 million. The company secured DIP financing from existing lenders to continue operations.

### Sale Process Status
- **Jan 4–5, 2026**: PrimaLend announced plans to seek court approval for bidding procedures to sell assets and maximize value of its loan portfolios.
- **Early Jan 2026**: A Texas court approved bidding procedures for PrimaLend's Chapter 11 asset sale, described as a "market test" for the subprime auto lending sector.
- **No confirmed auction outcome**: There is no publicly available evidence that a January 23, 2026 auction occurred or concluded. The sale process appears to be ongoing through the court-supervised bidding procedures. No buyer, acquisition price, or recovery rate has been publicly disclosed as of mid-February 2026.

### Creditor Plan Vote
No public information was found confirming a February 9 creditor plan vote deadline or its outcome. The case appears to remain in the sale process phase rather than a plan confirmation phase.

### Fraud Allegations
- **Jan 7, 2026**: Auto loan servicer Prime Asset LLC sued PrimaLend and CEO Mark Jensen in bankruptcy court, alleging PrimaLend used Prime Asset as a "dumping ground" for distressed assets, saddling it with nearly $45 million in debt and an under-performing portfolio. Allegations include breach of fiduciary duty and bad faith.
- No double-pledging or collateral manipulation allegations similar to Tricolor have surfaced in public filings.

### Market Implications
Industry observers are closely watching PrimaLend's sale outcome as a signal of market confidence in subprime auto lending. As Joseph Cioffi of Davis+Gilbert noted: "A significant sign to watch for the industry is just how much interest the sale generates and the price generated, and the impact on whether lenders going forward may find it attractive to come in to support a troubled company".

## 2. First Brands Group — Rapidly Deteriorating Situation

First Brands has become the dominant story in the subprime/auto-adjacent bankruptcy space. The timeline of developments from January through mid-February 2026 is extraordinary in its velocity.

### Examiner Investigation (Martin De Luca)
- **Jan 9, 2026**: U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Christopher Lopez approved the appointment of Martin De Luca, a partner at Boies Schiller Flexner, as examiner to investigate the multi-billion-dollar fraud allegations. The U.S. Trustee received more than 70 applications for the examiner role.
- **Budget**: $7 million, as previously ordered by the court.
- **Scope**: Investigation into allegations that former CEO Patrick James misappropriated funds and created at least $2.3 billion in liabilities through alleged misconduct involving invoice sales, including double- and triple-pledging collateral.
- **Report due**: February 25, 2026. **No preliminary findings have been publicly released** as of February 14. The mediation process and criminal indictments have overtaken the examiner timeline.

### Criminal Indictments (Jan 29, 2026)
This is the most significant development. Federal prosecutors in the Southern District of New York filed a nine-count indictment against:

- **Patrick James** (61), founder and former CEO — charged with operating a continuing financial crimes enterprise, bank fraud, wire fraud, and conspiracy to commit money laundering. Faces potential life sentence.
- **Edward James** (60), brother and former SVP — charged with similar offenses. Faces up to 30 years on most counts.

Key allegations from the indictment:
- The James brothers operated First Brands as a "Ponzi scheme" where new loan proceeds repaid old lenders and funded a lavish lifestyle.
- They inflated invoices for accounts receivable, double- and triple-pledged collateral, falsified corporate financial statements, and concealed substantial liabilities from lenders.
- Fraud dating back to 2018, yielding billions in fraudulent financing.
- A separate guilty plea from former executive Andy Brumbergs, who is cooperating with the government.
- At the time of filing for bankruptcy, First Brands had over $9 billion in debt and only $12 million in cash.

Patrick James denies the charges; his attorney says he "built First Brands from the ground up into a global leader". Edward James's attorney says his client "has maintained integrity and dignity".

### Section 363 Sale Process
- **Remaining brands**: FRAM, Trico, Raybestos (wipers, filters, pumps, lighting, towing, accessories) continue operating.
- **Stalking horse**: Ad Hoc Group of lenders in discussions to serve as stalking horse bidder for certain business segments.
- **International operations**: Excluded from Chapter 11 proceedings; company exploring separate transition to new ownership.
- Ford identified as "likely the most vulnerable" OEM customer given reliance on First Brands for F-150 wiper components.

### Kickback Allegations (Onset Financial)
Bloomberg Law reported on January 6 that creditors allege Onset Financial and Edward James were involved in a kickback scheme tied to "usurious" financing arrangements. Creditors claim Onset advanced no more than $2.5 billion, already received approximately $2.9 billion, and is still asserting $1.9 billion more in bankruptcy claims. If successfully litigated, this could reshape priority of payments.

## 3. Flagship Credit Acceptance — InterVest Acquisition

### Transaction Status
- **Announced**: November 20–21, 2025. InterVest Capital Partners, a New York-based specialty finance firm, entered a definitive agreement to acquire Flagship's operating assets through a newly formed entity, Flagship Financial Group LLC.
- **Leadership**: Jim Landy, a seasoned auto finance executive, will become CEO upon closing.
- **Estimated value**: Approximately $400 million was the estimate from about a year prior to the deal.
- **Prior ownership**: Flagship was held by funds affiliated with Perella Weinberg Partners Capital Management, sub-advised by Innovatus Capital Partners.
- **Advisors**: Deutsche Bank (financial advisor to seller), McDermott Will & Schulte (legal to seller), Piper Sandler (financial advisor to buyer), Willkie Farr & Gallagher (legal to buyer).

### What Is NOT Known
As of February 14, 2026, there is **no public disclosure** of:
- Closing date or closing confirmation for the InterVest transaction
- Forensic audit findings
- Ongoing mass layoffs beyond those confirmed in April 2024
- Collateral discrepancies similar to Tricolor's $800M gap

The deal's terms remain confidential. Neither party has publicly updated the market on the transaction's status since the November announcement. Flagship had over $16 billion in lifetime loan originations and more than 500 employees at the time of the deal.

### Risk Assessment
The lack of public disclosure is notable in the current environment where transparency around subprime auto collateral is paramount. Without confirmed forensic audit results or a closing confirmation, the market is left to monitor indirectly through ABS performance data and regulatory filings.

## 4. Fed Stress Test Scenarios (Published Feb 4, 2026)

The Federal Reserve Board finalized its 2026 stress test scenarios on February 4, 2026. The FDIC and OCC released coordinated scenarios on February 11.

| 10-year Treasury | 4.1% | 2.3% | Q4 2026 |
| Real GDP | — | −4.6% decline | Trough Q2 2027 |

### Auto Lending-Specific Scenarios
The 2026 stress test does **not** include explicit auto lending stress variables. The 28 scenario variables cover broad macroeconomic conditions (GDP, unemployment, interest rates, equity/house/CRE prices). Banks are required to model their own auto loan loss projections using these macro paths internally. There is no separate "auto lending exploratory scenario" as some had hoped.

### Comparison to Prior Years
- The unemployment peak of 10% and 58% equity decline are broadly consistent with recent years' severely adverse scenarios.
- The equity decline is somewhat steeper than the 2025 scenario due to 16% equity price appreciation in 2025, which mechanically increases the modeled decline under the scenario design framework.
- The Board calibrated most discretionary variables "near the upper one-third of their ranges of severity," indicating a deliberately aggressive calibration.
- CRE price declines of 39% are at the severe end of the 30–45% guide range.
- Notably, the scenario specifies the Japanese yen appreciates against the dollar (unique among all currencies), which has implications for yen carry trade dynamics.

### Key Enhancement
For 2026, the Board is publishing its macro model for Stress Testing and its global market shock (GMS) model for the first time, as part of an enhanced transparency initiative. The Board also voted to maintain current stress capital buffer requirements until 2027 while it considers public feedback.

## 5. Other Subprime Auto Lender Developments

### Tricolor Holdings — Criminal Case Advancing
- **Sept 10, 2025**: Filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy, liquidating ~65 dealerships across six states.
- **Dec 17, 2025**: Federal prosecutors charged CEO Daniel Chu and COO David Goodgame with conspiracy to defraud lenders. Two executives (Kollar and Seibold) have pleaded guilty and are cooperating.
- Allegations include double-pledging collateral, manipulating loan tapes, presenting defaulted loans as current, and an $800M+ collateral gap.
- Chu allegedly directed $6.25M in bonuses to himself as the company collapsed.
- Major banks (JPMorgan, Barclays, Fifth Third) facing losses approaching $200M each.
- Fifth Third Bank disclosed fraud in Tricolor's collateral files and expects $170–$200M impairment charge.
- Lenders reassessing risks in BHPH financing broadly.

The S&P CreditWatch actions on Lendbuzz and SAFCO securitizations are particularly notable because they reflect a new risk vector — immigration enforcement impacting subprime auto loan pools where "many of the underlying obligors include immigrants, and who might not be legal U.S. residents or citizens". This is a sector-specific risk that did not exist in prior cycles.

### Moody's 2026 Structured Finance Outlook
Moody's December 2025 outlook for global structured finance flagged shifting U.S. government policies as a risk for consumer ABS, noting "labor market weakening, still-high borrowing costs and persistent affordability issues". The outlook remained officially "stable" but with caveats around political polarization and consumer debt performance.

## Systemic Risk Assessment

The convergence of these cases reveals a pattern of structural fraud embedded in subprime auto lending and adjacent supply chain finance:

| Company | Fraud Type | Estimated Scale | Status |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| Tricolor Holdings | Double-pledging collateral, falsifying loan data | ~$2B, $800M+ collateral gap | Ch. 7 liquidation; CEO/COO indicted |
| First Brands Group | Invoice inflation, double/triple pledging, Ponzi-like structure | $2.3B+ missing from factored receivables; $9B+ total liabilities | Ch. 11 → partial Ch. 7 possible; CEO/SVP indicted |
| PrimaLend | Alleged distressed asset dumping, fiduciary breach | $45M Prime Asset lawsuit; $286M total debt | Ch. 11 sale process ongoing |

The common thread across Tricolor and First Brands is **double-pledging of collateral** — using the same assets to secure financing from multiple lenders simultaneously. This practice has drawn comparisons to the mortgage-backed securities fraud of 2007–2008 and has prompted warehouse lenders and factoring firms to reassess their due diligence and collateral verification processes.
