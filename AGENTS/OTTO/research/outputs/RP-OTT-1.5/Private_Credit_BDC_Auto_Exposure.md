# **Systematic Risk and Structural Vulnerabilities in Private Credit Auto Exposure: A Comprehensive Analysis of Business Development Companies and Specialized Finance Vehicles**

The migration of credit intermediation from traditional banking institutions to the private credit ecosystem represents one of the most significant structural shifts in global finance since the 2008 financial crisis. This evolution, often characterized as the "retailization" and "institutionalization" of non-bank lending, has transformed the $3 trillion private credit market into a primary pillar of corporate and specialty finance.1 Initially focused on senior secured loans to private equity-backed middle-market firms, the sector has aggressively expanded into asset-based finance (ABF) and specialty lending, with the automotive sector serving as a primary frontier for capital deployment. However, the recent and sudden collapses of subprime auto lender Tricolor Holdings, automotive parts conglomerate First Brands Group, and specialized financier PrimaLend Capital Partners have exposed deep-seated vulnerabilities in the due diligence, governance, and collateral management frameworks that underpin these private structures.3

## **The Strategic Expansion of Private Credit into Specialty Auto Finance**

The growth of private credit from a $460 billion niche in 2013 to a multi-trillion dollar asset class by 2025 has been driven by a confluence of regulatory arbitrage and shifting borrower preferences.1 Following the implementation of stricter capital requirements on traditional banks, non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs), including Business Development Companies (BDCs) and private debt funds, seized the opportunity to provide alternative credit free from the oversight of traditional banking regulators.1 This shift created what some industry leaders describe as a "race to the bottom" in lending standards, where competition for deal flow led to looser covenants and less stringent underwriting.1

Within the automotive ecosystem, private credit exposure is categorized into three primary structural archetypes: direct corporate lending to suppliers and manufacturers, warehouse participation for subprime originators, and the acquisition of residual equity tranches in asset-backed securitizations (ABS).8 The automotive sector is particularly attractive to private credit managers because it offers amortizing cash flows and hard collateral, which are intended to provide built-in downside protection compared to pure cash-flow-based lending.6 However, the complexity of these structures often obscures the underlying risks, particularly when collateral integrity is compromised by fraudulent activity.

### **Mapping BDC Portfolio Exposure to the Automotive Sector**

As of the second quarter of 2025, total assets in the BDC sector reached approximately $554 billion, with private credit accounting for nearly 60% of this expanding pool.12 While many top-tier BDCs maintain diversified portfolios across 20 to 30 industries, the concentration in specialty finance and automotive-related credits has become a focal point for risk assessment.13

The exposure to the First Brands Group bankruptcy serves as a critical indicator of how specific funds are impacted by a single large-scale default. While the aggregate BDC exposure to First Brands was a modest $237 million—representing only 0.05% of total industry AUM—the concentration within certain smaller vehicles created significant NAV volatility.8 For instance, Great Elm Capital Corp (GECC) held exposure representing 21% of its net book value, a level of concentration that exposes shareholders to catastrophic loss in the event of a total credit failure.15

Approximately $2.7 billion of First Brands loans are distributed across 517 CLO vehicles, many of which are held in the portfolios of larger BDCs.8

## **Case Study: The Tricolor Holdings Liquidation and Structural Fraud**

The collapse of Tricolor Holdings, a subprime auto lender and retailer based in Irving, Texas, provides a stark example of how aggressive growth can mask systematic fraud in the absence of rigorous external oversight. Tricolor filed for Chapter 7 bankruptcy liquidation on September 10, 2025, following a sudden shutdown of its 60 retail locations and the termination of over 1,000 employees.15

### **The Mechanics of the $800 Million Double-Pledging Scheme**

The Tricolor bankruptcy was the culmination of a multi-year fraudulent scheme that federal prosecutors allege began as early as 2018\.5 The core of the misconduct involved "double-pledging," where the company used the same vehicle loans to secure multiple lines of credit from different lenders simultaneously.5 By August 2025, Tricolor had allegedly pledged approximately $2.2 billion in collateral while holding only $1.4 billion in actual assets, resulting in an $800 million shortfall.5

Lenders, including major institutions like JPMorgan Chase and Fifth Third Bank, relied on borrowing base certificates and servicing reports that were systematically manipulated by Tricolor executives.5 Recorded conversations captured senior leaders discussing ways to explain discrepancies to lenders, often blaming software issues or creating misleading narratives about loan deferments.5 The fraud was only uncovered after Fifth Third Bancorp disclosed potential irregularities in a large loan portfolio, triggering a liquidity crisis that led to the Chapter 7 filing.5

The Tricolor case highlights the limitations of traditional asset-based lending (ABL) controls. When a borrower manages its own data and provides its own audits, the risk of misinformation increases exponentially.11 The fallout has rippled through the auto finance industry, forcing banks and private credit firms to brace for substantial losses while investors in securities backed by these loans have seen valuations plunge.5

## **Case Study: The First Brands Group $2.5 Billion Receivables Fraud**

If Tricolor demonstrated the risks of consumer subprime lending, the First Brands Group bankruptcy exposed the vulnerabilities of the industrial supply chain and off-balance-sheet financing. First Brands, an automotive parts supplier owning major brands such as FRAM, TRICO, and Autolite, filed for Chapter 11 protection on September 28, 2025, with liabilities exceeding $10 billion.19

### **Forensic Analysis of Fabricated Invoices**

Post-petition investigations by restructuring advisors at Alvarez & Marsal revealed a "Ponzi-style" operation where the company allegedly fabricated and inflated invoices to secure billions in financing.22 The investigation confirmed that approximately $2.5 billion in factored invoices could not be matched to actual inventory sales.22 In one instance cited in court documents, a sales invoice for $179.84 was altered to $9,271.25—a fifty-fold increase—to bolster the company's borrowing base.24

The complexity of First Brands' capital structure was a primary feature used to conceal the fraud. The company utilized extensive off-balance-sheet arrangements, including invoice factoring and supply chain finance, to access capital without reporting it as traditional debt.20 This created an illusion of financial health while the company was struggling with a massive liquidity crisis and $9 billion in debt against only $12 million in cash.20

| Financing Category | Amount ($B) | Risk/Issue Identified |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| On-Balance-Sheet Obligations | $6.1 | Term loans held by BDCs and CLOs |
| Factoring Liabilities | $2.3 | Fabricated invoices and double-pledging |
| Off-Balance-Sheet Missing Funds | $2.3 | Unaccounted-for liabilities |
| Supply Chain Finance | $0.8 | Hidden cash commitments |

The collapse was accelerated by "side-letter" arrangements between the company and certain lenders that allowed transactions to occur outside existing covenant limits, adding further opacity to the capital structure.21 Institutional exposure was significant, with Jefferies' Point Bonita Capital fund holding $715 million and UBS's O'Connor unit holding over $500 million in receivables-linked debt.8

## **Structural Vulnerabilities in Warehouse and Residual Participations**

The transmission of credit risk within the private credit auto ecosystem is deeply tied to the specific structural positions held by lenders. Private credit funds often participate in "warehouse" facilities, which are medium-term credit lines used to accumulate loans before they are securitized into ABS.10 While these are often first-lien positions, they are highly sensitive to the accuracy of the underlying collateral data.

### **The Role of Warehouse Participation**

Warehouse participation is viewed by many private credit managers as a "key competitive advantage," providing them with the ability to influence the terms of the eventual CLO or ABS issuance.10 However, these facilities are primarily secured by the contractual cash flows of the assets, and if those assets are non-existent or double-pledged, the security interest is effectively worthless.9

In the subprime auto sector, companies like Consumer Portfolio Services (CPS) depend heavily on these warehouse facilities as interim financing for their contract purchases.26 As of late 2024, CPS maintained two such facilities with a total capacity of $535 million.26 For BDCs, providing this type of leverage offers attractive yields but requires forensic-level monitoring of the originator's internal controls.

### **ABS Residuals and the "Mark-to-Myth" Challenge**

At the other end of the spectrum are residual interests, the most subordinated tranche of a securitization. Investors in residuals are entitled to the "excess spread"—the difference between the interest collected from auto borrowers and the interest paid to senior bondholders.27 While residuals offer high returns in a stable economy, they are the first to be wiped out during periods of rising delinquencies or falling recovery rates.

The valuation of these residual interests is a significant point of contention. Because they are not liquid or publicly traded, BDCs and private credit funds value them using internal models (Level 3 assets).4 Critics argue that these valuations often remain static or are even marked up through "accounting alchemy" while the underlying credit fundamentals are deteriorating.28 For example, Main Street Capital has been accused by short-sellers of inflating its NAV by 40% through aggressive fair value accounting of its "subprime" portfolio.28

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **First-Lien Warehouse** | Lower (Theoretical) | 1st | High loss if borrowing base is fraudulent |
| **Mezzanine Tranche** | Medium-High | Intermediate | Absorbs losses after residuals are gone |
| **ABS Residual (Equity)** | Very High | Last | Immediate wipeout upon rise in defaults |

## **Case Study: PrimaLend Capital Partners and the BHPH Sector**

PrimaLend Capital Partners, based in Plano, Texas, filed for Chapter 11 on October 22, 2025, providing a clear view into the specialized "Buy Here Pay Here" (BHPH) dealer finance segment.29 PrimaLend provided revolving lines of credit to BHPH dealerships based on the value of their Retail Installment Sales Contracts (RISCs).31

The BHPH model is structurally different from traditional auto finance because the dealership acts as both the retailer and the lender to subprime consumers.31 PrimaLend's role as a "lender to the lenders" created a layered leverage structure. When consumer delinquencies rose in late 2024 and 2025 due to inflation and higher unemployment, the pressure flowed upward through the dealerships to PrimaLend.1

## **Contagion and Transmission Risks in the Financial Plumbing**

The central question facing the private credit industry in 2026 is whether these defaults signal a broader contagion. The risk is not merely the loss of capital in individual loans, but the potential for a self-fulfilling downturn in credit as allocators flee the asset class.

### **Bank-NBFI Linkages and Back-Leverage**

While private credit is often described as "separate" from the banking system, the two are deeply interconnected through "back-leverage." Banks are exposed to private credit providers via subscription facilities and warehouse lines to the funds themselves.38 As of late 2024, the largest U.S. banks had committed approximately $95 billion to private credit vehicles.39

The "cockroach" theory, popularized by Jamie Dimon, suggests that when one high-profile failure is discovered, more are likely to follow.1 If multiple frauds are uncovered across the specialty finance space, banks may pull back on the leverage they provide to private credit funds, creating a liquidity squeeze.22 The Fed's Financial Stability Report has explicitly cited the "isolated bankruptcies" of an auto parts supplier and a subprime lender as examples of risks that could amplify spillovers between banks and non-banks.24

### **The Redemption Squeeze and BDC Pricing**

In the fourth quarter of 2025, several high-profile private credit BDCs saw a 200% increase in redemption requests.7 Ares Strategic Income Fund, Blackstone Private Credit Fund, and Oaktree Strategic Credit Fund all reported quarter-over-quarter increases in redemptions, with requests reaching up to 4.89% of NAV.7

| BDC Fund | Redemption Requests (% of NAV) |
| :---- | :---- |
| Ares Strategic Income | 2.71% |
| Blackstone (BCRED) | 3.50% |
| Oaktree Strategic Credit | 4.89% |

This withdrawal of capital creates a feedback loop: increased redemptions force funds to limit new lending or sell assets, which can lead to further NAV markdowns and more redemptions. The public BDC market serves as a leading indicator, where prices often trade at significant discounts to NAV during periods of stress.8 In September 2025, top-tier BDCs traded down by more than 10%, reflecting a "mild collective panic" and social contagion triggered by the news of the Tricolor and First Brands bankruptcies.19

## **Regulatory and Macroeconomic Headwinds**

The private credit auto ecosystem is also being shaped by shifting political and economic realities. The demographic focus of subprime lenders like Tricolor—primarily lower-income Hispanic households—made them particularly sensitive to changes in federal policy.1

### **Tariffs and the Cost of Credit**

First Brands Group's bankruptcy statement explicitly blamed Trump's tariffs for driving up the cost of imported materials, which undermined its margins and contributed to its collapse.1 For the broader automotive sector, a potential economic slowdown and the "lag effect" of high interest rates continue to pressure leveraged borrowers.12 While the Federal Reserve's rate cuts in late 2025 provided some relief, the benefits have yet to fully materialize for borrowers with heavy PIK-paying debt loads.12

| Macro Factor | Impact on Auto Finance / Private Credit |
| :---- | :---- |
| **High Interest Rates** | Increases borrower default risk and compress interest coverage |
| **Tariffs** | Drives up production costs for parts suppliers (e.g., First Brands) |
| **Immigration Policy** | Impacts the customer base for subprime lenders (e.g., Tricolor) |
| **Used Car Prices** | Declining values reduce recovery rates on repossessed vehicles |

The "retailization" of private credit is another area of regulatory focus. President Trump's August 2025 executive order directing federal agencies to facilitate access to alternative assets in 401(k) plans represents a massive new source of capital.2 However, this expansion also increases the systemic stakes of failures. Regulators are concerned that retail investors and 401(k) participants may not fully understand the "Level 3" valuation risks and the lack of transparency inherent in the private credit market.2

## **Conclusion: Lessons for the Next Phase of Specialty Finance**

The collapses of Tricolor, First Brands, and PrimaLend serve as "autopsy reports" on an industry that, in its pursuit of rapid growth, occasionally neglected the fundamentals of credit discipline.3 The lesson for private credit managers is that asset-backed finance is not a "risk-free" alternative to corporate lending; rather, it requires a different and more forensic set of underwriting skills.

Moving forward, the private credit market is likely to undergo a "flight to transparency".11 Investors are already demanding more frequent independent audits, direct access to real-time reporting from originators, and a more critical evaluation of "side-letter" arrangements and off-balance-sheet vehicles.11 Managers who can demonstrate a technology-driven monitoring approach and rigorous collateral validation will likely differentiate themselves as the asset class matures.11

While the "cockroaches" found in 2025 have shaken investor confidence, the fundamental drivers of private credit—bank retreat and the need for institutional yield—remain intact. The resilience of the sector will depend on whether it can successfully transition from an era of "Mark-to-Myth" accounting to one of "Institutional-Grade" execution. As the credit cycle turns, the distinction between managers who conducted surface-level due diligence and those who performed forensic scrutiny will determine the ultimate winners and losers in the specialty finance arena. The private credit auto exposure landscape is currently at a tipping point; the failures observed are not just isolated misfortunes but signals of a market testing its limits.2 Only through increased precision and accountability can the sector avoid turning a series of idiosyncratic defaults into a systemic financial crisis.
