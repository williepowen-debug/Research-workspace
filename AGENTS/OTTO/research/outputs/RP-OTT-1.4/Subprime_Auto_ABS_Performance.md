# **Market Analysis of U.S. Subprime Automobile Asset-Backed Securities: Performance, Structural Integrity, and Systematic Risk Assessment (2025-2026)**

The U.S. subprime automobile asset-backed securities (ABS) sector is currently navigating a period of unprecedented volatility and structural testing. As of late 2025 and entering early 2026, the market is characterized by a stark bifurcation between the prime and subprime segments, with the latter exhibiting delinquency and loss metrics that mirror, and in some categories exceed, the levels observed during the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) of 2008-2009. The fundamental question confronting investors, rating agencies, and originators is whether the current deterioration in credit performance is a temporary normalization following pandemic-era stimulus or a harbinger of a broader systemic failure driven by misrepresented collateral and the exhaustion of consumer financial buffers.

The macroeconomic environment has significantly shifted the "payment hierarchy" for the subprime consumer. While historically the auto loan was prioritized due to the necessity of vehicle access for employment, the combination of stagnant real wage growth for lower-income earners and the resumption of federal student loan payments has introduced new pressures. Furthermore, the collapse of high-profile subprime lenders such as Tricolor Holdings has exposed potential flaws in the "trust but verify" model of securitization, raising concerns about the veracity of loan-level representations and the effectiveness of warehouse lender oversight.

## **Current ABS Performance: Quantitative Analysis and Vintage Trends**

The quantitative performance of subprime auto ABS reflects a deep-seated stress in the lower-credit-score obligor base. Annualized net losses (ANL) in the subprime segment reached 10.13% in October 2025, a significant escalation from the 9.40% recorded in September 2025 and 9.50% in October 2024\. This trend is exacerbated by a precipitous decline in recovery rates, which reflect the softening values of used vehicles in the wholesale market.

### **Performance Dashboard by Vintage (2021-2024)**

The performance of auto ABS is highly vintage-dependent, with the 2022 and 2023 cohorts exhibiting the most significant deviations from initial loss expectations. These vintages were originated during a period of peak vehicle prices and high loan-to-value (LTV) ratios, leaving many borrowers with negative equity as car prices normalized.

| Vintage Year | Month of Seasoning | Cumulative Net Loss (CNL) (%) | 60+ Day Delinquency (%) | Comparative Trend |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 2020 | 31 | 13.91 | 4.31 | Strong (Stimulus Benefit) |
| 2021 | 41 | 21.30 | 5.64 | Weakening (Post-Stimulus) |
| 2022 | 31 | 22.42 | 6.80 | Worst Performing (Peak Pricing) |
| 2023 | 19 | 16.59 | 6.41 | Moderately Stressed |
| 2024 | 7 | 4.60 | 5.92 | Improving (Tighter Underwriting) |

The 2022 vintage remains the outlier in terms of credit deterioration. At 31 months of seasoning, its 22.42% CNL is substantially higher than the 19.34% recorded by the 2021 vintage at the same age. This deterioration is attributed to the combination of high acquisition prices and the withdrawal of pandemic-related support programs. Conversely, early data for the 2024 vintage suggests a potential stabilization, as lenders implemented more stringent underwriting criteria in late 2023\.

### **Recovery Rates and Prepayment Speeds**

Recovery rates, which serve as a critical secondary defense against net losses, have struggled to maintain historical averages. In July 2024, subprime recovery rates plummeted to 34.97%, the lowest July level in the history of several composite indices going back to 2006\. By October 2025, recoveries remained subdued at 35.87%.

Prepayment speeds have also shifted. High interest rates have discouraged voluntary prepayments (refinancing), while involuntary prepayments (defaults) have surged. Absolute Prepayment Speeds (ABS) as modeled by agencies now reflect a environment where borrowers are "trapped" in high-interest loans, increasing the probability of eventual default.

## **Performance by Originator: Institutional Stress and Fraud Contagion**

The subprime auto ABS market has been defined by idiosyncratic shocks in late 2025\. The most prominent event was the collapse of Tricolor Holdings, a Texas-based subprime lender focusing on the "underbanked" demographic. The collapse revealed systemic weaknesses in private credit and warehouse lending, with allegations that the company double-pledged loans and generated $800 million in fictitious collateral.

### **Originator Risk Table**

Institutional performance varies significantly, with large, diversified issuers like Santander Consumer USA (SDART) maintaining relative stability compared to "deep subprime" specialists.

| Originator | Program / Trust | Risk Status | Key Observations |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Tricolor Holdings | TAST | Default / Failed | Liquidation (Chapter 7); Fraud investigation |
| Exeter Finance | EART | Underperforming | ECNLs raised repeatedly (2023-2024 deals) |
| First Help Financial | FHF | Underperforming | Ratings downgraded; Caps lowered to 'A' |
| Global Lending Svcs | GSAR | Stable | Performance within historical norms |
| Santander (SHUSA) | SDART | Leading | Stable performance; upgraded classes in Aug 2024 |
| First Investors | FIAOT | Stressed | Performance worse than original ECNLs |

The failure of Tricolor resulted in the downgrade of 34 classes across seven transactions to 'D (sf)' following missed interest payments in September, October, and November 2025\. This liquidation event has forced warehouse lenders like JPMorgan, Barclays, and Fifth Third to prepare for losses totaling billions. Furthermore, First Brands Group, an automotive aftermarket supplier with significant accounts receivable pledging, entered Chapter 11 with $11.6 billion in liabilities, further unnerving the ABS community.

### **Exeter Finance Case Study**

Exeter Finance provides a notable example of a major issuer navigating severe performance headwinds. During November 2025 surveillance, S\&P Global Ratings increased the Expected Cumulative Net Loss (ECNL) for six Exeter transactions (2023-1 through 2024-4). For the 2023-1 series, the revised ECNL was moved from 21.00% to 26.50%. Despite these revisions, many senior tranches were upgraded or affirmed due to the structural deleveraging of the transactions, which increases credit support as a percentage of the remaining pool balance.

## **Rating Agency Actions: Surveillance and Sector Outlook**

Rating agencies have shifted to a more defensive stance regarding subprime auto ABS, particularly for non-prime and deep subprime cohorts. While the absolute number of downgrades remains small relative to the thousands of outstanding ratings, the frequency and severity of negative actions increased in 2025\.

### **Rating Action Log (2025)**

The surveillance cycle for 2025 reflects a "cleansing" of the market, where idiosyncratic failures are isolated while the broader sector remains supported by structural protections.

| Date | Agency | Trust / Series | Action | Reason |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Nov 2025 | S\&P | Tricolor (TAST) | Downgrade to 'D (sf)' | Missed interest payments |

Rating agencies have noted that the 2022 and 2023 vintages are the primary sources of concern. KBRA’s 2026 outlook indicates that while overall issuance remains strong, the "non-prime auto and solar" sectors will continue to experience the most rating volatility. The KBRA Stability Ratio (KSR) for non-investment grade (Non-IG) ABS stood at 90.8%, significantly lower than the 98% for investment-grade ratings, highlighting the concentration of risk in the lower tranches.

## **Spreads and Pricing: Market Volatility and Investor Sentiment**

The pricing of subprime auto ABS in the secondary market became highly reactive to the Tricolor collapse and broader negative consumer news in late 2025\. While prime spreads tightened at the start of 2026, subprime spreads experienced a "sharp widening" in the second half of 2025\.

### **Spread Chart: Benchmark 3-Year AAA/BBB/BB**

The spread differential between prime and subprime has widened, reflecting a higher risk premium for the latter.

| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Prime AAA | 50 | 42 | 38-45 | Tightening |

Subprime BBB-rated tranches saw the most aggressive movement, widening by 50 basis points in a single month following the Tricolor bankruptcy and weak earnings from major used-car retailers. This widening has created what some analysts call "attractive entry points" for discerning investors who differentiate between idiosyncratic fraud and fundamental credit trends.

### **Secondary Liquidity and Issuance Momentum**

Despite the stress, the primary market remains robust. Year-to-date issuance in 2025 reached $358 billion, a 16.8% increase compared to 2024\. Trading activity also accelerated, with average daily volumes climbing 17% to $2.15 billion. This suggests that liquidity is sufficient for tier-1 originators, even as secondary spreads for lower-tier tranches remain wide.

## **Structural Protections: Testing the Resilience of Credit Enhancement**

The architecture of auto ABS deals—built on subordination, overcollateralization (OC), and excess spread—is being tested by the current loss environment. In many 2022 and 2023 transactions, the built-in "hard credit enhancement" has been the only factor preventing downgrades of senior notes.

### **Structural Testing Data (Typical Subprime Trust)**

Deals like the United Auto Credit Securitization Trust (UACST) 2026-1 illustrate the current levels of protection required to obtain investment-grade ratings.

| Class | Rating | Initial CE (%) | Target OC (%) | Excess Spread (est.) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Class A | AAA (sf) | 65.10 | 25.00 | 12.52% |
| Class B | AA (sf) | 55.90 | 25.00 | 12.52% |
| Class C | A (sf) | 47.80 | 25.00 | 12.52% |
| Class D | BBB (sf) | 37.40 | 25.00 | 12.52% |
| Class E | BB (sf) | 33.30 | 25.00 | 12.52% |

Excess spread remains a vital first line of defense, but it is being compressed by higher funding costs and the "haircut" applied to it in stressed scenarios. Furthermore, many transactions feature "performance triggers." For example, some structures release excess collateral only if cumulative collection rates remain above 90%. If delinquencies spike further, these triggers could stop the release of cash to equity holders, instead diverting it to pay down note principal—a "structural self-correction" that protects bondholders at the expense of originators.

## **Risk Assessment: Is a "Downgrade Wave" Likely?**

The probability of a widespread "downgrade wave" is currently assessed as moderate but concentrated. While total market stress is high, several factors mitigate the risk of a systemic rating collapse.

### **Vintages and Originators Most at Risk**

* **2022 Vintage:** The most critical risk factor. The combination of peak-market LTVs and normalizing collateral values makes this vintage highly susceptible to further negative revisions.  
* **Deep Subprime Specialists:** Smaller originators or those with high concentrations in the "unbanked" or "no-FICO" segments (similar to Tricolor) face the most intense liquidity and surveillance scrutiny.  
* **Subordinate Tranches (BB/B):** These classes lack the massive attachment points of senior notes. If CNL expectations continue to rise toward 30% for troubled deals, these tranches will face imminent downgrades.

### **The Argument Against a Systemic Wave**

Analysis by Janus Henderson and Bank of America suggests that the Tricolor and First Brands events were idiosyncratic failures rather than a systemic consumer credit meltdown. Prime borrowers (75% of the total auto ABS market) remain on solid footing, with 30-day delinquencies steady at 1.9%. The "bankruptcy remote" nature of ABS structures ensures that even if an originator fails, a backup servicer can continue to manage the collateral for the benefit of investors.

## **Summary of Findings**

The subprime auto ABS market in 2026 is a study in structural resilience versus consumer fatigue. While the headline delinquency rates are at 2009 levels, the structural protections embedded in modern ABS—including significantly higher subordination levels and more robust excess spread—provide a buffer that was largely absent in the pre-2008 era.

However, the "misrepresented collateral" risk highlighted in the original request is no longer theoretical. The Tricolor fraud has demonstrated that systematic misrepresentation can bypass traditional credit modeling. For the 2026 outlook, success will depend on the market's ability to differentiate between fundamentally sound (though stressed) consumer credit and fraudulent or unsustainable business models. Investors should expect continued spread volatility and concentrated downgrades in the 2022 vintage, while the 2024-2025 cohorts may offer a recovery path as the effects of tighter underwriting begin to materialize in the data.
