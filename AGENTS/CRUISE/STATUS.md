**We went looking for what is actually happening in the cruise business, and we found the discounting itself — not an analyst's description of it.** Norwegian ran the **first Semi-Annual Sale in its history on 8 July: half off every cruise**, free tips thrown in, Caribbean sailings eligible straight through 2027. That is the company's own announcement, not a rumour. A **third bank — Truist, on 23 July — called it "the most aggressive post-Covid outside of Black Friday promos"** and cut Norwegian to Hold. That matters because Truist wrote **seven weeks before** the two banks we were relying on, so the worry that our evidence was two copies of one opinion is largely answered.

**And we found why Norwegian is doing it.** It added **8.9% more capacity** in the spring quarter — roughly **four times Carnival's rate** — and it is **the only one of the three losing occupancy** (down 1.5 points). A cruise line with a lot more cabins, fewer people in them, and its own filing admitting it is behind on bookings has exactly one lever left before the ship sails: cut the price. Empty cabins are worth nothing once the ship leaves.

**But the same day Truist cut Norwegian, it RAISED Carnival's price target — from $29 to $31.** That is the strongest evidence yet **against** the part of our thesis that matters for the trade. Our story has two halves: *Norwegian is discounting* (now proven), and *that discounting drags Carnival's prices down too* (still unproven, and the analyst who documented the discounting best clearly does not believe it). **On balance this makes a bet against Carnival less attractive, not more.**

**Two cautions that could each change the call.** The **"50% off" is a marketing number, not a price cut** — Norwegian's own example is a saving of "from $280" for two people on a week-long cruise, which is nearer 10–15%, and about what the free tips alone are worth. **We do not know what the half-price applies to, so we cannot size the damage.** And **part of Norwegian's weakness is its own building programme, not the American consumer** — Royal Caribbean added 4.9% capacity in the same quarter, kept its ships just as full, and **charged more**. That argues against reading Norwegian's trouble as a signal about household spending, which is how this desk has been treating it.

**One thing that is not in doubt: Norwegian is the weak one financially, and now we can measure it.** It holds cash and credit worth **10% of its debt**, against **26% at Carnival and 30% at Royal Caribbean**. Royal Caribbean borrowed **$1.25 billion for eight years in August at a very ordinary rate, unsecured**, with 34 banks in the deal. Norwegian cannot borrow that way at all and four of its sixteen ordered ships only happen **if it finds the financing**.

**Nothing here is a trade. No money moved, no level set, both rows still WATCH.** Carnival closed at a 52-week low, **ten cents above our red line** — and a breach is a measurement, not an action. **Everything still points at Tuesday 29 September**, and Norwegian's sale ran *inside* the quarter Carnival reports that day — so **Carnival's pricing line is the first place the contagion could show up, if it exists at all.** Read it in order: **yield guide → share count (net of 69.1M shares issued and ~30M bought back) → dollar fuel line → earnings last.**
