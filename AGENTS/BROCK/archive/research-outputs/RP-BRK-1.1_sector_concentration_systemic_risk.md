# **Sector Concentration and Systemic Risk Assessment in the Business Development Company Ecosystem: Evaluating Software Overweight and Portfolio Contagion**

The institutionalization of private credit has fundamentally altered the capital structure of the American middle market, with Business Development Companies (BDCs) serving as the primary conduit for this transformation. As the industry moves into 2026, the aggregate fair value of investments held within BDC portfolios has reached approximately $481.7 billion, representing a significant expansion from the previous cycles.1 This growth, however, has not been uniform across all industrial sectors. Instead, a pronounced concentration in software and technology has emerged, driven by the perceived stability of recurring revenue models and high enterprise value-to-EBITDA multiples. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the top 10 BDCs by assets under management (AUM), examining their sector-specific concentrations, identifying entities with excessive exposure to technology, and mapping the portfolio overlap that creates systemic risk across the private debt landscape.

## **The Macro-Financial Environment and Asset Expansion**

The BDC market has demonstrated remarkable resilience despite a period characterized by interest rate volatility and shifting inflationary pressures. Total BDC assets grew by 32.5% over the past year, as private credit managers aggressively captured market share from traditional banking institutions.2 This expansion is underpinned by a floating-rate structure that, as of early 2026, continues to provide high single-digit and low double-digit yields, often between 9% and 11%.3 However, the net interest spread, which peaked in 2023, has begun to moderate, contracting to approximately 3.8% as of the second quarter of 2025\.1

The industry's concentration is increasingly skewed toward a handful of mega-cap managers. The top 10 managers now control approximately 66% of the total assets in the BDC space, even though they represent only 16% of the total fund count.1 This concentration of management power implies that the credit underwriting standards and sector preferences of a few large investment committees dictate the risk profile for the majority of retail and institutional capital invested in this asset class.

## **Sectoral Concentrations and the Technology Overweight**

The software sector has become the dominant industry exposure for modern BDC portfolios, accounting for roughly 20% of total assets, or approximately $100 billion, by the third quarter of 2025\.7 The attraction to software stems from its historical performance as a defensive, high-margin business with mission-critical services that supposedly insulate it from cyclical downturns. However, recent volatility in software equities and the accelerating adoption of artificial intelligence have led to a re-evaluation of these valuation assumptions.7

### **Defining the Dangerous Threshold**

In the context of credit risk, a "dangerous" concentration is often defined as any single sector exposure exceeding 30% of the total portfolio fair value. This threshold is critical because it limits the manager's ability to absorb sector-specific shocks without impacting the fund's net asset value (NAV) or its ability to cover dividend distributions. Several major BDCs currently approach or exceed this threshold, particularly when sub-sectors like IT services and healthcare technology are aggregated with enterprise software.

## **Detailed Fund Deep Dives: Sector Dynamics and Credit Quality**

The risk of software overweighting cannot be assessed by percentage alone; one must look at the specific credit metrics of the underlying borrowers, the vintage of the loans, and the manager's history of managing distressed situations.

### **Ares Capital Corporation (ARCC)**

As of its third-quarter 2025 filing on October 28, 2025, Ares Capital managed a diversified portfolio of 587 companies with a fair value of $28.7 billion.9 Software and Services remains its largest concentration at 24%, yet management has proactively defended this position by highlighting that their software portfolio companies have a weighted average EBITDA exceeding $350 million.9 This focus on upper-middle-market, market-leading firms is a strategic attempt to mitigate the risks associated with smaller, venture-stage software companies that are more vulnerable to AI-driven disruption. ARCC's software loans carry a conservative average loan-to-value (LTV) ratio of 36%, and notably, as of late 2025, zero software positions were on non-accrual status.9

The credit quality of ARCC is further supported by falling non-accruals, which dropped to 1.8% at cost and 1% at fair value by September 30, 2025\.9 This is significantly below the BDC industry average of 3.8%.9 By shifting toward first-lien senior secured loans, which now represent 59% of the portfolio (up from 50% in mid-2024), ARCC has adopted a more defensive posture in anticipation of potential market turbulence.17

### **Blue Owl Capital Corporation (OBDC)**

Blue Owl’s flagship BDC maintains a technology exposure of approximately 18% when combining internet software (12%) and healthcare technology (6%).11 However, the broader Blue Owl platform manages more concentrated vehicles, such as Blue Owl Technology Finance Corp (OTF), which holds a staggering 82.5% concentration in tech-related investments.18 For the public vehicle (OBDC), the focus remains on the upper-middle market, with weighted average portfolio company EBITDA of approximately $229 million and an interest coverage ratio of 1.9x.11

A significant trend for Blue Owl in 2025 has been its merger activity, specifically the definitive agreement to merge OBDC with OBDC II, intended to enhance long-term shareholder value and streamline management.19 Despite a slight decline in NAV to $14.89 in late 2025, driven by unrealized depreciation on a small number of names, OBDC remains a staple for investors seeking exposure to large-cap private credit.19

### **Golub Capital BDC (GBDC)**

Golub Capital presents a higher risk profile regarding sector concentration, with a combined software and IT services exposure of 32%.10 This exceeds the dangerous overweight threshold, making GBDC highly sensitive to shifts in the software lending market. Management has mitigated this risk through a highly diversified borrower base, consisting of over 333 portfolio companies with an average investment size of only 0.30% of the total portfolio.10

GBDC’s performance in 2025 was marked by a decline in non-accrual investments to 0.3% at fair value, demonstrating effective credit monitoring despite the heavy sector bet.20 The fund’s structure, which includes a leading fee arrangement (1.0% management fee and 15% incentive fee), provides a buffer for shareholders during periods of yield compression.20

### **FS KKR Capital Corp (FSK)**

FS KKR has historically struggled with higher non-accrual rates, which stood at 3.5% on a cost basis in early 2025\.21 Its software exposure is relatively modest at 16.2%, but its reliance on payment-in-kind (PIK) interest is a major red flag.12 FSK’s PIK income reached 27% of its total interest, suggesting that a significant portion of its borrowers are not meeting their interest obligations with cash.22

Management has utilized KKR's proprietary "CreditQB" technology to identify risks across its 2,400+ issuer library, yet the fund’s NAV has faced consistent pressure, decreasing to $21.99 by September 2025\.12 FSK’s focus on the upper-middle market (companies with $50-$150 million+ of EBITDA) is intended to provide resilience, but the high PIK levels indicate that even larger borrowers are feeling the strain of "higher-for-longer" interest rates.12

## **Portfolio Overlap: The Anatomy of Systemic Contagion**

One of the most critical findings in the BDC space is the high degree of portfolio overlap among the largest funds. Because the debt requirements for large private equity buyouts—such as those led by Thoma Bravo and Vista Equity Partners—often exceed $1 billion, these loans are frequently "clubbed" among the largest BDCs.

### **The Pluralsight Restructuring**

The restructuring of Pluralsight, a workforce development software firm owned by Vista Equity, serves as a textbook example of systemic overlap. The original $1.175 billion recurring revenue loan was financed by a consortium of BDCs including Blue Owl, Ares, Goldman Sachs, Oaktree, and Golub Capital.24 When the company’s performance faltered, these lenders were forced into a debt-for-equity swap that extinguished $1.3 billion in debt.26

The impact of the Pluralsight event was felt across multiple BDC portfolios simultaneously, leading to synchronized markdowns. For instance, in early 2024, Blue Owl marked its position down to 83.5 cents on the dollar, a move mirrored by other consortium members.24 This highlights that diversification across different BDC managers may be illusory for investors, as the same "problem child" assets are often held by all major players.

### **Influence of Mega-Sponsors**

BDCs are heavily reliant on relationships with top-tier private equity sponsors. In 2025, Thoma Bravo and Vista Equity Partners remained the primary originators of software deal flow. Thoma Bravo's 16th flagship fund raised over $24 billion, while Vista Equity Partners closed its eighth flagship at $20 billion.27 BDCs like Ares and Sixth Street often act as the primary lenders to these sponsors' portfolio companies, such as Dayforce and SolarWinds.28 This creates a causal relationship where the underwriting discipline of the private equity sponsor becomes just as important as the lender's own analysis.

## **Sub-Sector Risks: Vertical SaaS and Cybersecurity**

While the broad category of "software" is often treated as a monolith, the risks within specific sub-sectors vary significantly.

### **Vertical SaaS and Value Compression**

Vertical SaaS companies, which serve specific industries like healthcare or retail, have seen a plethora of deals in 2025\.28 However, these companies are currently facing valuation pressure as artificial intelligence begins to automate the very workflows these software tools were designed to manage. A "pricing reset" in the secondary loan markets for public software credits has already begun, with sector loans ending January 2026 down nearly 3%—the worst showing since late 2022\.8 This reset may force BDC managers to recognize further markdowns in their private software holdings, particularly for companies that have failed to integrate AI effectively.

### **Cybersecurity and Mission-Critical Stability**

Cybersecurity remains a preferred sub-sector due to its non-discretionary nature. Lenders like Hercules Capital have been active in this space, providing commitments to firms like Akeyless for machine identity security.29 While these companies carry high growth potential, they are also prone to intense competition and rapid technological obsolescence. The risk for BDCs in this space is not a lack of demand, but the potential for borrower margins to be eroded by the need for constant R\&D spending, which can impair their ability to service high-interest debt.

## **PIK Income and the Masking of Credit Stress**

A significant and often overlooked risk in the BDC sector is the utilization of Payment-in-Kind (PIK) interest. PIK interest allows a borrower to defer cash payments by adding the interest to the loan's principal balance. While this provides cash flow flexibility, it also results in a compounding debt load that can lead to a "debt trap" for the borrower.

As of mid-2025, PIK interest income across the top 15 BDCs reached a run rate of $1 billion.22 However, the concentration of PIK is highly skewed. Prospect Capital (PSEC) reported a 35% PIK loan concentration, while FS KKR (FSK) and Blackstone (BXSL) reported 27% and 20% respectively.22 These levels are significantly higher than those of more conservative peers like Main Street Capital (MAIN). High PIK levels often precede non-accruals, as they indicate that the borrower’s organic cash flow is insufficient to support its current capital structure under prevailing SOFR rates.

## **The Specialized Vanguards: Hercules and Trinity**

Specialized BDCs like Hercules Capital (HTGC) and Trinity Capital (TRIN) present a different risk profile. These funds focus almost exclusively on technology, life sciences, and venture-backed companies.30 Hercules, for instance, surpassed $25 billion in cumulative debt commitments by 2025, supporting over 700 companies.29

While their tech concentration is intentionally above the 30% "dangerous" threshold, their underwriting model is built around warrant participation and equity kickers, which provide a different return profile than traditional middle-market lending. Hercules reported a robust 17.4% return on average equity in late 2025, driven by strong investment income and liquidity exceeding $1 billion.32 However, their performance is tethered to the health of the venture capital ecosystem; a prolonged IPO drought or a pullback in VC funding can quickly lead to an increase in non-accruals and valuation markdowns.

## **Systemic Risks in the Non-Traded and Private BDC Cohort**

The growth of private, non-traded BDCs—managed by firms like Apollo, Blackstone (BCRED), and Blue Owl (OCIC)—has added a new layer of complexity to the market. These funds now manage approximately 68% of the total assets in the BDC market, up from just 17% in 2020\.1 Private BDCs often employ higher leverage and have a more concentrated focus on the upper-middle market.34

Blackstone’s private vehicle, BCRED, reports that 90% of its portfolio is invested in "historically lower-default rate sectors" including software, professional services, and healthcare.35 While this sounds defensive, it also means that a systemic downturn in these specific sectors would be devastating for the fund’s valuation. Furthermore, private BDCs reprice their loans less frequently than public ones, which can create a "smoothing" effect that hides volatility until it is too late for investors to exit at NAV.37

## **The 2026 Outlook: Convergence or Divergence?**

As we move deeper into 2026, the BDC sector is expected to undergo a period of "continuing divergence in operating performance".34 High-rated, large BDCs are likely to leverage their scale and sponsor relationships to navigate the cycle, while smaller BDCs with poor sector diversification may face significant headwinds.

### **Interest Rate Lag and Borrower Fatigue**

While the Federal Reserve began cutting rates in late 2025, the benefit to borrowers is not immediate due to lag effects.2 Many software companies are still servicing debt at the peak of the rate cycle, leading to "borrower fatigue." This fatigue is most evident in the rising number of amended loan terms and the move to PIK structures. If rates do not fall rapidly enough, we can expect a second wave of non-accruals in the software sector, particularly for those companies that were highly levered in the 2021 vintage.

### **Dividend Sustainability**

The focus for the sector is shifting from credit quality to dividend coverage.38 As portfolio yields decline in an easing interest rate environment, BDCs that are currently over-levered or have high non-cash income may be forced to cut distributions. Analysts expect that future portfolio growth from increasing M\&A and LBO activity may partially offset lower earnings, but the "golden age" of 12%+ BDC yields may be coming to a close.34

## **Conclusion: Strategic Assessment of Concentration Risk**

The Business Development Company industry stands at a crossroads, with technology and software serving as both a primary growth engine and a major systemic vulnerability. The actual sector concentrations revealed in 2025 and 2026 filings show that while the broad industry exposure to software is manageable at approximately 20%, individual funds like Golub Capital BDC (GBDC) and specialized venture lenders carry concentrations that exceed the dangerous 30% threshold.

The mapping of portfolio overlap, particularly in large-scale software recapitalizations like Pluralsight, proves that the private credit market is more interconnected than previously understood. This interconnectedness means that a default by a major software borrower will not be an isolated event but will resonate across the balance sheets of the industry's largest managers simultaneously.

For institutional investors and advisors, the priority must be on identifying managers with high cash-to-PIK ratios and disciplined underwriting in the vertical SaaS and cybersecurity sub-sectors. While the upper-middle-market focus of Ares and Blue Owl provides a layer of protection through the scale of their borrowers, the underlying valuation compression in the software market suggests that NAV volatility will remain a persistent challenge through 2026\. The funds that successfully navigate this period will be those that prioritize capital preservation over "yield chasing" and leverage their deep sector expertise to separate the winners from the losers in the rapidly evolving technology landscape.
