# US State & Local (Muni) Fiscal Stress 2026 — Where, How Severe, and the Data-Center Question
**Date:** 2026-06-27 | **Mode:** Thesis | **Confidence:** High (the three quantified liabilities + named issuers) · Low (the AI/data-center fiscal call — thin verified evidence)

**Flag:** REQ-DEWEY-20260627-003 (WALTER Phase-2.8) · Origin: SIG-W-20260627-024 (Ciccarone $1.03T US-cities deferred-infrastructure liability; muni-fiscal coverage-gap candidate).

---

## Key Finding

US state & local fiscal stress in 2026 is **concentrated, not systemic — and largely structural/slow rather than acute.** The two largest headline liabilities are *not* near-term catalysts: the **$1.03T "infrastructure burden" is a deferrable, single-firm depreciation estimate, not a debt-like obligation**, and **aggregate pension funding is actually *improving*** (third straight year, ~77–82% funded). The genuinely acute stress is **issuer-specific** — NYC and Chicago — plus the **ARPA spend-by-end-2026 cliff** now drawing down reserves. The one thread that is both unresolved and faster-moving is the **AI/data-center fiscal question**, which is state-/structure-specific and has the thinnest evidence. On the routing decision: the evidence leans toward **keeping muni-fiscal as a CARL (national)/CORAL (FL) channel**, with the data-center→utility cost-shift the only piece that might merit a focused watch (and it bridges to AI_INFRA_CAPEX, not just consumer).

## Evidence

### (a) The $1.03T figure — real, but a deferrable replacement *estimate*, not a liability
The cited ~$1.03T is Merritt Research/Investortools' proprietary **"Infrastructure & Capital Asset (ICA) Burden,"** authored by Richard Ciccarone, **released May 5 2026**, from audited financials of ~2,000 US cities [INSTITUTIONAL: Investortools press release; Bond Buyer]. It is the **inflation-adjusted cost to replace the already-consumed-but-still-in-service portion of infrastructure** — a deferred-replacement obligation, not a bond or cash liability. The firm frames it as **2.6× cities' direct debt (~$390B), 4.1× their unfunded pensions (~$255B), and 1.8× record 2025 muni issuance ($579.9B)** — "larger than debt + pension + OPEB combined."

**DEWEY caveat (load-bearing):** this is a **proprietary, self-described "first-of-its-kind" single-firm metric, NOT a GASB/GAAP balance-sheet liability** — GASB explicitly holds that deferred maintenance does not meet the definition of a liability. The multiples are apples-to-oranges (a *cumulative* replacement obligation vs *point-in-time* debt/pension and *single-year* issuance). Ciccarone himself redid the methodology after finding "too many flaws." **Treat $1.03T as the firm's modeled estimate of a slow-burn structural risk, not a debt bomb.** Cities can defer maintenance for years with no accounting consequence; the risk is service disruption / emergency repair, not default.

Worst-positioned 500k+ cities on the metric: **San Jose CA, Portland OR, Indianapolis IN, Baltimore MD.** Best-positioned: **Jacksonville FL, San Francisco CA, Columbus OH** (Jacksonville best-positioned is relevant to the CORAL/FL channel).

### (b) Pension underfunding — *improving* in aggregate; concentrated in five states
Aggregate public-pension funding **rose for a third straight year** to roughly **77–82% funded in FY2025**, with the spread a pure methodology artifact [PRIMARY/ACADEMIC: Equable, CRR-Boston College, NASRA, Pew]:

| Source | Funded ratio | Basis | Unfunded |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equable State of Pensions 2025 | **82.5%** (FY2025e, ↑ from 78.0% FY2024) | market value | **$1.27T** (↓ from $1.54T FY2024) |
| CRR / Boston College | 77.7% (FY2025e, ↑ from 76.2% FY2023) | — | — |
| NASRA Public Fund Survey | 76.7% (FY2024, ↑ from 75.7%) | smoothed actuarial | ~$1.5T |
| Pew (state-only) | 74% (FY2023) | — | $1.32T gap |

Do **not** blend these — NASRA uses smoothed actuarial value, Equable market value, and FY2025 figures are projections on ~9.5% returns. CRR's own framing: the improvement is **modest given the S&P rose >40% over the same period**. **The widely-cited Pew "$1.3T / FY2022 / ~66% of own-source revenue" figure is the trough vintage** (driven by 2022 market losses) and was adversarially refuted as "most recent" — superseded by FY2023/FY2025 data.

**Stress is concentrated, not uniform:** funded ratios range **28%–108%** across plans (NASRA median 77.8%); CRR's bottom third is ~58% funded vs 95% for the top third. The **distressed tier below 60% funded (FY2023): Kentucky 50.1%, Illinois 50.9%, New Jersey 53.5%, Connecticut 57.8%, South Carolina 59.9%** [Equable via ai-CIO — the weakest single claim here, 2-1 verify, but the *grouping* is durable across Pew/Reason/Fed]. Contribution adequacy slipped: states meeting the net-amortization benchmark fell from 49 (2022) to 41 (2023), nine ran negative amortization 2019–2023. **Net: pension is a structural concentration story, not an aggregate-deterioration story — and it cuts *against* escalation.**

### (c) The post-ARPA cliff — the one genuinely acute, near-term thread
ARPA SLFRF ($350B total) had to be **obligated by end-2024 and spent by end-2026** — so the cliff bites FY2026–FY2027 budgets *now* [PRIMARY: NASBO Spring 2026 Fiscal Survey; NY OSC]. The mechanism that makes it dangerous: many governments spent one-time funds on **recurring** costs, so expiry exposes the structural deficit underneath.

- **State reserves are drawing down:** total balances peaked at $437B (38.8% of GF spending) in FY2023, dipped to $397B FY2024, rebounded to $405B FY2025, and are projected to fall to **$324B (23.5%) FY2026 → $274B (20.2%) FY2027** — still above the FY2019 pre-recession 14.0%, so a cushion remains.
- **Stress is emerging but narrow:** **five states made mid-year spending cuts on revenue shortfalls in FY2026 — the most since FY2021.**
- NY example of the local distribution: $4.8B to localities ex-NYC (counties 47%, cities 31%, towns/villages 22%).

### Named most-stressed large issuers (the acute layer)
- **New York City — the most acutely stressed large GO issuer.** All three of **Moody's (Mar 11), Fitch (Mar 20), and KBRA (Mar 20) revised NYC's GO outlook to negative within ~9 days** in early 2026; S&P kept stable but issued a warning. Ratings held (Aa2/AA/AA/AA+). Moody's available **fund-balance ratio is estimated to turn sharply negative at −3.31% in FY2026** (from 0.01% FY2025, 2.5% FY2021) — near-elimination of the budgetary cushion [PRIMARY: NYC Comptroller; Moody's/KBRA releases].
- **Chicago — second most-stressed.** **S&P revised its GO outlook to negative Nov 5 2025** (affirming BBB; ≥1-in-3 downgrade chance within two years) on structural imbalance, depleted reserves, and pension policy. It entered FY2026 with a **~$1.15–1.2B gap (largest ex-COVID in its history)** and the proposed budget **halved the supplemental pension contribution to $120.2M** (from $259.6M) — though Council later mandated the full ~$260M and the city made a ~$130M half-payment in Jan 2026 [PRIMARY: Chicago COFA; S&P].

### (d) AI / data-center: net fiscal positive OR credit risk? — state-specific, and the thinnest evidence
This is the half of the brief with the **weakest verified evidence** — the deep-research pass extracted data-center sources but none survived its verification budget, so this rests primarily on DEWEY's own pass + fetched-but-unverified sources. Treat as **[NEWS/INSTITUTIONAL], Low confidence, contested.** The honest answer is **"it depends on the tariff/abatement structure":**
- **Net-positive case:** property- and sales-tax revenue + utility rate-base growth for host munis — **but routinely eroded by the tax abatements used to attract them** (e.g., Texas' ~$1B data-center sales-tax break; several states now putting data-center tax breaks "on the chopping block") [NEWS: Texas Tribune; Stateline; Tax Foundation].
- **Credit-risk case:** if projected load is canceled/withdrawn, utility build-out costs **shift to existing ratepayers** as **stranded assets** (Wisconsin ratepayers still owe ~$1B on shuttered plants; WI regulators *rejected* a cost-share proposal) [NEWS: Wisconsin Watch; Utility Dive].
- **Mitigants emerging in 2026:** dedicated large-load rate classes (Virginia), minimum-billing demand charges, and **10–15-year take-or-pay tariffs** that make the data center pay regardless of operation — these shift risk back to the operator [NEWS/INSTITUTIONAL: American Action Forum; Latitude Media].
- **No specific Moody's/S&P muni-utility downgrade analysis was confirmed** in either pass — a real data gap.

**Verdict:** strong take-or-pay large-load tariffs → risk sits with the data center (net-positive for the muni); weak/no tariff or aggressive abatements → ratepayer/stranded-asset exposure. Not a uniform answer, and it bridges to the **AI_INFRA_CAPEX** cluster as much as to consumer.

## Counter-Evidence (mandatory)

**The case that muni-fiscal is NOT a near-term stress catalyst (strong):**
1. **Pension — the largest quantifiable liability — is improving**, not deteriorating, and remains structurally concentrated in the same five states it always has been.
2. **The $1.03T is not a liability** in any accounting sense; it is deferrable for years.
3. **Reserves remain above pre-pandemic norms** even mid-drawdown (20%+ vs 14% in FY2019).
4. **Stress is concentrated** — five states with mid-year cuts, two distressed large cities (NYC, Chicago) with idiosyncratic drivers (NYC migrant/labor costs; Chicago pension policy). This reads as *narrow*, not systemic.
5. Pew's own data shows states *collectively* still met the net-amortization benchmark and **paid down $36.3B of pension debt in 2023**.

**The case that it IS building stress:** the ARPA cliff is real and near-term; the structural-deficit-masked-by-one-time-money mechanism is exactly how fiscal cliffs become budget crises; NYC's near-zero-to-negative fund balance and Chicago's pension backsliding are genuine deteriorations; and the data-center cost-shift is a new, fast-moving channel that could surprise.

**What would escalate this:** the FY2026 mid-year-cut count rising materially in the next NASBO survey; a NYC or Chicago *downgrade* (not just outlook); broad-based GO/revenue spread widening (MSRB/EMMA — not surfaced here); a state forced to raise the discount rate / skip an actuarial contribution. **What would de-escalate it:** continued strong pension returns; reserves stabilizing; ARPA-cliff budgets closing without service collapse.

## Source Quality Assessment

**Overall: High on (a)(b)(c) and named issuers; Low on (d) data-center.** Pension rests on **four independent primary/academic datasets** (Equable, CRR-BC, NASRA, Pew) that converge on direction; ARPA on **NASBO** (the authoritative state-budget source) + NY OSC; named issuers on **rating-agency primaries** (Moody's/S&P/KBRA + city comptroller reports). The $1.03T is **single-firm institutional** — solid as a published estimate, but down-weight any framing of it as a GAAP liability.

**Weaknesses / gaps:**
- **Data-center fiscal question is the major gap** — zero claims survived the deep-research verification budget; this report's (d) is the thinnest section and needs a dedicated pass if it becomes load-bearing.
- **No muni-bond spread data (MSRB/EMMA)** and no breadth of rating actions beyond NYC/Chicago — the issuer-distress picture is two cities deep, not a survey.
- **No property/sales-tax-base dynamics** quantified (CRE reassessment risk to property-tax bases was in scope, not surfaced) — though PROMPT-4 supplies the residential property-tax leg (+3.7% bills into −1.7% values).
- **Methodology/vintage mixing risk** on pensions — kept separate above; do not blend the 76.7%–82.5% range into one number.
