## Key Finding

**The question contains two different clocks, and conflating them is the error to avoid: the magazine is measured in WEEKS, the replenishment in YEARS.** At the campaign expenditure rate estimated by CSIS, remaining Patriot stocks represent **≈21–30 days of peak-rate combat**, while restoring the pre-war inventory takes **≈2.4–2.5 years at the 2025 production rate** (CSIS puts actual replenishment at ~mid-2029). The single most decision-relevant number is the ratio between them: **combat consumed interceptors 16–22× faster than they are built**, so a 39-day campaign burned **1.7–2.3 full years of production.**

**For FALCON specifically: your formulation is correct and now has arithmetic behind it.** You flagged the Patriot↔Caine "join" as *"a risk vector, NOT a fact — different inventories, shared production line."* **Both halves hold.** The Greek-operated battery at Yanbu draws on a *different* national inventory than USCENTCOM's, so US expenditure does not deplete it. But PAC-3 MSE comes off **one line at Camden, Arkansas**, and with global output at ~620/yr against a US-only shortfall of ~1,500, **allied replenishment competes with US restocking on a multi-year queue.** The practical consequence: **a consumable battery that fires its magazine at Yanbu cannot be resupplied on any tactically relevant timescale.** The join is real — it just operates on a scale of years, not within a campaign.

**"Physical floor under the pause": the thesis survives, but the mechanism is not what the phrase implies.** The floor is a **replenishment-rate floor, not an inventory floor.** Nothing in the public record shows the US unable to resume; CSIS's own framing is that renewed war would *test* diminished inventories, and the Chairman reportedly assessed major operations as still feasible. What the arithmetic supports is narrower and more durable: **resumption at the observed 2026 tempo is magazine-limited in weeks and cannot be re-stocked inside the conflict.**

## 1. The supply side — what the budget documents actually say

*All figures in this section are [PRIMARY] from justification books pulled this session, or [INSTITUTIONAL] from named corporate statements.*

### PAC-3 MSE (the Patriot interceptor)

| Year | Output | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 2024 | ~500 produced | [INSTITUTIONAL: Lockheed Martin public statements] |
| 2025 | **620 delivered** | [INSTITUTIONAL: Lockheed Martin, record production year] |
| ~2027 | **650/yr** capacity target | Army contract awarded Oct 2024 |
| end-2030 | **2,000/yr** target | **Jan 2026** framework agreement with the Department of War, seven-year |

Production is centred at **Camden, Arkansas**. Lockheed has also announced **PAC-3 ACE**, a variant explicitly aimed at cutting per-round cost, which is itself evidence that cost-per-interception is being treated as a binding programmatic problem rather than an academic one.

⚠️ **The 2,000/yr figure is a 2030 goal, not current capacity, and it should not be used in any near-term arithmetic.** The tripling is contracted but unbuilt; the FPRI industrial-base literature treats scaling of this kind as the hard part, not the contracting.

### THAAD — and a structural change that matters more than the quantities

**🔴 MDA requests NO THAAD funding in FY2027.** Verbatim, twice, from the FY2027 MDA procurement justification:

> *"No funding is requested in FY 2027 for THAAD."* — and the associated **−248 cFTEs** reduction is *"due to the transition and transfer of the THAAD operations and sustainment requirements to the U.S. Army in support of the Secretary of War's Military Defense Strategy."*

**THAAD has moved from MDA to the Army.** This is an accounting/organisational migration, not a capability cut — but it means **THAAD procurement is no longer visible in the Defense-Wide books**, and it lands in the one book I could not obtain (§Source Quality).

Two THAAD numbers I *could* recover, both [PRIMARY]:

- **Lead time, and it is the sharpest supply datum in this report:** *"The additional **twelve (12) THAAD Interceptors** procured with mandatory (reconciliation) funding **will be delivered with Lot 18 in FY 2029.**"* [MDA FY2026 procurement book] — **incremental THAAD ordered under reconciliation funding arrives in FY2029.** Roughly a three-to-four-year lag between money and missile.
- **Force structure and tempo:** *"Army force structure for THAAD missile defense is currently set at **eight (8) batteries with six (6) launchers** operated by 90 Soldiers. The battery is organized to conduct **120-day deployments** (45 days of entry operations and 75 days of 17-hour/day combat operations). **During actual deployments, batteries have been operating at 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year operational tempo.**"*

**That last sentence is an official statement, in a budget justification, that the force is operating past its design tempo.** It is the closest thing in the public record to a documented answer on sustainability, and it is about *crews and equipment*, not magazines.

### SM-3 — a terminated production line, restarted under war pressure

This is the clearest war-driven industrial-base reversal visible in the primaries, and it is only visible by reading two years' books against each other.

**FY2026 book (planned termination):**
> *"Decrease from FY 2025 to FY 2026 reflects the **termination of SM-3 Block IB new production** as a result in a shift in Department priorities. After FY 2024, the Department will **discontinue the SM-3 Block IB procurements** in favor of SM-3 Block IIA… FY 2025 provides $110 million Congressional Plus Up for procurement of **six (6)** SM-3 Block IBs and $85 million for critical investment spares to support repair and recertification of fielded SM-3 Block IB AURs **in anticipation of supply chain attrition following termination of new production.**"*

The same book carries a line item for **"SM-3 Block IB Production Shutdown costs."**

**FY2027 book (the reversal):**
> FY 2025 restated as *"$110.000 million CPU for procurement of **eight (8)** SM-3 Block IBs, **$252.838 million in supplemental for procurement of twenty-one (21) SM-3 Block IBs**"* — and the FY2027 request is **"procurement of up to seventy-eight (78) Standard Missile-3 (SM-3) Block IB missiles,"** alongside **"up to one hundred fourteen (114) SM-3 Block IIA missiles,"** each described as *"consistent with validated requirements and industrial base capacity."*

**Trajectory: terminate after FY2024 → 6 → 8 + 21 supplemental → up to 78 requested.** A line the Department had formally decided to shut down, and whose supply chain it expected to suffer "attrition," is being run at ~78/yr. Corroborating operational pressure appears in the O&M books: **"+12 cFTEs for SM-3 Block IA/IB recertifications in support of USCENTCOM military operations"** and **"+19 cFTEs for equipment reset of one THAAD battery after returning to the U.S. in FY 2026."**

## 2. The consumption side — and why no official number exists

**There is no published US government figure for interceptors expended or remaining. Those inventories are classified.** Everything in this section is a **named third-party reconstruction**, and it is labelled as such throughout. This is the honest boundary of the commission, and I am not going to launder an estimate into a fact.

**The originating analysis is CSIS (Mark Cancian and Chris Park), derived — per their own description — from "Pentagon budget materials for fiscal year 2027 and publicly released information."** [INSTITUTIONAL]

| Quantity | CSIS estimate |
|---|---|
| Pre-war Patriot inventory | ~**2,330** |
| Patriot fired, 39-day campaign | **1,060–1,430** |
| Patriot remaining, mid-2026 | **759–827** (≈**−65% to −67%**) |
| THAAD remaining | ~**250** |
| Replenishment complete | ~**mid-2029** (a little over three years) |

CSIS's own published article states the current position more conservatively still — **Patriot "under 1,000," THAAD "about 250"** — without the granular ranges the press coverage carries. ⚠️ **The 2,330 / 759–827 / 1,060–1,430 figures reached me via press reporting of a CSIS product I could not locate directly; the "under 1,000 / about 250" figures are from CSIS's own page.** Treat the granular ranges as one notch weaker than the round ones.

**On the Caine anchor the commission named:** what is in the public record is **reported private briefing, via anonymous sources** — that the Chairman cautioned major operations remained militarily feasible but could *"dangerously deplete"* CENTCOM interceptors. **This is not an officially stated expenditure rate**, and the commission's framing of it as "officially stated" is, on the evidence I can find, too strong. I found no on-the-record DoD statement of expenditure quantities.

**⚠️ Counter-attribution, load-bearing:** the figures were publicly disputed — **Mike Waltz, US Ambassador to the UN**: *"The U.S. military has everything it needs to conduct this campaign as effectively as it needs to be. The people that are leaking this nonsense deserve to be in jail."* Note precisely what this is and is not: **a denial of adequacy, from a diplomat rather than the Pentagon, that does not engage any specific number.** It is not a technical rebuttal, but it is the only official pushback on the record and it belongs in the file.

## 3. The arithmetic

*Supply inputs [PRIMARY/public]. Consumption inputs [INSTITUTIONAL, CSIS]. **The arithmetic is only as good as the CSIS inputs** — it is arithmetic on an estimate, not on a measurement.*

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Production, 2025 actual | 620/yr = **1.70/day** |
| Combat expenditure, 39-day campaign | 1,060–1,430 = **27.2–36.7/day** |
| **Burn : build ratio** | **16.0× – 21.6×** |
| Campaign consumption in production-years | **1.71 – 2.31 years of output, in 39 days** |
| **Magazine depth at peak rate** (759–827 remaining) | **≈21 – 30 days of combat** |
| Shortfall vs pre-war baseline | **1,503 – 1,571** interceptors |
| Replenishment at 620/yr | **2.4 – 2.5 years** |
| Replenishment at 650/yr (contracted) | **2.3 – 2.4 years** |
| Replenishment at 2,000/yr (end-2030 goal) | 0.8 years — **not available before 2030** |

⚠️ **The replenishment figures are FLOORS.** They assume every interceptor built goes to US restocking — no ongoing expenditure, no foreign military sales, no training. CSIS's ~mid-2029 (~3-year) estimate is *longer* than my floor, which is what you would expect once those claims are added, and the two are consistent rather than competing.

**So: WEEKS *and* YEARS, and they answer different questions.**
- *"Can the US sustain the observed tempo?"* → **weeks.** ~21–30 days at peak rate.
- *"Can the US rebuild before it must?"* → **years.** 2.4–3, and no faster path exists before 2030.
- *"Is supply the binding constraint?"* → **it is a rate constraint, not a stock constraint.** The stock is currently adequate for a short campaign; what cannot be fixed inside any plausible conflict window is the **rate**.

## 4. What this does to FALCON's live logic

**(a) The Patriot↔Caine join — CONFIRMED as you framed it, now quantified.** Different inventories (Greek national stock ≠ USCENTCOM stock), shared production line (Camden, AR). **The join is a queue effect on a multi-year clock, not a within-campaign depletion.** Concretely: US expenditure does **not** draw down the Yanbu battery. But because global PAC-3 MSE output is ~620/yr against a ~1,500 US shortfall, **allied resupply and US restocking are competing for the same constrained line for years.** Your "consumable" characterisation of the Yanbu battery is the right one, and the reason is now explicit: **it is consumable because it is unreplenishable on a relevant timescale**, not because the magazine is unusually small.

**(b) "Right about the PORT and wrong about the LINE" — the arithmetic reinforces this and I would put it more strongly.** Point defence of a fixed high-value terminal is exactly the case where a finite magazine is *most* efficiently used. The 1,200 km of Petroline is not a magazine problem at all; **no interceptor inventory, at any of the production rates above, changes that exposure.** Interceptor economics is the wrong lens for the Petroline vector entirely.

**(c) "Physical floor under the pause" — survives, re-specified.** The public record does not support "the US cannot resume." It supports: **resumption at 2026 tempo burns ~16–22× the build rate, is magazine-limited in weeks, and cannot be re-stocked before ~2029.** That is a floor made of *replenishment rate*, and it is a durable structural fact rather than a contingent inventory reading — which makes it more useful to you, not less, because it does not decay as stocks are quietly topped up.

## Counter-Evidence

1. **The consumption figures are contested and unofficial.** The US government has not confirmed any of them, and the one on-the-record official response called the reporting "nonsense." Every "weeks" conclusion inherits this uncertainty; the "years" conclusion does not (it rests on production rates, which are public).
2. **CSIS's own published page is more conservative** ("under 1,000," "about 250") than the granular ranges in press coverage. If the true remaining stock is nearer 1,000, magazine depth extends proportionally.
3. **Expenditure rate is not a constant.** The 27–37/day figure is a *peak-campaign* rate. CSIS notes Iranian *"launches have so far been at a lower rate than before the ceasefire"* — at a lower salvo rate the magazine stretches, potentially by a large multiple. **The 21–30 day figure is a floor scenario, not a forecast.**
4. **Interception is not one-for-one, and doctrine is adjustable.** Shot doctrine (rounds per incoming) is a policy variable; CSIS explicitly anticipates that diminished stocks may push the US and partners to *"take more risks with interceptions"* — i.e. the consumption rate is partly a choice, and it is already expected to fall.
5. **Supply is being actively fixed, with real money.** $95B munitions request in FY2027 plus $21B in war supplemental; the PAC-3 line is contracted to triple; a terminated SM-3 line has been restarted at scale. **The rate constraint is recognised and funded — the constraint is calendar, not budget.**
6. **My arithmetic layers a public production rate onto a third-party inventory estimate.** If CSIS's pre-war baseline is wrong, both the shortfall and the replenishment years move with it.

**What would disprove the finding:** an official DoD statement of expenditure or remaining inventory materially above the CSIS range would break the "weeks" leg outright. Conversely, evidence that PAC-3 MSE deliveries have already exceeded 650/yr would compress the "years" leg. **The single highest-value unobtained document is the Army's FY2027 missile procurement justification book** — it now holds both PAC-3 MSE *and* THAAD quantities, and would replace the corporate-statement production figures with appropriated quantities.
