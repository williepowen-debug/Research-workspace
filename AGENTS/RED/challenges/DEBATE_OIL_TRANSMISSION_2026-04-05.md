## THE CASE THAT RED IS RATIONALIZING (The Prosecution)

### 1. The 2008 Analog Is Cherry-Picked

RED and CARL cite 2008 as proof that oil can break consumers before employment breaks. But 2008 is the EXCEPTION in oil shock history, not the rule:

| Oil Shock | Peak | Employment? | Consumer Credit? |
|-----------|------|:-----------:|:----------------:|
| 1973 Arab embargo | $12 ($80 adj) | Recession followed | Yes, but WITH employment |
| 1979 Iran Revolution | $40 ($140 adj) | Recession followed | Yes, but WITH employment |
| 1990 Gulf War | $41 ($85 adj) | Mild recession | Minimal credit stress |
| 2008 Financial Crisis | $147 | Massive job losses | Massive — but HOUSING bubble was the amplifier |
| 2011 Arab Spring | $128 | No recession | No credit stress |
| 2014 Collapse | $115→$26 | Energy sector only | Contained |
| 2022 Russia-Ukraine | $125 | No recession | No credit stress |

**Base rate:** In 7 oil shocks since 1973, only 2008 saw consumer credit break before employment. And 2008 had a housing bubble that amplified the oil shock — subprime borrowers with ARMs and 100% LTV were uniquely vulnerable to ANY income shock. The oil was the match, not the dynamite.

**2026 doesn't have a housing bubble.** LTVs are lower, most mortgages are fixed-rate, and housing supply is still constrained. The 2008 amplification mechanism doesn't exist.

**Counter-example: 2022.** Brent hit $125. Gas hit $5.02. Consumer savings were HIGHER than now. No consumer credit crisis. No recession. Why? Because employment held. The market absorbed the oil shock through behavioral adjustment (driving less, substitution) without cascading to credit.

### 2. Current Delinquency Was Rising BEFORE Oil Spiked

CARL shows CC 90+ DQ at 12.7% and subprime auto 60+ DQ at 6.9% ATR. These numbers are alarming. But look at the timeline:

- CC DQ trend: rising since Q2 2023. Three years of normalization from pandemic lows.
- Auto DQ: rising since Q3 2023. Same pandemic normalization pattern.
- Student loan DQ: directly attributable to SAVE plan ending and payment restart, not oil.

**The oil shock hit in March 2026.** The delinquency trend predates it by 2.5 years. CARL is attributing acceleration to oil, but the base trend was already there. The question isn't whether DQ is high — it is. The question is whether OIL is causing incremental DQ beyond the existing trend. That's not proven.

### 3. $4.08 in 2026 ≠ $4.15 in 2008

Inflation-adjusted, $4.08 in 2026 is roughly $3.00-3.20 in 2008 dollars (CPI has risen ~28% since 2008). The REAL gasoline burden on households is significantly lower than the 2008 peak.

Additionally, the US is now the world's largest oil producer (~13M bpd). In 2008, the US produced ~5M bpd. The windfall profits from $141 crude flow to US energy companies, energy workers, and energy-state economies (TX, ND, NM, PA, OK). At the MACRO level, high oil prices are a wash for the US in a way they weren't in 2008.

**The losers are concentrated:** Low-income consumers with long commutes in non-energy states. But the macro GDP impact is structurally smaller than 2008 because domestic production offsets imports.

### 4. The 30-60 Day Timeline Is Suspiciously Convenient

CARL's DQ conversion research says energy shocks → visible delinquency in 30-60 days. Gas breached $4 on March 31. So 30-60 days = May 1 to May 31.

RED's thesis says near-dated puts are "less vulnerable than Apr 2 assessment suggested because oil transmission is faster than employment transmission."

Notice the convenience: RED needed a mechanism that (a) makes the thesis work despite strong employment, AND (b) works on a timeline that saves the near-dated puts. The dual transmission model delivers exactly what RED needed, exactly when RED needed it.

**Occam's razor:** RED was facing a framework that said "downgrade to 65%." RED invented a new framework that said "stay at 77%." The simplest explanation is motivated reasoning, not analytical breakthrough.

### 5. Hamilton Says 4 Quarters, Not 30-60 Days

BRENT's NOPI research uses Hamilton coefficients showing GDP drag of -3.0 to -4.9pp from the current oil shock. But Hamilton's OWN DATA says the peak impact is at a **4-quarter lag**. That puts the peak at Q1 2027.

CARL claims consumer stress "front-loads" ahead of Hamilton's timeline. But this is a modification of Hamilton, not Hamilton. The actual empirical evidence for oil → GDP transmission shows a multi-quarter lag, not a 30-60 day snap.

If Hamilton is right about timing, the near-dated puts are wrong regardless of which transmission path you choose.

### 6. Three Cost Vectors = Triple-Counting the Same Consumer

CARL's thesis cites three independent vectors: gas, food CPI, UI exhaustion. But:

- Gas and food CPI are correlated (fuel costs drive food transport costs). Not independent.
- UI exhaustion affects ~4,500 workers in FL Wave 1. That's a rounding error in a 160M labor force.
- The "simultaneous firing" thesis overstates the shock by counting correlated impacts as independent.

True independent stress = gas + student loan restart. That's a real double hit. But it's not new — student loan payments restarted in late 2024. The marginal shock is gas only.

**Acceleration test:** If the oil shock adds incremental stress, we should see DQ growth rates inflect in April-May 2026 compared to the 2023-2025 trend. The counterfactual isn't "flat DQ" — it's "DQ continuing at +0.3%/quarter trend." If we see +0.6%/quarter in Q2 2026, that's oil-attributable.

### Q3: Hamilton vs CARL timing — who is right?

**Answer:** Both are right about different things.

**Hamilton (4-quarter lag):** Measures GDP response. GDP = consumption + investment + government + net exports. The consumption component of GDP responds with a lag because households cut durables first, then non-durables, then services — and each takes time to flow through quarterly accounts.

**CARL (30-60 day DQ response):** Measures credit distress at the **margin**. The household at 99% debt-to-income doesn't wait 4 quarters to miss a payment. They miss it in 30-60 days when the gas bill hits.

These are different populations:
- **Hamilton's 4-quarter lag:** Applies to the median household (debt-to-income 90%, some savings, cuts back gradually)
- **CARL's 30-60 day snap:** Applies to the 90th percentile debt-to-income household (subprime auto, high-rate CC, zero savings)

The K-shape means both are true. The median household shows Hamilton lag. The marginal household (who holds subprime debt and drives the DQ statistics) shows CARL acceleration.

**Resolution:** Don't use Hamilton to time CARL's credit stress. Use Hamilton for GDP forecasts, CARL for DQ forecasts.

### Q4: Net producer offset — how to model winners vs losers?

**Answer:** The offset is real but **concentrated and lagged**, while the stress is immediate and dispersed.

**Winners (energy producers):**
- TX, ND, NM, PA, OK
- High-income workers (energy sector wages)
- Corporate profits (delayed pass-through to labor via buybacks, not wages)

**Losers (gas consumers):**
- Lower-48 states, all income levels, especially <$50K households
- Immediate impact (weekly fill-ups)
- No offsetting income

**Net macro effect:** Positive for GDP (exports, energy investment), neutral-to-negative for consumption (losers spend more on gas, winners save/invest). But the **distribution** is what matters for credit stress:

- Subprime auto borrowers are OVERWHELMINGLY in loser geographies (Southeast, Midwest, rural everywhere)
- Subprime auto borrowers are UNDERREPRESENTED in winner geographies (Permian Basin workers drive trucks, don't finance Honda Civics at 18% APR)

**The offset doesn't reach the credit-stressed cohort.** It's a macro wash but a micro concentration.

**Quantification:** If 15% of households are net energy winners and 30% are significant gas losers, the GDP impact is neutral but the credit impact is negative (losers default, winners don't underwrite new subprime loans to compensate).
