Private-Label CMBS Holds Half of Delinquent Debt Share This Article October 15th, 2026 | 9 min. read Subscribe Stephen Buschbom Print/Save as PDF A total of $11.6 billion of the $635.82 billion universe of securitized multifamily loans was delinquent in August, and half of that delinquent debt sits in private-label commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) deals, despite private-label CMBS accounting for just 12% of the total outstanding balance. Table 1: U.S. Multifamily Delinquency, August 2026 Outstanding Balance Delinquency Rate Delinquent Balance Share of Delinquent Balance Private-Label $75.48B 7.69% $5.80B 50.20% Freddie Mac $518.99B 0.80% $4.15B 35.90% CRE CLO $41.35B 3.88% $1.60B 13.90% Total $635.82B $11.60B Source: Trepp Table 1 shows that private-label securities hold $75.48 billion of multifamily debt and have a 7.69% delinquency rate, leaving $5.8 billion of delinquent debt, or 50.2% of the total. Freddie Mac, meanwhile, has $518.99 billion of multifamily debt outstanding, or 82% of the total tracked, but its 0.80% delinquency rate translates to $4.2 billion of delinquent debt, or 35.9% of the total. Commercial real estate collateralized loan obligations (CRE CLOs), which primarily pool floating-rate transitional loans, hold $41.3 billion of debt. Their 3.88% delinquency rate translates to $1.6 billion of delinquent debt, or 13.9% of the total. How the Distribution of Delinquent Multifamily Debt Has Shifted Table 2: U.S. Multifamily Delinquency, August 2024 Outstanding Balance Delinquency Rate Delinquent Balance Share of Delinquent Balance Private-Label $58.32B 3.30% $1.92B 27.30% Freddie Mac $476.81B 0.48% $2.29B 32.50% CRE CLO $46.80B 6.05% $2.83B 40.20% Total $581.93B $7.04B Source: Trepp The composition of delinquent multifamily debt has changed substantially. Two years ago, CRE CLOs accounted for the largest share, with about 40% of the delinquent debt, while Freddie held 32% and private-label accounted for the smallest share of delinquent multifamily debt at 27%. Changes in the composition of outstanding loans help provide context for this shift. Breaking down CRE CLO debt by property vintage highlights how the pool has evolved. Over the two years, the total CRE CLO balance fell from $46.8 billion to $41.3 billion, with debt tied to buildings constructed before 1980 falling from $16.2 billion to $6.8 billion. CRE CLO debt tied to properties built in 2000 or later increased from $19.2 billion to $28.5 billion. Meanwhile, private-label debt tied to buildings constructed before 1980 increased from $20.4 billion to $29.1 billion over the same period. Another factor is that CRE CLO managers can remove defaulted or otherwise credit-impaired loans from transaction pools. As a result, the $1.6 billion of delinquent loans currently in the $41.35 billion CRE CLO universe may understate the level of distress among loans that have passed through those transactions. While managers aren't necessarily required to remove distressed loans, they're incentivized to do so. If a CLO fails its overcollateralization test, cash that would typically be distributed to equity holders and certain subordinate noteholders can instead be diverted toward paying down senior notes. The overcollateralization test is designed to ensure that a CRE CLO has enough collateral to cover the notes it has issued. For example, a CRE CLO with $1 billion of loans and $800 million of notes would have a 125% overcollateralization ratio. If a $100 million loan defaults and is carried at 50% of par for purposes of the overcollateralization calculation, the collateral value would fall to $950 million, reducing the ratio to 118.75% and causing the deal to fail a 120% test. CRE CLOs also must meet separate interest-coverage tests, which ensure that the interest generated by the collateral is sufficient to cover interest owed on the issued notes. The Bottom Line The distribution of delinquent multifamily debt has changed substantially over the past two years. Private-label securities now account for the largest share, while CRE CLOs account for much less than they did two years ago. The change in reported delinquency may reflect not only differences in the loans entering each market but also those leaving transaction pools, particularly in CRE CLOs, where distressed loans can be removed.
