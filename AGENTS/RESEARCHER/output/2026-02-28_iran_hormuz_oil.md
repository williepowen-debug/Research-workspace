## 2. Disruption Scenarios

### 2a. Full Closure (Worst Case: ~5–10% probability near-term)
- Iran mines the strait and uses IRGC naval assets to enforce a no-passage zone
- 20 mb/d offline immediately; no replacement possible at short notice
- Oil would spike toward $150+ (1973/1979 level analog in today's market)
- **Constraint on Iran:** Full closure invites overwhelming US naval response (mine sweeping under force protection). Iran cannot sustain a full closure against US Navy for extended period — weeks at most, not months. But weeks is enough to crater global markets.
- **Likelihood reduced by:** US naval assets being pre-positioned; Iran's economic dependence on Gulf Arab transit routes surviving (their own exports need open water)

### 2b. Mining + Harassment (Most Likely Active Scenario: ~50% probability)
- Iran seeds the strait with modern influence mines (acoustic/magnetic/pressure-activated)
- Does not need to physically prevent passage — **doubt is enough**
- Even *suspected* mining forces commercial traffic to pause, insurance to spike, and re-routing to begin
- Precedent: 1988, USS Samuel B. Roberts nearly sank after Iranian mine; US operation Praying Mantis followed
- Iran's mine stockpile estimated at 5,000–6,000 units (Army Recognition, 2026), including modern bottom mines slow to sweep
- Mine clearing under missile threat is extremely difficult; US MCM force is limited
- **Duration:** Harassment campaign with mines + swarm boats could persist weeks to months

### 2c. Partial Blockade / Selective Targeting (Most Likely Initial Mode: ~40% probability)
- Iran targets specific tankers (e.g., tankers serving US allies, Israeli-linked vessels)
- Uses anti-ship missiles (Noor, Khalij Fars), IRGC speedboat swarms, shore-based systems
- Creates a "war risk zone" that de facto deters commercial shipping without full closure
- Precedent: 2019 attacks on tankers off UAE coast (4 vessels sabotaged, attributed to Iran)
- Precedent: June 2025 12-day conflict — oil briefly spiked to ~$80 but flows were not physically cut

### 2d. Infrastructure Targeting (Emerging evidence today)
- Reports of explosions at/near Kharg Island (Iran's primary export terminal — 90% of Iran crude exports)
- Reports of strikes near UAE/Kuwait infrastructure (Iranian retaliation)
- **Iran pre-moved oil stored at Kharg onto tankers in recent days** — they anticipated this scenario
- If UAE/Saudi/Kuwaiti export infrastructure is damaged in Iranian counter-strikes, volume effects are direct and immediate

## 3. Historical Precedents

### Tanker War (1984–1988)
- Iraq and Iran attacked each other's shipping to damage oil revenues
- **Over 500 vessels damaged or destroyed** across the campaign
- Physical chokepoint (Hormuz) was *never fully closed* — transit continued at reduced rates with war risk premium
- US reflagged Kuwaiti tankers (Operation Earnest Will, 1987) and provided naval escorts
- Price impact: Moderate — oil markets had slack and OPEC spare capacity absorbed much of the risk signal
- **Key lesson:** Shipping continues even under attack if insurance/military backstop exists. The chokepoint is psychological as much as physical.

### 2019 Tanker Attacks
- May 2019: 4 vessels sabotaged off UAE coast (outside Hormuz, in Gulf of Oman)
- June 2019: 2 additional tankers struck
- September 2019: Abqaiq/Khurais drone/missile attack — knocked out ~5.7 mb/d of Saudi capacity briefly (largest single-point supply shock in history)
- Brent spiked ~15% in one session after Abqaiq, then fully retraced within 2 weeks as Saudi production was restored faster than expected
- **Key lesson:** Markets can price in large shock and then rapidly discount it if supply is restored. Duration matters enormously.

### June 2025 (12-Day Conflict)
- Prior US-Iran confrontation (referenced in Reuters/Capital Economics reporting)
- Brent peaked at ~$80 before returning
- Physical Hormuz flows were not cut
- Current conflict is described as materially larger in scope

## 5. Iran's Military Capability to Disrupt Hormuz

### Assets (per Army Recognition, 2026 analysis)
- **Mines:** 5,000–6,000 stockpile including modern influence mines (acoustic/magnetic/pressure). Even partial seeding creates massive commercial disruption.
- **Anti-ship missiles:** Noor (C-802 derivative), Khalij Fars (anti-ship ballistic missile), Ra'ad — shore-based and mobile. Can threaten VLCCs at range.
- **Submarines:** 3 Kilo-class + multiple midget subs. Useful for mine-laying under cover.
- **IRGC speedboat swarms:** Asymmetric saturation attacks; hard to defend against with Aegis systems
- **Coastal defenses:** Multiple overlapping SAM layers + Fateh-series land-attack missiles

### Strategy
Iran's doctrine is **not to win naval battles but to manufacture sustained friction**:
- Make the strait "unsafe enough" that commercial traffic self-deters
- Force US to commit mine-sweeping resources under ongoing missile threat
- Bleed US interceptor inventories across multiple theater vectors
- Sustain dispersed, mobile launch platforms that survive initial US strikes

### Key Constraints
- Iran cannot sustain full closure against active US naval presence for >weeks (likely)
- US carrier air wing + Aegis destroyers can suppress IRGC surface assets rapidly
- But **mine clearing takes months** — this is Iran's most durable tool
- IRGC operates separately from regular Iranian Navy; decentralized/resilient command

### Sustainment Assessment
- **Harassment campaign (mining + sporadic attacks):** Weeks to months — sustainable
- **Active naval engagement with US:** Days to 2 weeks before force degraded
- **Full closure against US escorts:** Not viable beyond short shock (days)
- **Iran's strategic calculus:** Use just enough disruption to hurt markets/allies without triggering existential US response

## 6. Alternative Routes — Capacity Analysis

### Available Bypass Infrastructure

| Route | Capacity | Current Utilization | Notes |
|-------|----------|---------------------|-------|
| Saudi Petroline (East-West pipeline) | 5.0 mb/d capacity | ~1.9 mb/d flowing | Delivers to Yanbu (Red Sea). ~3.1 mb/d available unused capacity |
| UAE ADNOC Habshan-Fujairah pipeline | ~1.8 mb/d | Partial | Bypasses strait to Gulf of Oman |
| **Total bypass pipeline capacity** | **~2.6 mb/d** | — | EIA estimate of immediately available bypass |
| Cape of Good Hope re-routing | Unlimited (if tankers available) | Low | Adds ~2 weeks transit time; tanker fleet reallocation takes months |

### Critical Gap
- Strait handles **~20 mb/d**
- Maximum bypass pipeline capacity: **~2.6 mb/d** (EIA)
- **Gap: ~17.4 mb/d cannot be rerouted** through existing infrastructure even at maximum bypass utilization
- Cape re-routing doesn't add supply — it just lengthens transit time, effectively taking tankers off rotation and tightening the tanker market further

### Bottlenecks
- Saudi Petroline can move ~5 mb/d but currently only using ~1.9 mb/d — additional flow requires ramping operations, not trivial
- UAE pipeline terminates at Fujairah (Gulf of Oman) — fine if Hormuz is the problem, but vulnerable if Iran extends mining into Gulf of Oman approaches
- Cape routing: each VLCC reroute adds ~15–20 days to voyage; effective tanker supply drops proportionally; freight rates spike

**Bottom line on alternatives:** Even full pipeline utilization covers only ~13% of normal Hormuz throughput. There is no near-term alternative for the other 87%.

### Release Capacity
- Maximum drawdown rate: ~4.4 mb/d (physical limit of cavern extraction + distribution)
- Typical authorized release: 1–2 mb/d (politically calibrated)
- IEA coordinated release: US + allies could add ~10–15 mb/d globally for short period
- SPR is designed for **short-term shocks** (weeks to months), not structural supply elimination

### Context
- At 411 MMbbl, SPR is meaningfully recovered from Biden-era drawdown lows but still ~300 MMbbl below capacity
- A 1 mb/d SPR release = ~3.6 months before tank is empty at that rate
- SPR cannot substitute for 20 mb/d Hormuz flow — it's a bridge tool, not a solution
- IEA member nations hold additional strategic reserves; coordinated release would be announced quickly in a full closure scenario

## Synthesis & Risk Assessment

### The Core Tension
Markets are currently pricing ~$72–73 Brent (Friday close), implying significant but not catastrophic disruption. The gap between "containment" ($80) and "full disruption" ($100+) is entirely dependent on **whether physical flows through Hormuz are actually cut** versus merely threatened.

### What Matters Most for Monday
1. **Will Iran mine the strait?** — If confirmed mining, insurance/shipping self-deters and you get the disruption effect without Iran needing to fire another shot
2. **Infrastructure damage assessment** — Kharg Island, UAE/Kuwait facilities. Any confirmed hits on export infrastructure is direct supply loss
3. **US diplomatic signaling** — Does Trump offer an off-ramp, or does rhetoric escalate?
4. **Saudi/UAE response** — Do they ramp pipeline bypasses and signal increased production? This is the market's safety valve

### Probability-Weighted Monday Open Estimate
- **Base case (conflict doesn't escalate overnight):** Brent opens $75–82, trades with extreme volatility
- **Escalation case (Iran mines Hormuz or hits major export hub):** Brent $85–95+
- **Worst case (confirmed 5+ mb/d offline):** $100–120

### Historical Calibration
2019 Abqaiq was 5.7 mb/d offline → 15% Brent spike in one session, then retraced in 2 weeks.  
A **confirmed Hormuz disruption is categorically different** — it's not one producer's infrastructure, it's the whole region's export artery. No quick restoration is possible.
