II.

Filing 

On July 31, 2026, PJM submitted revisions to the Tariff and RAA to establish the 

RBP, a one-time procurement of new capacity to address resource adequacy challenges 
primarily driven by an influx of large loads.  According to PJM, the goal of the RBP is   
to obtain commitments of new RBP UCAP MW24 from eligible new resources, called 
RBP Resources, for a fixed term of up to 15 years, starting with the 2028/2029 delivery 
year at the earliest and ending no later than the 2042/2043 delivery year.25  PJM states 
that committing supply for terms of up to 15 years creates revenue certainty for the 
supply resources, generally can reduce financial and development risk, and may improve 
financing opportunities through lower capital costs or more favorable financing terms.26

Participants in the RBP are:  (1) sellers with RBP Sell Offers submitted on    
behalf of RBP Resources; and (2) PJMSettlement, Inc., acting as a counterparty on behalf 
of buyers, i.e., LSEs.27  PJM states that the RBP aims to procure capacity equal to the 
6,831 MW shortfall in the 2028/2029 BRA (Initial RBP Target), adjusted downward to 
reflect qualifying new capacity (Qualifying RBP Offset UCAP MW) serving new load 
and certain qualifying Electric Distributors that elect and meet requirements to opt out of 
the RBP (Opt-Out UCAP MW).  The Final RBP Target is the Initial RBP Target after 
downward adjustments.  Pursuant to the proposed Tariff provisions, new load is load that 
has an executed electric service agreement or transmission service agreement indicating 
that the load will come online during the 2027/2028 or 2028/2029 delivery year.28

To avoid double procurement, PJM proposes to require all available committed 

RBP UCAP MW to offer into the RPM Auctions as a price-taker, i.e., $0/MW-day 
UCAP, beginning with the first RPM Auction following the RBP commitment, for the 
applicable offered delivery year in which the term of the committed RBP UCAP MW

1.

PJM’s Existing Tariff May Be Unjust and Unreasonable 

We preliminarily find that PJM’s existing Tariff may be unjust and unreasonable 
because the existing reliability backstop provisions appear to be insufficient to prevent 
the grave resource adequacy concerns that led PJM to file the instant proposal in the first 
place.  We think it clear that PJM’s ordinary instrument for procuring sufficient capacity 
to meet PJM’s reliability requirement, the RPM, has failed to keep pace with the 
unprecedented influx of large load into the PJM Region in recent years.   

The existing Reliability Backstop Auction provision in PJM’s Tariff may be unjust 

and unreasonable because it has failed to remedy these reliability concerns.38  As PJM 
states, the Reliability Backstop Auction, which was proposed over 20 years ago and has 
never been used, was not designed to respond to the circumstances surrounding PJM’s 
current capacity shortfall.39  The Reliability Backstop Auction is triggered after three 
consecutive BRAs clear with a total unforced capacity amount that is more than one 
percent below PJM’s reserve margin.40  As stated above, PJM’s last two BRAs have 
cleared more than five percent below PJM’s reserve margin.  Moreover, once the 
Reliability Backstop Auction is triggered, PJM cannot run the auction until it “make[s] a 
filing with FERC for approval to conduct a Reliability Backstop Auction.”41

The aspects of the existing Reliability Backstop Auction discussed above call into 

question the sufficiency of the current Tariff mechanism to adequately respond in a 
timely manner to the magnitude of the resource adequacy challenges that the PJM Region

2.

Certain Aspects of PJM’s Proposal Are Just and Reasonable 

a.

Seller Eligibility Requirements, Sell Offers, and RPM 
Must-Offer Requirement 

i.

Filing 

Like offset capacity, RBP Resources (i.e., resources that bid into the RBP auction) 

must have never cleared in an RPM Auction and must have an expected in-service date 
by June 1, 2032.42  PJM, however, states that because RBP Resources have longer-term 
commitments and price certainty—as compared to resources that provide offset 
capacity—PJM proposes more restrictive criteria for RBP Resources to limit them to 
those resources that have demonstrated a high likelihood of success in reaching 
commercial operation in the near term and remaining available throughout the

iii.

Determination 

As a general matter, we find that PJM has demonstrated that its proposed 
eligibility requirements for RBP Resources are just and reasonable.  Specifically, we 
agree with PJM that its proposed eligibility requirements will limit RBP Resources to 
those resources that have demonstrated a high likelihood of success in reaching 
commercial operation and remaining available throughout the RBP commitment period, 
while not being so restrictive as to foreclose significant auction participation.  We further 
find that the stringency of the eligibility requirements for RBP Resources is reasonable 
given the revenue certainty that they will obtain over the RBP commitment period should 
they clear the RBP auction.   

We disagree with the Market Monitor that PJM should have attempted to match 
the duration of supply with the duration of data center demand.  The same energy must-
offer requirements apply to RBP Resources based on their resource type as would apply 
to any other resource of that type that cleared the RPM Auctions.  Similarly, we disagree 
with the Market Monitor’s arguments that uprates, Demand Resources, and DER 
Aggregation Resources should not be eligible RBP Resources.  The Commission has 
dismissed similar arguments from the Market Monitor in the context of the RPM.73

We acknowledge AEU’s concern that few Demand Resources and DER 
Aggregation Resources can lock in their customers for 15 years, as PJM’s proposal  
would require for them to qualify as RBP Resources.  We find, however, that PJM’s 
proposal is nonetheless reasonable.  As PJM explains, in return for the revenue certainty 
that participating in the RBP auction will provide, it is reasonable to expect these

We find that PJM’s proposed RBP UCAP MW shortfall charges are just and 

reasonable because they will deter speculative resources from seeking to participate in  
the RBP auction without creating an undue barrier to entry.  Under PJM’s proposal, an 
RBP UCAP MW shortfall occurs when a committed RBP Resource has not reached 
commercial operation for the relevant delivery year for up to three consecutive years.  
RBP shortfall charges are equal to the product of the RBP Resource’s committed RBP 
UCAP MW shortfall and 20% of the RBP price.  We find that PJM’s proposal to assess 
penalties at this level, for up to three years, is just and reasonable because these penalties, 
along with the pay-as-bid RBP clearing price and the promise of up to 15 years of 
revenue certainty, will incentivize developers of RBP Resources to deliver their RBP 
UCAP MW.     

We disagree with the Market Monitor’s various criticisms of the proposed

inappropriately proposes to shift ELCC-related risk from generators to customers.  Under 
the proposal, LSEs pay RBP Charges for only the RBP UCAP MW delivered.  Thus, the 
risk for customers is that they end up paying for less RBP capacity than they anticipated.  
However, PJM’s proposal mitigates this risk because, as discussed above, other aspects 
of the proposal strongly encourage RBP Resources to deliver.

iii.

Determination 

We are unpersuaded by arguments that PJM’s filing is unjust and unreasonable 

because it lacks locational constraints and direct market power mitigation.  First, we find 
that the proposal’s lack of locational constraints does not render PJM’s overall proposal 
unjust and unreasonable.  Specifically, the volume of capacity that PJM will procure 
through the RBP auction—up to 6,831 MW—is relatively small compared with total 
existing supply in PJM (149 GW), so even if RBP Resources are not located in the exact 
same Locational Deliverability Areas as new loads, the effect of any locational 
constraints should be limited.  Moreover, as PJM explains, the RBP is designed to 
address the PJM Region’s capacity shortfall, such that it is addressing an effectively 
region-wide problem.136  PIOs are correct that, depending on where RBP Resources 
choose to site themselves, significant Network Upgrades could be triggered.  But one can 
reasonably assume that RBP Resources will include anticipated Network Upgrade costs 
in their RBP Sell Offers—meaning that RBP Resources that anticipate significant 
Network Upgrade costs will be less likely to clear in the RBP auction. 

Second, we find that the lack of direct market power screens or mitigation for the 
RBP does not render PJM’s overall proposal unjust and reasonable.  As PJM explains, in 
the context of a pay-as-bid structure with a maximum willingness to pay set at $555/MW-
day, the effectiveness of such measures would be limited.  In addition, PJM’s proposed 
average-price cap of $555/MW-day further disciplines costs by increasing the risk that 
high offers do not clear.  And as PJM argues, the exercise of market power is unlikely if 
even a portion of the more than 130 GW of supply identified in the Charles River 
Associates Survey Results decides to participate and drive down prices.137

Regarding PJM’s proposal to exclude FRR Entities from the RBP, EKPC and 

Vistra assert that the proposal is unduly discriminatory because PJM proposes to include 
LSEs that meet their reliability requirements through self-supply, even though such LSEs 
are similarly situated to FRR Entities.246  In response, PJM argues that the scope of RBP 
is to procure capacity for new large loads that did not bring their own capacity, which 
does not implicate FRR Entities who by definition must bring new capacity for all new 
loads within their territory or otherwise be subject to FRR Commitment Insufficiency 
Charges.247  EKPC answers that it does not argue that FRR Entities should be subjected 
to the RBP, but rather that PJM fails to explain why similar planning conduct by FRR 
Entities and self-supply LSEs warrants different treatment under the RBP.248
