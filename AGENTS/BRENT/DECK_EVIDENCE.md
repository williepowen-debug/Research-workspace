## "WHY CAN'T THEY JUST FIX IT?" — Skeptic Objections Addressed

---

**"Can't OPEC just pump more?"**
Over 90% of OPEC's spare capacity belongs to countries that export exclusively through the closed Strait — it's physically inaccessible. The emergency quota increase on March 1 added 206,000 barrels per day against a 13–14 million barrel per day gap; that's 1.4%.

---

**"Won't high prices bring US shale production roaring back?"**
At maximum theoretical pace, US shale can add 200,000–240,000 barrels per day in 90 days — less than 2% of the gap — and that's only if operators start today, which the Baker Hughes data shows they are not doing; rig counts are actually declining.

---

**"Can't the US/allies just clear the mines and reopen Hormuz?"**
Mine clearance of a navigable corridor takes 30–90 days minimum with dedicated MCM (mine countermeasure) forces — the 1991 Gulf War required ~90 days to declare safe corridors after Iraq's 1,300 mines. Then P&I insurance clubs need 4–12 additional weeks of verified safe transit before reinstating coverage. Ships don't move without insurance.

---

**"Won't a ceasefire end this quickly?"**
A ceasefire announcement would cause an immediate oil price sell-off (the market trades on expectations) — which is actually our planned exit trigger. But the physical supply restoration takes 30–90+ days regardless of politics, because of mine clearance and insurance reinstatement. The price crash from a ceasefire would be tradeable; the supply restoration would be slow.

---

**"The SPR release will fix it — governments can intervene."**
The largest SPR release in history, just announced, covers ~30% of the daily supply shortfall, takes 13–15 days to reach the market, and lasts roughly 90 days. It's a ceiling on extreme price spikes, not a solution. The gap remains.

---

**"Oil shocks always turn out to be temporary."**
True — but "temporary" in oil shock history means 6–18 months, not 6 days. The 1990 Gulf War shock lasted ~5 months and still tipped the US into recession. The transmission to consumer spending, lending standards, and Fed policy is already underway; reversing it requires not just reopening Hormuz but rebuilding consumer confidence, refilling supply chains, and seeing the Fed pivot — each of which takes quarters.
