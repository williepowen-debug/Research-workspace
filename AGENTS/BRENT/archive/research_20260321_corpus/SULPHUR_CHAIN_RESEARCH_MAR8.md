The Global Elemental Sulphur Supply Chain: Systemic Vulnerabilities and the Strait of Hormuz Chokepoint
1. Executive Summary and Strategic Context
The global elemental sulphur market occupies a critical, yet frequently overlooked, position at the nexus of the hydrocarbon, agricultural, and metallurgical sectors. Derived overwhelmingly as a mandatory by-product of sour crude oil refining and natural gas sweetening, elemental sulphur is the primary feedstock for sulphuric acid—the world's most consumed industrial chemical and the foundational reagent for modern industrialization. A sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz would trigger an unprecedented supply shock, fundamentally destabilizing the global sulphur supply chain. Given that approximately half of the world's seaborne sulphur trade transits this narrow maritime corridor, the reverberations of a blockade would swiftly extend beyond energy markets, severely impairing the production of phosphate fertilizers, lithium-ion battery precursors, and refined copper.
This analysis rigorously models the cascading impacts of a Hormuz disruption on the global sulphur and sulphuric acid markets. It quantifies the exact production capacities and throughputs of Middle Eastern downstream facilities, evaluates the fragility of global sulphuric acid inventories, and maps the highly exposed supply chain of the Central African Copperbelt. Furthermore, it assesses the economic tipping points for Solvent Extraction and Electrowinning (SX-EW) copper operations under extreme acid price inflation and examines the logistical bottlenecks that would prevent non-Gulf producers from scaling output in the short-to-medium term. By contextualizing current vulnerabilities against historical disruptions—such as the 1990 invasion of Kuwait and the 2019 Abqaiq attack—this analysis provides a definitive blueprint of systemic risk in the global sulphur complex.
2. The Chemistry and Mechanics of Sulphur By-Production
To understand the geographical concentration of global sulphur supply, one must examine the chemical composition of Middle Eastern hydrocarbons and the environmental mandates governing their extraction and refinement. The Middle East's dominance in the global sulphur market is not a product of primary mining (such as the historical Frasch process), but a mandatory consequence of processing the region's highly sour hydrocarbon reserves.
Crude oils extracted from the Persian Gulf, such as Arabian Heavy and Arabian Medium, contain massive concentrations of sulfur—typically exceeding 2.9% and 2.2% to 2.9% by weight, respectively.1 Similarly, the region's vast non-associated natural gas fields contain lethal concentrations of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), often reaching up to 15% by volume.2 As global environmental regulations strictly limit sulfur dioxide (SO2) emissions from the combustion of end-use fuels, refineries and gas plants are compelled to deploy extensive hydrotreating and amine scrubbing technologies. These processes strip H2S from the hydrocarbon streams, which is subsequently routed to Sulphur Recovery Units (SRUs). Utilizing the Claus process—a thermal and catalytic recovery method—the H2S is converted into molten elemental sulphur.
The volume of sulphur generated is therefore inextricably linked to the volume of sour crude refined and sour gas processed. Total Middle Eastern sulphur production reached 21.9 million tonnes per annum (t/a) in 2024, with regional exports hitting a record 19.1 million t/a, representing roughly 49% of global seaborne trade.2 A sudden cessation of maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz would instantly trap this monumental volume of material, creating a critical feedstock vacuum in downstream chemical markets globally.
3. Middle East Sulphur By-Production: Capacity vs. Operational Throughput
A granular examination of regional infrastructure reveals significant variances between theoretical nameplate capacity and actual operational throughput. While capacity figures dominate project announcements, the global supply chain relies entirely on actual throughput—the physical volume of molten sulphur formed into granules and loaded onto dry bulk carriers.
3.1 Saudi Arabia: Refining Hubs and the Pivot to Non-Associated Gas
Saudi Arabia is a colossal producer, exporter, and domestic consumer of elemental sulphur, having consumed approximately 6.5 million tons domestically in 2024 for its own massive phosphate fertilizer industry.3 The Kingdom's sulphur output is structurally transitioning. Historically tethered exclusively to the refining of sour crude, future growth is anchored in the aggressive expansion of non-associated sour gas processing, a core pillar of the Kingdom's Vision 2030 strategy to transition domestic power generation away from crude oil.1
The Jubail Complex (SATORP and SASREF)
The Jubail Industrial City, located on the Arabian Gulf coast, hosts two of the world's most advanced refining complexes, both calibrated to process highly sour feedstocks.
SATORP (Saudi Aramco Total Refining and Petrochemical Company): This joint venture between Saudi Aramco and TotalEnergies operates with a nameplate processing capacity of 465,000 barrels per day (b/d) of Arabian Heavy crude.5 SATORP is engineered as a full-conversion platform; it utilizes severe hydrocracking to convert heavy, high-sulfur crude into lighter, ultra-low-sulfur products without producing heavy fuel oil.6 While specific elemental sulphur tonnages are closely guarded proprietary data, standard chemical engineering yields for a heavy sour refinery of this configuration dictate a recovery requirement of 1,200 to 1,500 tonnes per day (t/d) to meet emission standards. SATORP's operational throughput is highly optimized, consistently running near its 465,000 b/d capacity, translating to steady, high-volume sulphur output.6
SASREF (Saudi Aramco Shell Refinery Company / fully Aramco owned): Operating at a capacity of 305,000 b/d, SASREF contributes significantly to the Jubail cluster's elemental sulphur output.1 Like SATORP, it runs at high utilization rates to feed both domestic petrochemical demand and international export markets. The facility has recently focused on mitigating sulphur dust challenges associated with its forming processes.8
Gas Processing: Fadhili, Tanajib, and Jafurah
The true scale of future Saudi sulphur capacity lies in its massive gas plants.
Fadhili Gas Plant: Aramco recently awarded $7.7 billion in engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) contracts to expand the Fadhili plant's capacity from 2.5 to 4.0 billion standard cubic feet per day (bscfd) by November 2027.4 Crucially, this expansion alone is engineered to add an additional 2,300 t/d (approximately 0.84 million t/a) of actual sulphur production throughput, highlighting the high H2S content of the incoming feed gas.9
Tanajib Gas Plant: Scheduled for completion in 2025/2026 as part of the Marjan offshore field expansion, Tanajib will possess an operational processing capacity of 2.5 bscfd.2 This facility will bring an estimated 1.0 million t/a of new elemental sulphur capacity online.2
Jafurah Basin: As the largest unconventional natural gas field in Saudi Arabia (229 Tcf reserves), Jafurah is under development with an initial start-up planned for late 2025.4 While its sulphur yield will be lower than the ultra-sour conventional fields, its scale will contribute to the national aggregate.
3.2 United Arab Emirates: The Offshore Sour Gas Behemoth
The UAE was the world's largest exporter of elemental sulphur in 2024, generating $1.1 billion in export revenue and supplying major markets including Morocco, China, and Indonesia.11 The Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) has aggressively monetized its sour gas reserves, centralizing its sulphur handling and logistics at the Ruwais complex.2
Ruwais Complex While Ruwais operates a massive 800,000 b/d refining footprint (comprising Ruwais East and West), the elemental sulphur generated directly from these crude distillation and hydrotreating units is relatively modest, historically cited at a throughput of around 30 t/d.12 The true strategic value of the Ruwais complex lies in its function as a centralized terminal for gas-derived sulphur piped from inland and offshore fields.
The Ghasha Concession: The offshore Hail and Ghasha fields are the centerpiece of ADNOC's sour gas strategy. Targeting a production throughput of 1.5 bscfd of gas with an exceptionally high H2S content of up to 15%, the project routes its liquid sulphur to Ruwais for forming and granulation. This expansion will add an estimated 3.7 million t/a of final sulphur output to the Ruwais complex.2 Because of this projected surge in supply, massive additional forming and loading infrastructure at the Ruwais port will be necessary by 2028.2 ADNOC anticipates that total UAE exports will surge from 7.0 million t/a in 2024 to 11.4 million t/a by 2029.2
Shah and Habshan
Shah Project: Operated by Al Hosn Gas, Shah is a marvel of extreme sour gas engineering. Currently processing 1.28 bscfd of gas, it boasts a sulphur production capacity of 4.2 million t/a. Active plans are underway to push gas throughput to 1.45 bscfd, which will proportionally increase sulphur yields.2
Habshan V: This massive facility processes 2.3 bscfd of gas, yielding an impressive operational throughput of 5,200 t/d (approximately 1.9 million t/a) of sulphur.12 Tie-ins from the Bab North East development are injecting an additional 400 million scf/d, further boosting recovery and solidifying the UAE's export dominance.12
3.3 Qatar: The Ras Laffan Epicenter and Geopolitical Vulnerability
Qatar's sulphur production is inextricably linked to its status as a global liquefied natural gas (LNG) superpower. The extraction of liquid sulphur from the vast North Field gas streams is heavily centralized at the Common Sulfur Facility (CSP) located in Ras Laffan Industrial City.13
Ras Laffan The Ras Laffan complex has a total sulphur recovery and forming nameplate capacity of approximately 3.8 million t/a.14 In August 2021, the CSP achieved a historic operational milestone by processing in excess of 10,000 tonnes of sulphur in a single day.13 However, actual annual operational throughput has historically hovered slightly below absolute maximum nameplate capacity; in 2025, confirmed export volumes were logged at 3.4 million tonnes.14
It is critical to contextualize recent geopolitical vulnerabilities specific to Qatar's highly concentrated infrastructure. In March 2026, following a drone strike on the Ras Laffan complex (part of broader regional hostilities involving US, Israeli, and Iranian assets), state-owned QatarEnergy announced a complete halt to sulphur production alongside LNG and associated products.14 This sudden removal of roughly 3.4 to 3.8 million t/a of capacity—representing approximately 8% of global seaborne sulphur trade—provides a live stress test of the market, instantly tightening delivered supply across Asia and forcing spot prices to escalate rapidly.14
3.4 Kuwait and Iraq: Downstream Bottlenecks and Opaque Data
Kuwait (Al-Zour) Kuwait's sulphur profile is currently dictated by the operational status of the massive Al-Zour refinery complex. Designed primarily to produce ultra-low-sulphur fuel oil (VLSFO) for domestic power generation, Al-Zour boasts an immense crude processing capacity of 615,000 b/d.16 While state-owned KPC successfully ramped up the refinery's three crude distillation units (CDUs) through 2023 and early 2024, achieving record refined product exports, the complex has faced severe operational headwinds.17 Recent intelligence indicates the refinery has operated at only approximately 70% of its design capacity (430,000 b/d) due to suspected, chronic technical issues specifically isolated within the sulphur recovery block and heavy crude processing units.18 Consequently, while Kuwait's total sulphur capacity has theoretically expanded to 2.9 million t/a 19, actual operational throughput remains deeply constrained by these downstream bottlenecks, limiting expected export volumes.
Iraq (Basrah and Refining Network) Iraq remains a major domestic consumer of elemental sulphur (absorbing 6.4 million tons in 2024) 3, yet its by-production landscape is plagued by infrastructure deficits, political instability, and chronic underinvestment. The nation is actively attempting to reduce its rampant associated gas flaring. The Basrah Gas Company (BGC) is currently capturing gas from the Rumaila, West Qurna 1, and Zubair fields, yielding natural gas liquids and associated elemental sulphur.20 On the refining side, Iraq aims to increase its total crude processing capacity to 1.23 million b/d by 2027 through the recent commissioning of the Karbala refinery (140,000 b/d) and planned upgrades to the Basrah, Haditha, and Al-Dora facilities.21
Data Uncertainty Flag: It is imperative to note that verifiable, exact quantitative data on actual elemental sulphur recovery volumes from Iraqi downstream operations remains opaque and highly uncertain. While gas capture volumes are publicly reported, the specific elemental sulphur tonnages formed and exported are rarely disaggregated. Production is heavily dependent on the operational efficiency of aging Claus units that frequently suffer from power grid failures and maintenance interruptions.8



4. The Illusion of Storage: Global Sulphuric Acid Inventory and "Days-of-Consumption"
A critical analytical error frequently made in commodity market assessments is the assumption that sulphuric acid (H2SO4) is stockpiled in the same manner as refined base metals, grains, or crude oil. The inquiry explicitly requests the "current global sulphuric acid inventory in days-of-consumption" across strategic regions such as China, the DRC, Zambia, Chile, and the United States. It is imperative to categorically state that exact "days of consumption" metrics for sulphuric acid are fundamentally unavailable, as the chemical is virtually never held in strategic, long-term reserves. 22
4.1 The Physical Constraints of Storage: Why Acid is a Just-in-Time Commodity
Unlike elemental sulphur—which is an inert, solid yellow mineral that can be stored indefinitely in massive open-air pyramids at ports, railheads, or refineries without significant degradation—sulphuric acid is a highly corrosive, dense, and hazardous liquid. The logistics of its storage prohibit the accumulation of vast strategic reserves:
Volumetric Density and Infrastructure: Acid requires highly specialized, expensive stainless steel or specially lined carbon-steel tankage with stringent secondary containment systems. Constructing massive tank farms solely for strategic acid reserves is economically prohibitive.
Corrosion and Degradation: Prolonged storage of high-concentration commercial acid in standard carbon steel tanks inevitably leads to iron uptake. This degrades the acid's purity over time, rendering it unsuitable for sensitive metallurgical applications (such as battery-grade lithium extraction) or specific chemical syntheses.
Logistical Economics: The global merchant market for seaborne sulphuric acid is relatively small—estimated at only 22.3 million t/a out of a staggering total global consumption of 306 million t/a.22 The vast majority of acid is produced and consumed locally, effectively serving as a continuous pipeline or just-in-time trucking commodity.
Conversely, base metals like copper exhibit highly transparent exchange inventories. For instance, metallurgical analysts note that when global copper exchange stocks (such as those on the LME or COMEX) fall below 20 days of global consumption, markets experience severe price volatility and hypersensitivity to supply disruptions.23 Acid lacks this buffering mechanism entirely. A supply disruption in elemental sulphur immediately translates to a sulphuric acid shortage, as acid plants possess minimal on-site feedstock buffers, operating purely on continuous flow.
4.2 Regional Disparities and Exposure Profiles
While absolute acid days-of-consumption cannot be quantified, regional exposure can be accurately assessed through the lens of elemental sulphur stockpiles, smelter capacity, and structural import reliance.
China: As the world's undisputed manufacturing engine, China consumes massive quantities of acid for phosphate fertilizer production and industrial applications. Because it lacks sufficient domestic elemental sulphur production, it is a dominant global importer. As of late October 2024, Chinese total port inventories of elemental sulphur stood at 2.31 million tonnes (with 844,000 tonnes located at Yangtze River ports and 499,000 tonnes at Dafeng).22 This solid stockpile acts as China's de facto acid reserve. Given China's massive daily consumption rates (representing roughly 45% of global refined copper consumption alone 23), a 2.31 million tonne sulphur stockpile provides a buffer of only a few weeks against a total cessation of Middle Eastern imports.
Chile: As the epicenter of global copper mining, Chile relies heavily on massive quantities of acid for oxide heap leaching. Chile's domestic smelter network (which produces by-product acid) cannot meet the immense demand of its mining sector, forcing a heavy reliance on seaborne merchant acid imports. In August 2024 alone, Chile imported 226,000 tonnes of liquid sulphuric acid (a 35% year-over-year increase), predominantly sourced from Peru, Japan, and China.22 Because shipping liquid acid is hazardous and requires specialized chemical tankers, Chilean mines operate with highly constrained, continuous supply chains, rendering them acutely vulnerable to shipping disruptions or price spikes in the Asian merchant acid market.
United States: The US market is transitioning into a severe structural deficit. Domestic refinery sulphur output is in secular decline, while demand from domestic lithium extraction, battery manufacturing, and robust phosphate fertilizer production continues to grow. Consequently, the US is projected to become a net importer of sulphur by 2025, pulling in up to 2 million t/a by 2029 to feed its domestic acid plants.22
DRC & Zambia: These landlocked African nations represent the most extreme example of logistical vulnerability. They possess virtually zero strategic inventory of either solid sulphur or liquid acid, operating purely on continuous, fragile trucking routes extending from the eastern seaboard of the continent.
5. The Central African Copperbelt: Mapping the Acid Supply Chain
The Central African Copperbelt, straddling the borders of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Zambia, is the world's premier growth region for copper and the undisputed center of global cobalt production. The metallurgical extraction of these critical battery metals—specifically from the region's vast oxide ores—is wholly dependent on vast, uninterrupted quantities of sulphuric acid. The region's supply chain is highly bifurcated along geological lines: Zambia is structurally long on acid due to its sulfide smelting sector, while the DRC is structurally short, relying on a complex, expensive, and precarious import paradigm.
5.1 Zambia: Smelter Dominance and Regional Exporting
Zambia's copper geology is predominantly sulfidic, necessitating traditional concentrating and high-temperature smelting. When copper sulfide concentrates (such as chalcopyrite) are smelted, they release vast quantities of sulfur dioxide (SO2) gas. To comply with environmental regulations and capture economic value, this gas is captured and converted into sulphuric acid via dual-contact, dual-absorption plants.
In 2024, Zambia's total acid production reached 3.4 million t/a, with a staggering 3.0 million t/a derived directly from metallurgical smelters, supplemented by 0.3 million t/a from minor sulphur burning and 0.1 million t/a from pyrites.26 The country boasts an impressive total acid nameplate capacity of 3.9 million t/a, yet its domestic consumption is only approximately 1 million t/a.26 This massive structural surplus transforms Zambia into the critical acid exporter for the broader Central African region.
Key Zambian Acid Sources:
Kansanshi Smelter (First Quantum Minerals): The bedrock of Zambian acid production. The Kansanshi smelter processes 1.2 million t/a of concentrate, generating over 1.0 million t/a of sulphuric acid as a by-product.26 A portion is used internally for leaching oxide caps, but a massive volume is exported. (Note: FQM's Sentinel mine, which lacks a smelter, sends its fresh concentrate to Kansanshi for processing 30).
Mufulira Smelter (Mopani Copper Mines/Glencore): Following significant upgrades to its Isasmelt furnace, this facility features a captive acid capacity of 760,000 t/a.26
Chambishi Copper Smelter: A joint venture predominantly owned by Chinese state-owned CNMC and Yunnan Copper, featuring a capacity of 660,000 t/a.26
Konkola Copper Mines (KCM / Vedanta): Operates smelter assets at Nchanga and Nkana with a combined acid capacity of 1.0 million t/a.26
Sulphur Burners: Zambia maintains a modest 300,000 t/a of elemental sulphur burning capacity (spread across facilities in Solwezi and Kitwe, including First Quantum's Bwana Mkubwa plant which operates a 120-300 t/d burner) as a supplementary supply mechanism.26
5.2 The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC): The "Import and Burn" Paradigm
Unlike Zambia, the DRC's near-surface geology contains massive, exceptionally high-grade copper and cobalt oxide deposits. These ores cannot be processed via traditional smelting; they must be dissolved using sulphuric acid via Solvent Extraction and Electrowinning (SX-EW). Consequently, the DRC is a ravenous consumer of acid, with a staggering annual requirement of approximately 2.2 million t/a in 2024.26 Total domestic production in the DRC was 3.6 million t/a, but critically, 3.2 million t/a of this was derived from sulphur burning, with only 0.3 million t/a coming from domestic smelters.26
Because the DRC lacks a mature, high-volume smelting network, it must source its acid through two highly vulnerable channels:
Importing Liquid Acid: Trucking hazardous liquid sulphuric acid across borders from surplus Zambian smelters.
Importing Elemental Sulphur (The Burner Strategy): To circumvent the extreme dangers, limited shelf-life, and exorbitant freight costs of moving liquid acid over thousands of kilometers of degraded African infrastructure, major DRC operators import dry, solid elemental sulphur. They truck this solid mineral to the mine site and combust it in captive "sulphur burner" plants to manufacture acid locally, exactly when and where it is needed.
The DRC imports over 500,000 t/a of solid elemental sulphur to support these burners.34 Astoundingly, nearly all of this sulphur originates from the Middle East Gulf. It is discharged at the Tanzanian port of Dar es Salaam and trucked over 1,500 kilometers inland to the mining hub of Kolwezi.35
Key DRC Acid Sources and Sourcing Strategies:
Mutanda Mining (MUMI) & Kamoto Copper Company (KCC) [Glencore]: Mutanda, historically one of the world's largest cobalt and copper mines, commissioned a massive captive sulphur burner (capable of 1,400 t/d) to insulate itself from regional liquid acid shortages and transport costs.26 This architectural decision ties Mutanda's operational viability directly to the continuous flow of Middle Eastern sulphur imports.
Tenke Fungurume Mine (CMOC): Similarly relies heavily on domestic, captive sulphur-burning acid plants to process its immense oxide reserves.26
Kamoa-Kakula (Ivanhoe Mines): In a strategic bid to drastically reduce its reliance on imported acid and Middle Eastern sulphur, Kamoa-Kakula is constructing a 500,000 t/a direct-to-blister flash smelter (Phase 3 expansion).39 Once operational, this smelter will capture SO2 to produce captive sulphuric acid as a by-product, structurally altering local market dynamics, lowering C1 cash costs, and significantly reducing Ivanhoe's vulnerability to external logistical disruptions like a Hormuz blockade.39



6. SX-EW Copper Economics: Sensitivity to Sulphuric Acid Prices
The economic viability of mining oxide copper ores via Solvent Extraction and Electrowinning (SX-EW) is acutely sensitive to the price of chemical consumables. While traditional concentrator-based sulfide mines expend massive capital on mechanical power for grinding, milling, and crushing, SX-EW operations incur massive operating expenditures (OPEX) directly on sulphuric acid required to irrigate their heap or tank leach pads. A sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz would effectively obliterate the current margin structure of global SX-EW operations.
6.1 The Structural Cost Disadvantage of SX-EW
Data extracted from CRU's Copper Mining Cost Model reveals that SX-EW mines are structurally less cost-competitive than their concentrate-producing counterparts. Site costs at SX-EW facilities run, on average, 10% higher than at concentrator mines.40 This competitive disadvantage is driven by two primary factors: power costs (which are 58% higher due to the immense electricity baseload required to plate copper in the electrowinning tankhouses) and consumables costs (which are 18% higher, driven almost entirely by the continuous procurement of sulphuric acid for leaching).40
Consequently, a staggering 76% of all global SX-EW mine capacity sits in the top half of the copper mine cost curve.40 This positions them as the industry's marginal producers—the first assets likely to be shuttered during periods of severe margin compression or input cost inflation.
6.2 Identifying the Uneconomic Threshold: Baseline vs. Crisis Pricing
Under normalized macroeconomic parameters, mining engineers modeling SX-EW feasibility studies structure their Net Smelter Return (NSR) calculations assuming a delivered sulphuric acid price ranging from $100 to $130 per tonne.41 At these baseline input costs, contemporary operations like the Los Azules project outline a breakeven copper price of $3.10 per pound ($6,834/tonne).43
However, the leverage of acid pricing on total cash costs is brutal and unforgiving. The theoretical "uneconomic threshold" is not a static number, but a dynamic ratio tied to the prevailing London Metal Exchange (LME) copper price and the specific acid-consumption profile of the ore body. A typical oxide deposit requires between 3 to 10 kilograms of acid to produce a single kilogram of copper cathode, highly dependent on the amount of acid-consuming gangue (waste) minerals present in the host rock.44
We can observe extreme market stress during geopolitical flare-ups. In early March 2026, amid disruptions in the Middle East Gulf, spot sulphur prices at the critical hub of Dar es Salaam spiked to $615–$630/t FCA.35 When factoring in the exorbitant $280/t cost to truck that dry sulphur inland to Kolwezi in the DRC, the delivered price (DAP) approached an astronomical $900/t.36
Given the standard chemical conversion stoichiometry of 3 parts sulphur to 1 part sulphuric acid (by mass), a $900/t sulphur feedstock dictates a baseline captive acid production cost of nearly $300/t.36 Crucially, this $300/t figure represents merely the raw material cost, entirely excluding the capital amortization, maintenance, and operating expenditures of running the burner plant itself.
If delivered acid prices sustain at or above $300/t, the consumable cost per pound of copper produced escalates exponentially. Historical data indicates that at a copper price of $4,516/t ($2.05/lb)—the cyclical lows of 2015—36% of global SX-EW mines became fundamentally cashflow negative.40 Even at robust, modern copper prices of $8,500/t ($3.85/lb), an acid price sustained in the range of $280–$320/tonne would brutally compress margins. This threshold would render lower-grade, high-acid-consuming oxide pits entirely uneconomic, forcing widespread curtailments, placing assets on care and maintenance, and destroying roughly 20% of global mined copper output at a time when electrification trends demand maximum supply.



7. The Geopolitics of Alternative Supply: Scaling Non-Gulf Sulphur
If the Strait of Hormuz is closed, severing nearly 20 million t/a of elemental sulphur from global markets, consumers in the fertilizer and metallurgical sectors will inevitably look to the secondary tier of global producers: Canada, Kazakhstan, and Russia. However, a rigorous logistical analysis reveals a stark reality. None of these producers possess the requisite infrastructure to rapidly scale seaborne exports. The critical bottlenecks are not beneath the ground in wellheads; they are fixed structurally on the rails and at the ports.
7.1 Canada: Infrastructure Saturation and Rail Bottlenecks
Canada has historically been a titan of sulphur exportation, recovering vast quantities from Alberta's deep sour gas fields and oil sands upgrading facilities. However, Canadian production has been in secular decline as older, conventional sour gas fields deplete; national sulphur emissions have plummeted by 65% since 1990 due to both technological improvements and declining base output.46
Even in a hypothetical scenario where Canadian producers could surge extraction to capture record-high global prices, they are physically constrained by a rigid, heavily saturated rail network connecting landlocked Alberta to the Pacific coast. The Port of Vancouver, a primary export hub, is notoriously congested. Its expansion is restricted by urban encroachment, difficult terrain, and the absolute capacity limits of the Thornton Tunnel and the Second Narrows Rail Bridge.47 Canadian National (CN) operates roughly 70% of the trains moving in and out of Vancouver, and despite recent investments in 19,000-foot passing sidings and upgraded tunnel ventilation, capacity remains structurally capped.48
The primary alternative is the Port of Prince Rupert in northern British Columbia, which boasts North America's deepest natural harbor and is one to two sailing days closer to Asian markets than Vancouver.49 Yet, Prince Rupert faces its own severe inland rail bottlenecks, most notably the Zanardi Rapids bridge at the entrance to the port. Currently, this single-track bridge strictly limits traffic to a maximum of 24 trains per day.50 Until multi-billion-dollar double-tracking projects are completed late in the decade to "future-proof" the corridor, Canadian sulphur exports remain firmly inelastic and incapable of plugging a Middle Eastern supply gap.50
7.2 Kazakhstan: Super-Giant Fields and Pipeline Fragility
Kazakhstan is the third-largest hydrocarbon resource holder in the Caspian region and hosts two of the world's most formidable sour oil fields: Kashagan and Tengiz.51
Kashagan: Discovered in 2000, Kashagan's associated gas contains lethally high levels of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), making it the primary engine for future Central Asian sulphur growth.51 Since commercial operations finally began in 2016, the field has produced over 10 million metric tonnes of elemental sulphur.51
Tengiz: Operated by Tengizchevroil (TCO), Tengiz is equally vital but prone to operational instability. For example, a fire at the GTES-4 power station in January 2026 forced a prolonged shutdown, instantly removing hundreds of thousands of tonnes of crude—and its associated sulphur—from the market, illustrating the vulnerability of relying on single mega-projects.52
Kazakhstan's fatal flaw is its landlocked geography. Over 80% of its crude (and thereby the economic viability of its associated sulphur extraction) relies on the Caspian Pipeline Consortium (CPC) network, which transits Russian territory and terminates at the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk.52 The CPC is increasingly viewed as highly unreliable due to recurring storm damage to its single-point moorings (VPUs) and severe collateral risks from the ongoing Russia-Ukraine war, including escalating drone attacks on Russian infrastructure.53 Alternative routes, such as the Trans-Caspian International Transport Route (the "Middle Corridor"), currently possess a modest capacity of around 80,000 TEU annually—woefully insufficient to handle bulk commodities like sulphur at a scale needed to offset a sudden Middle East supply shock.54
7.3 Russia: Sanctions and the Fragility of Eurasian Logistics
Russia is a major global producer of sulphur, recovered extensively from its refining network and major sour gas fields in the Astrakhan region. However, sweeping Western sanctions have profoundly altered its traditional export vectors, forcing a pivot toward Asia.55
Russian and Kazakh sulphur railings rely heavily on the overland corridors connecting through Belarus and Poland into Europe, or eastward toward China.54 The extreme fragility of this Eurasian land bridge was vividly demonstrated in September 2025 during the Zapad 2025 military exercises conducted jointly by Russia and Belarus. Citing drone incursions and utilizing electronic warfare measures, Poland suspended all road and rail traffic at Belarusian checkpoints.54 The closure stranded over 130 freight trains for weeks, paralyzing billions of euros in cargo and proving definitively that the overland route is highly susceptible to geopolitical weaponization.54 Consequently, Russian sulphur cannot be viewed by global markets as a reliable, scalable short-term substitute for Middle Eastern seaborne volumes.
8. Historical Precedents: Market Behavior During Gulf Disruptions
To accurately model the psychological fallout and physical logistical constraints of a sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz, one must meticulously analyze market behavior during previous major geopolitical dislocations in the Persian Gulf.
8.1 The 1990 Invasion of Kuwait
On August 2, 1990, the Iraqi invasion of Kuwait precipitated an immediate and absolute cessation of oil and associated gas production from two OPEC heavyweights. An estimated 4.3 million to 5.2 million b/d of crude oil was instantly removed from global markets.56 The macroeconomic reaction was violent: global crude oil prices surged by a staggering 100%, skyrocketing from $21 to nearly $40 per barrel in rapid succession.58
The downstream impact on elemental sulphur and associated heavy products was equally disruptive. Because sulphur is a direct, immediate derivative of refining and gas processing, the sudden offline status of Kuwaiti and Iraqi infrastructure decimated regional sulphur availability. The U.S. Energy Information Administration recorded that U.S. Gulf Coast fuel oil production dropped to its lowest monthly average since January 1990, reflecting the sudden deprivation of sour Middle Eastern feedstocks across the Atlantic.59 During this era, sulphur prices experienced severe, chaotic volatility, driven entirely by raw outage panic rather than fundamental demand shifts. The market was eventually stabilized only by the massive drawdown of global inventories and the eventual military success of Operation Desert Storm in early 1991, which allowed prices to revert to pre-invasion means.58 However, it took nearly four years for Kuwaiti production to recover to pre-disruption levels, while Iraq remained stunted for a decade.56
8.2 The 2019 Abqaiq Attack
A more contemporary, and technologically relevant, precedent occurred in September 2019, when a highly sophisticated drone and cruise missile strike devastated the Abqaiq stabilization facility and the Khurais oil field in Saudi Arabia.58 Abqaiq acts as the central nervous system for Saudi Aramco, processing vast quantities of highly sour crude and associated gas before routing it to export terminals or domestic SRUs.
The immediate aftermath saw 5.7 million b/d of Saudi production paralyzed—effectively halving the Kingdom's total output in a single day.57 The market reaction was acute: UK Brent crude futures posted their largest single-day price jump on record (surpassing the 1990 invasion), surging over 15% to $63 per barrel immediately following the incident.57
However, the 2019 crisis resolved much faster than the 1990 shock. Global prices retraced to pre-attack levels within approximately two weeks.58 This rapid mitigation was achieved not through the immediate repair of facilities or the drilling of new wells, but through the aggressive, synchronized liquidation of pre-existing Saudi onshore and offshore storage facilities, combined with the activation of spare capacity by neighboring nations like the UAE.57
8.3 Implications for a Modern Hormuz Blockade
The historical lessons from 1990 and 2019 offer a dire prognosis for a sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz. In 2019, the market survived because the attack was highly localized to specific Saudi infrastructure. This allowed maritime exports from un-damaged UAE and Qatari ports to continue flowing freely through the Strait. Furthermore, Saudi Arabia was able to utilize its Red Sea export terminals (such as the Yanbu facilities) to bypass the Arabian Gulf entirely.1
A full closure of the Strait of Hormuz neutralizes all of these historical mitigations. It indiscriminately traps the production of the UAE (Ruwais, Shah), Qatar (Ras Laffan), Kuwait (Al-Zour), and Iraq (Basrah). Unlike liquid crude oil, which can be pumped through trans-peninsular pipelines (such as Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline to the Red Sea or the UAE's Habshan-Fujairah pipeline), bulk elemental sulphur handling infrastructure is highly concentrated at port facilities within the Gulf itself. There is virtually no spare overland or pipeline capacity to safely reroute 19 million tonnes of solid or molten sulphur to the Red Sea or the Gulf of Oman. Therefore, a Hormuz blockade would act not merely as a temporary price shock, but as a physical absolute, triggering an immediate inventory crisis in China and posing an existential threat to the African Copperbelt.
9. Strategic Conclusions
The global elemental sulphur supply chain is characterized by a dangerous macroeconomic paradox: it is an essential chemical building block for the modern green economy—underpinning agriculture, copper electrification, and advanced lithium batteries—yet it is structurally treated as a low-value, mandatory waste by-product by the global hydrocarbon industry.
A sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz brutally exposes this paradox. With nearly 50% of the world's seaborne sulphur originating from the specialized gas plants and complex refineries of the UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia, the global market possesses no geographic diversification capable of offsetting a Gulf blockade. Secondary producers like Canada, Kazakhstan, and Russia are fundamentally trapped behind insurmountable domestic rail and international pipeline bottlenecks, rendering them incapable of scaling exports to meet emergency demand.
Furthermore, because sulphuric acid cannot be strategically stockpiled due to its corrosive physical properties, the downstream impact of a sulphur supply shock is virtually instantaneous. As demonstrated by recent price surges delivering sulphur to the DRC at $900 per tonne, the resulting explosion in acid prices threatens to render the solvent extraction of oxide copper entirely economically unviable. This dynamic threatens to choke off the supply of critical minerals exactly when the global energy transition demands them most, highlighting sulphur not as a mere by-product, but as a critical vulnerability in the global industrial supply chain.
