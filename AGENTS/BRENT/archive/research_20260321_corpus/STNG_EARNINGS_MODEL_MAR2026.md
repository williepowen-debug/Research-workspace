3.1 The Strait of Hormuz Chokepoint and Ton-Mile Expansion
The Strait of Hormuz represents the single most vital geographic chokepoint in global energy logistics. Bounded by Oman and Iran, it functions as the exclusive maritime exit for petroleum exports from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iraq, Qatar, Bahrain, and the UAE.22 The geopolitical escalation in March 2026, characterized by the Iranian Revolutionary Guard's threats, actual attacks on merchant vessels, and the deployment of advanced anti-ship capabilities, triggered an immediate exodus of commercial shipping.3 The removal of war risk insurance by underwriting syndicates created a functional blockade, forcing vessels to seek alternative supply routes.4
For the product tanker market, the closure of Hormuz does not merely restrict supply; it structurally alters global trade routes in a manner that exponentially increases "ton-mile demand"—the shipping industry's core metric, calculated by multiplying the volume of cargo transported by the distance it travels. Prior to the conflict, European and Asian demand for refined petroleum products was met via relatively efficient routes. With Middle Eastern supply effectively quarantined, global importers must source clean petroleum products (CPP) such as diesel, jet fuel, and naphtha from alternative refineries in the US Gulf Coast, Latin America, or the Far East.4 These alternative routes represent vastly longer geographical distances. A vessel transporting fuel from the US Gulf Coast to Asia takes significantly longer than one traveling from the Middle East to Asia, effectively removing that vessel from the global fleet pool for a longer duration and causing severe supply-side tightness.4
3.2 The VLCC Correlation and Clean-Dirty Switching
While Scorpio Tankers operates clean product tankers, the parabolic surge in the VLCC TD3C index (Middle East Gulf to China) to WS400+ ($423,736 per day) has profound implications for STNG's asset base.7 The global tanker fleet is not perfectly siloed; it possesses inherent elasticity through a mechanism known as "clean-dirty switching."
Historically, when product tanker rates significantly outpace crude tanker rates, newly built VLCCs and Suezmax crude carriers undertake their maiden voyages transporting clean refined products before switching to dirty crude oil.16 However, the March 2026 crisis has driven crude rates to unprecedented levels, entirely eliminating the economic incentive for crude carriers to participate in the clean product trade. The total absence of this supplementary capacity has left the product tanker market fully exposed to the ton-mile demand shock, allowing LR2 and MR rates to experience pure price discovery without the dampening effect of crude tanker cannibalization.4
Furthermore, the aging profile of the global product tanker fleet—where 30% of vessels will be over 20 years old by 2028—coupled with a historically low order book and the sequestration of older vessels into sanctioned "shadow fleets" trading Russian and Venezuelan crude, means there is virtually zero latent capacity to absorb the Hormuz shock.9

6. Quantitative Lead-Lag Relationships in Maritime Equities
For institutional investors, hedge funds, and sophisticated retail traders holding STNG at the $80+ threshold, establishing a rigorous, quantitative exit strategy is paramount. The current premium valuation is heavily reliant on a geopolitical event that historical data proves is highly volatile and transient. A robust exit framework requires deeply understanding the temporal relationship between shipping equities and physical spot rates. The critical question is: Does the stock market predict the peak in tanker rates, or does it lag behind the physical market?
Extensive quantitative academic research on the lead-lag relationship between varying asset classes yields crucial, actionable insights for trading STNG during a crisis. Standard academic studies utilizing complex methodologies, such as the Thermal Optimal Path method and high-frequency intraday data analysis, generally indicate that financial derivatives and broad equity indices tend to lead physical cash markets during periods of normal, predictable economic fluctuation, acting as a highly efficient price discovery mechanism.46 Under the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH), stocks price in future cash flows before they manifest.46
However, this dynamic frequently inverts, or exhibits severe incongruities, during periods of sudden, exogenous geopolitical or supply-chain shock.49 In the specific context of the global tanker industry, empirical research analyzing the cointegration and Granger causality of West African-US Gulf rates versus WTI spot prices demonstrates that while oil prices, inventory levels, and tanker rates are intrinsically linked, shipping equities often lag sudden physical market shocks.50
The reasoning is behavioral and structural: because physical spot rates can literally spike 100% in a single week (as witnessed with LR2s hitting $91,603/day 4), equity markets understandably hesitate to immediately price in the full annualized value of that spike.1 Institutional algorithms and portfolio managers heavily discount the probability that a $400,000/day VLCC rate will persist for 365 days. Therefore, the stock price moves up, but it trails the explosive trajectory of the Baltic indices.
Crucially, this lagging effect works in reverse during the unwinding of a crisis. STNG's stock price will likely trail the absolute zenith of the Baltic Clean Tanker Index, but it will suffer severe multiple compression the moment the narrative shifts. The stock will not peak on the exact day physical rates hit their highest absolute dollar value; rather, the equity will begin to sell off aggressively the moment the rate of change in spot rates turns negative, or the exact moment a geopolitical resolution appears viable, even if absolute spot rates mathematically remain at historically elevated, highly profitable levels.

7.1 The Decay Function of War Risk Premiums
The most critical input for timing an exit in STNG is understanding the historical decay rate of shipping premiums once a blocked or threatened chokepoint reopens.
Operation Praying Mantis (April 1988): During the culmination of the violent "Tanker War" phase of the Iran-Iraq conflict (1980-1988), the U.S. Navy heavily engaged Iranian forces in the Persian Gulf.53 Prior to this definitive operation, oil prices and tanker risk premiums surged dramatically, with crude surging 10.7% in the prior month.54 However, immediately following the naval engagement and the implementation of escorted commercial transits, the risk premium evaporated instantly. Oil prices dropped immediately and fell a further 4.6% in the subsequent month.54 Tanker rates similarly normalized as the physical threat was neutralized by naval power, rendering the "risk premium" obsolete.
Gulf of Oman Tanker Attacks (May/June 2019): Following coordinated, damaging attacks on tankers (most notably the Front Altair and Kokuka Courageous), war risk insurance premiums for vessels transiting the Gulf surged overnight from roughly $50,000 to over $200,000 for VLCCs.56 Charterers panicked and temporarily halted bookings, spiking global rates. However, as the U.S. and allied nations implemented heavy naval monitoring and the initial psychological shock faded, the logistical rerouting was absorbed by the broader market. The spike in physical spot rates decayed rapidly over the subsequent 30 to 45 days.54
History dictates unequivocally that the introduction of organized naval escorts (as was actively proposed by the U.S. administration in early March 2026 5) or the provision of state-backed political risk insurance through entities like the U.S. Development Finance Corporation (DFC) 58 serves as the immediate catalyst for rapid rate decay. Therefore, the optimal exit trigger for STNG is not the actual, physical reopening of the Strait, but the initial, formal announcement of state-sponsored naval escorts or a ceasefire dialogue.
