Systemic Fragility: Modeling the Contagion of a Hormuz Closure on Taiwan's Energy Grid and Global Semiconductor Supply
Executive Summary
The intersection of global maritime chokepoints, isolated energy infrastructure, and advanced semiconductor manufacturing creates one of the most acute macroeconomic vulnerabilities in the modern geopolitical landscape. Taiwan, which produces the vast majority of the world's sub-7-nanometer (nm) logic chips, operates on an independent electricity grid that is structurally dependent on seaborne fossil fuel imports. This comprehensive research report models a highly disruptive black-swan geopolitical stress test: a sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz. The analysis meticulously evaluates the cascading impacts of such an event on Taiwan's liquefied natural gas (LNG) supply, the operational stability of the Taipower electricity grid, and the subsequent systemic disruption to global technology supply chains.
By integrating 2025 customs data, state-owned enterprise disclosures, power grid infrastructure metrics, and semiconductor fabrication operational tolerances, the analysis demonstrates that Taiwan's current strategic energy buffers are fundamentally insufficient to weather a protracted Middle Eastern supply shock. The data indicates that nearly 40% of Taiwan's LNG must physically transit the Strait of Hormuz.1 Furthermore, structural shifts in Taiwan's power generation mix—specifically the mandated phase-out of baseload nuclear capacity—have left the grid with minimal dispatchable redundancy.3 A severe drop in LNG availability would result in multi-gigawatt power shortfalls, necessitating rolling industrial rationing that would instantaneously halt production at the world's most critical fabrication facilities, including Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company's (TSMC) Fab 18.
The resulting loss of up to 160,000 advanced node wafers per month would rapidly exhaust the inventory buffers of global technology integrators such as Apple, NVIDIA, and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD), triggering a severe contraction in global hardware supply chains.5 Finally, the analysis confirms that geographic diversification efforts, including TSMC's multi-billion-dollar expansions in Arizona, Kumamoto, and Dresden, lack the immediate technological maturity and volumetric capacity to act as viable failsafes in the event of a near-term grid failure in Taiwan.8 This report details the precise mechanisms of this vulnerability across energy procurement, thermodynamic storage limitations, grid balancing, power quality standards, and inventory depletion timelines.
1. The Architecture of Taiwan's LNG Dependency and the Hormuz Vulnerability
Taiwan’s macroeconomic architecture is fundamentally reliant on energy imports, which account for approximately 99% of its total primary energy supply.10 Central to this energy architecture is Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG), a fuel source that has grown systematically in strategic importance as the government executes policies to offset declining nuclear generation and highly polluting coal assets. Understanding the exact contours of Taiwan's LNG procurement strategy, origin diversification, and contractual structures is vital for assessing its exposure to Middle Eastern geopolitical volatility.
1.1 LNG Import Volumes and Sovereign Origins (2024–2025)
The trajectory of Taiwan's LNG consumption highlights an accelerating and rigid dependence on the fuel for electricity generation and industrial activity. In 2024, the island imported a total of 21.5 million tonnes (mt) of LNG.3 By the end of 2025, driven primarily by the decommissioning of domestic nuclear assets and rising power demand from the semiconductor sector, total LNG imports surged by 12.8% year-over-year to reach 23.75 million tonnes.1 At an annualized cost of $12.65 billion in 2025, LNG represents both a critical physical energy lifeline and a massive ongoing economic outlay for the state.1
A granular breakdown of 2025 import customs data reveals a highly concentrated supplier base, with the vast majority of volume originating from a handful of exporting nations across the Asia-Pacific and the Middle East. 1.2 The Structure of Procurement: Long-Term SPAs vs. Spot Market Reliance
The physical vulnerability of Taiwan's LNG supply route through the Strait of Hormuz is severely exacerbated by its contractual procurement structure. LNG is generally purchased via two mechanisms: multi-decade Sale and Purchase Agreements (SPAs) that provide baseline price and volume stability, or on the highly volatile short-term spot market, which fluctuates wildly based on immediate global supply and demand dynamics.
State-owned CPC Corporation operates the entirety of Taiwan's LNG import infrastructure and manages the national procurement strategy. Currently, CPC maintains eight major long-term SPAs representing approximately 10.9 million tonnes per annum (MTPA) of firm import volume.13 Recent geopolitical efforts to diversify away from Middle Eastern reliance include two key long-term agreements with U.S.-based Cheniere Energy. These include an ongoing 2 MTPA contract signed in 2018 (running through 2046) and a newly executed 1.2 MTPA contract scheduled for delivery from 2026 through 2050, indexed to the Henry Hub price plus a fixed liquefaction fee.14
However, juxtaposing the 10.9 MTPA firm long-term volume against the 23.75 million tonnes of actual consumption in 2025 reveals a massive structural deficit in guaranteed supply. Over 50% of Taiwan's annual LNG requirements are currently sourced outside of foundational long-term SPAs, forcing CPC to rely heavily on short-term contracts and spot market tenders to bridge the gap.1 In the first quarter of 2025 alone, CPC was forced to issue nine spot tenders—four more than the prior year—to cover immediate power generation needs leading into the summer.3
If the Strait of Hormuz is closed, global LNG spot markets will immediately fracture and hyper-inflate. The sudden removal of approximately 20% of global supply would trigger an unprecedented price shock, historically estimated to push oil equivalents to $130–$300 per barrel.2 Because Asian spot buyers, including Taiwan, Japan, and South Korea, would be forced into a frantic bidding war for remaining Atlantic, African, and Australian cargoes, Taiwan's heavy reliance on the spot market transforms a physical supply risk into an existential fiscal risk.2 Even if alternative, non-Hormuz molecules are physically available, the immediate economic cost to Taipower and CPC to secure them on the open market would be devastating, draining foreign exchange reserves and forcing astronomical domestic tariff hikes.
2. Strategic Storage: Evaluating CPC Corporation's Buffers and Thermodynamic Constraints
Given the inherent risks of maritime supply chains spanning thousands of miles, domestic strategic storage acts as the primary shock absorber against localized disruptions, pirate activity, or geopolitical blockades. However, stockpiling LNG is thermodynamically complex and vastly different from storing traditional fossil fuels. Unlike crude oil or thermal coal, which can be stored indefinitely in simple steel tanks or open yards with negligible degradation, LNG must be maintained in cryogenic states at -162°C (-260°F).
Over time, despite heavy insulation, ambient heat transfer causes the cryogenic liquid to slowly revert to a gaseous state—a phenomenon known in the industry as "boil-off".16 This phase change builds hazardous physical pressure within the specialized storage tanks, necessitating constant, energy-intensive reliquefaction or immediate injection into the domestic pipeline distribution network. Because of these stringent boil-off constraints, building immense, long-term strategic LNG reserves is both economically prohibitive and technologically challenging.16
2.1 Demystifying the "11-Day Statutory Reserve"
A widely cited metric in discussions of Taiwanese energy security and defense planning is the "11-day reserve." It is critical to clarify that this figure does not represent the maximum physical capacity of Taiwan's receiving infrastructure, but rather a minimum mandatory statutory buffer dictated by the Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA).11
Following regulatory updates designed to bolster national resilience, natural gas import enterprises in Taiwan are legally required to maintain a security stockpile equivalent to at least 11 days of daily average gas supply through 2025.11 Concurrently, the total physical storage capacity requirement for the infrastructure network is mandated to be at least 20 days in 2025.11 By 2027, to address growing semiconductor demand, these regulatory requirements are slated to increase to a 14-day mandatory active stockpile and 24 days of physical storage capacity.11
However, independent assessments and stress-tests indicate that under the extreme conditions of a total naval blockade or absolute supply severance, the island's actual usable active stockpile would last for exactly 11 days of normal baseline use before total exhaustion.16 This stands in stark contrast to Taiwan's thermal coal reserves, which, due to their ease of storage, comfortably exceed 40 to 41 days of normal consumption.16
2.2 Volumetric Capacity and Burn Rates in MMBtu
To fully contextualize the fragility of this buffer, the storage capacity must be quantified in millions of British thermal units (MMBtu) against the daily burn rate of the island.
Burn Rate: In 2025, Taiwan consumed 23.75 million tonnes of LNG.1 This equates to an average daily burn rate of roughly 65,068 tonnes.
Energy Equivalent: One tonne of LNG is approximately equivalent to 52 MMBtu. Therefore, Taiwan's daily LNG consumption is approximately 3.38 million MMBtu.
Total Inventory Capacity: An 11-day active reserve implies that Taiwan typically holds only 37.2 million MMBtu (approximately 715,000 tonnes) of LNG on hand at any given moment to manage grid operations.
This gas is housed across CPC's highly concentrated receiving terminals. The legacy Yung-An terminal in Kaohsiung operates with a capacity of 10.5 MTPA.18 The Taichung terminal currently operates at 6 MTPA, equipped with six 160,000-cubic-meter above-ground storage tanks totaling 960,000 CBM.18 The newly commissioned Guantang terminal in Taoyuan (Taiwan's third terminal) adds 3 MTPA of capacity, having successfully received its first delivery of 63,780 tonnes in April 2025.3
While CPC is currently executing Phase 2 and Phase 3 expansions at the Taichung facility—adding two new 180,000 CBM tanks by the end of 2026 and four more by 2028—these infrastructure expansions are designed merely to keep pace with rising baseline demand from new gas-fired power plants, rather than offering a paradigm shift in long-term strategic reserve capabilities.19
In summary, if the 39% of LNG originating from the Middle East is abruptly cut off, Taiwan's 11-day active buffer provides less than two weeks for policymakers to secure vast volumes of emergency rerouted shipments on a deeply constrained global spot market before the electricity grid begins to experience absolute fuel starvation.
3. Power Grid Fragility: Modeling Taipower Generation Shortfalls
To project the systemic impact of a sustained LNG deficit on Taiwan's power grid, it is necessary to establish the baseline generation mix and the strict physical limits of electrical load balancing. The state utility, Taipower, operates an isolated, independent grid with zero interconnectivity to neighboring nations; unlike European countries, Taiwan cannot import emergency electricity across borders.20 Every megawatt consumed must be generated domestically in real-time.
3.1 The 2024–2025 Baseline Generation Mix
Driven by the relentless expansion of high-tech manufacturing, Taiwan's peak electricity demand routinely approaches 40 gigawatts (GW) during summer months, with total annual generation reaching 288.5 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2024.21
The 2024 annual power generation mix was deeply weighted toward imported fossil fuels:
Gas-Fired (LNG): 47.3%
Coal-Fired: 33.4%
Renewable Energy: 11.9%
Nuclear: 4.7%
Other (Fuel oil, pumped storage): 2.7% 4
Crucially, the grid's dispatchable flexibility is currently diminishing due to strict political mandates. Taiwan's long-standing energy policy requires the total phase-out of all nuclear generation. The Maanshan nuclear unit 1 was shut down in July 2024, and the final 951-megawatt (MW) Maanshan unit 2 was mandated for permanent decommissioning on May 17, 2025.3 Consequently, entering the latter half of 2025, nuclear power provides 0% of Taiwan's baseline generation, forcing gas and coal to absorb the entirety of the island's base load requirements.3
3.2 Backfill Potential: Coal and Renewables
If the Strait of Hormuz closes and LNG imports crater, Taipower's immediate operational response would dictate prioritizing the remaining gas for peak-load management while maximizing alternative thermal generation.
The Coal Buffer: Taiwan maintains a robust strategic stockpile of thermal coal, legally and physically rated at approximately 40 to 41 days of normal consumption.16 Anticipating Middle Eastern volatility, the Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA) has established emergency protocols to restart dormant coal infrastructure. Specifically, units 1 through 4 of the Hsinta coal-fired power plant—totaling 2.1 GW of generation capacity—were officially retired between 2023 and 2025 but preserved as emergency back-up assets.22 Under emergency dispatch rules, these highly polluting units can be reactivated if the operating reserve margin falls below 8%.22
The Renewable Limitation: While Taiwan has aggressively expanded solar photovoltaics and offshore wind (targeting 20% by 2025), renewables are highly intermittent and currently lack the grid-scale battery storage capable of compensating for multi-gigawatt baseload losses. They cannot dispatch on command to fill a sudden 24/7 void left by missing LNG.
3.3 Modeling Shortfall Scenarios: 30%, 50%, and 100% LNG Reductions
Based on a conservative peak load demand of roughly 40 GW, LNG-fired generation (accounting for 47.3% of the mix) must output approximately 18.9 GW of continuous power to maintain grid stability and prevent under-frequency load shedding.
Scenario A: 30% LNG Supply Drop
Lost Generation: 30% of 18.9 GW = 5.67 GW missing from the grid.
Backfill Activation: Taipower restarts all four emergency units at Hsinta, adding a maximum of 2.1 GW of coal base load. Renewables offer marginal daytime support but zero dispatchable nighttime support.
Net Shortfall: 3.57 GW.
Grid Impact: Taipower would be forced to implement Stage 1 and Stage 2 load shedding. Non-essential public infrastructure and heavy industrial users with interruptible contracts would face targeted, scheduled curtailments. Advanced semiconductor fabs would likely be protected through utility triage, relying on their internal UPS systems during minor frequency dips, though industrial output across secondary sectors would stall.
Scenario B: 50% LNG Supply Drop (The "Hormuz Plus" Scenario)
Lost Generation: 50% of 18.9 GW = 9.45 GW missing from the grid.
Backfill Activation: Maximum coal (Hsinta 2.1 GW) is exhausted.
Net Shortfall: 7.35 GW.
Grid Impact: This represents a loss of over 18% of total national peak capacity. Operating reserves (historically sitting around 3 GW or 8%) are entirely wiped out.21 Widespread, nationwide rolling blackouts are mathematically unavoidable. Taipower would be forced to institute strict power rationing across all strategic science parks. Semiconductor fabs would be ordered to cut consumption by 15% to 25%, forcing immediate tool idling and significant wafer scrapping.
Scenario C: 100% LNG Supply Drop (Total Blockade)
Lost Generation: 100% of 18.9 GW = 18.9 GW missing from the grid.
Backfill Activation: 2.1 GW coal.
Net Shortfall: 16.8 GW.
Grid Impact: Catastrophic grid failure. A 16.8 GW gap cannot be managed via orderly rolling blackouts; it poses an acute risk of cascading under-frequency trips and total grid collapse. Industrial power would be completely severed to preserve life-safety infrastructure (hospitals, water pumping, emergency communications). All semiconductor manufacturing would halt instantly.



4. Semiconductor Manufacturing Resilience: Power Quality and Voltage Sags
Before reaching the extremity of absolute rolling blackouts, severe grid instability manifests as micro-anomalies in power quality—specifically, voltage sags and AC frequency drops. Advanced semiconductor fabrication is uniquely hypersensitive to these micro-anomalies.
The cutting-edge lithography tools utilized by TSMC—particularly Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) systems manufactured by ASML—are arguably the most complex and power-hungry commercial machines on the planet. Operating a single EUV tool requires up to 1 megawatt of power simply to generate the plasma necessary for extreme ultraviolet light.23 When the national power grid experiences a supply-demand mismatch (such as a gas plant tripping offline due to fuel shortages), the grid's AC frequency deviates from its standard 60 Hz.
4.1 Historical Precedents: TSMC and UMC Vulnerability
Recent history provides vital empirical data on how Taiwanese fabrication plants react to minor grid perturbations:
May 13, 2021: A malfunction at the Hsinta Thermal Power Plant tripped four generators, instantly removing 2.2 GW of supply from the grid and triggering rolling blackouts for 4 million households.24 During this event, TSMC facilities experienced a "brief power dip," while UMC's Tainan fabs registered notable voltage drops.24 Both companies had to rely heavily on emergency UPS systems and massive diesel generators to prevent catastrophic tool crashes.
March 3, 2022: A turbine steam leak at a Taipower plant in Kaohsiung triggered an emergency shutdown, dropping 10.5 MW of supply and causing localized AC frequency to plummet out of safe operational tolerances.25 TSMC plants registered a severe voltage drop lasting between 400 and 1,000 milliseconds.25 At UMC's Nanke facility, factory equipment was actively affected by the drop and required manual restarting.25
While specific wafer loss counts and their exact financial impacts are closely guarded corporate secrets and routinely omitted from public disclosures, the necessity to restart tools implies a disruption to the continuous manufacturing process. While TSMC managed to prevent catastrophic financial losses during these brief micro-outages through robust backup circuits, the physical reality is that uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) and localized diesel generators are engineered specifically to bridge gaps measured in seconds or minutes, allowing for the safe, orderly shutdown of tools. They cannot sustain active high-volume manufacturing (HVM) during a multi-day or rolling blackout scenario.
4.2 The Limitations of SEMI F47 Power Quality Standards
The global semiconductor industry attempts to standardize tool resilience through SEMI F47, a rigorous specification governing voltage sag immunity. Under the revised SEMI F47-0706 standard, processing equipment must be engineered to withstand:
A 50% voltage sag for 0.2 seconds (200 milliseconds).
A 70% voltage sag for 0.5 seconds (500 milliseconds).
An 80% voltage sag for 1.0 second.27
However, field data has proven this standard to be insufficient in the face of macro-grid stress. Studies conducted by the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) and the SEMI Voltage Sag Immunity Task Force reveal that while F47 addresses single- and two-phase sags effectively, it does not mandate testing for complex three-phase events.30 Three-phase voltage sags account for up to 20% of tool downtime incidents in fabs.30 During the grid chaos of a 30% LNG shortfall, overlapping three-phase sags and repeated vector shifts would routinely bypass F47 protections, causing EUV tools to crash mid-exposure. A single tool crash forces the immediate scrapping of the active wafer lot, costing hundreds of thousands of dollars in ruined silicon and necessitating days of highly calibrated tool restart procedures.
4.3 Financial Impact and TSMC Risk Disclosures
TSMC is acutely aware of its exposure to Taiwan’s energy grid constraints, actively and comprehensively disclosing the financial perils of utility instability in its SEC Form 10-K filings, 20-F filings, and annual reports.
The filings emphasize that "outages, shortages or interruptions in electricity supply could further be exacerbated by changes in the energy policy of the governments".31 TSMC explicitly notes that its inability to secure reliable and uninterrupted electricity directly jeopardizes order fulfillment.31 Furthermore, TSMC is already bearing the heavy financial burden of Taiwan's transition away from cheap nuclear baseload; the company was subjected to a staggering 25% electricity tariff hike on April 1, 2024, followed by an additional 14% hike in October 2024.31
In its 2024 Annual Report, TSMC highlights that the primary detriment to its gross profit margins—partially offsetting revenue gains from higher capacity utilization—was the high power cost of the 3-nanometer production ramp coupled with these escalating electricity tariffs.33 Should grid instability lead to actual physical tool damage or structural facility damage (akin to the NT$5.3 billion loss recognized from the early 2024 earthquakes, which serves as a proxy for physical disruption costs 31), the financial impact would be devastating to both the company and its downstream clients.
5. Global Tech Supply Chain Contagion: Rolling Rationing and Inventory Depletion
If Taiwan initiates Stage 3 or Stage 4 load shedding (requiring 15% to 50% consumption cuts for heavy industry), high-volume manufacturing of advanced node chips will cease entirely. The global technology economy operates on a highly optimized, "just-in-time" silicon supply chain. The sudden loss of output from Taiwan would trigger a contagion event that ripples from the foundry to fabless designers to original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) in a matter of weeks.
5.1 Modeling the Loss of Advanced Node Capacity (3nm and 5nm)
The epicenter of global advanced semiconductor manufacturing is TSMC's Fab 18, a massive "GIGAFAB" located in the Southern Taiwan Science Park (STSP). Fab 18 serves as TSMC's primary, undisputed manufacturing hub for both the 5nm and 3nm process nodes.34
By the end of 2024, TSMC’s 3nm production capacity sat between 100,000 and 110,000 12-inch equivalent wafers per month (WPM).5 Driven primarily by voracious demand from NVIDIA for its AI accelerators, and Apple for its A-series and M-series mobile silicon, 3nm output is aggressively scaling, projected to reach an astounding 160,000 wafers per month by the end of 2025.5
A rolling blackout preventing full utilization would result in proportional wafer losses. A total grid shutdown over one month equates to the loss of 160,000 3nm wafers.
For Apple: A standard 300mm wafer can yield roughly 400 to 500 smartphone-sized SoC dies depending on the specific architecture. The loss of a single month's production roughly translates to a deficit of 30 to 40 million iPhones, iPads, and MacBooks entering the supply chain.
For NVIDIA: The calculus for artificial intelligence accelerators is vastly different. The forthcoming Blackwell architecture relies on enormous dies pushing the absolute limits of TSMC's reticle size, utilizing a custom 4NP (enhanced 4nm) process.36 A dual-die Blackwell package consumes massive wafer real estate; a 300mm wafer may yield fewer than 30 functional GPUs after defect rates are accounted for. Consequently, losing just a fraction of TSMC's capacity creates an immediate, insurmountable bottleneck in the deployment of global AI data centers.

6. The Geographic Diversification Fallacy: Limits of Offshore Fabs
In response to the acute geopolitical risks surrounding the Taiwan Strait, global policymakers have enacted sweeping subsidies—most notably the U.S. CHIPS and Science Act—to incentivize TSMC to offshore its manufacturing base. This has resulted in the rapid construction of new foundries in Arizona, Kumamoto (Japan), and Dresden (Germany).
A common misconception among financial analysts and policymakers is that these overseas facilities can act as immediate fail-safes should Taiwan's fabs go dark. A rigorous technical assessment of node capabilities and High-Volume Manufacturing (HVM) timelines proves this to be a fallacy; TSMC cannot shift its most advanced production out of Taiwan in the near term.
6.1 TSMC Arizona (United States)
Backed by an eventual $65 billion investment, TSMC is constructing a massive "megafab cluster" in Phoenix, Arizona.9 However, the capability of these plants significantly lags behind the cutting-edge processes utilized in Taiwan.
Fab 1: Entered high-volume production in Q4 2024. Crucially, this fab utilizes N4 (4nm) process technology.39 It cannot produce 3nm silicon.
Fab 2: Originally slated for 2028, TSMC accelerated the timeline due to immense U.S. customer demand. However, equipment installation will only begin in the third quarter of 2026, targeting 3nm mass production by the second half of 2027.8
Fab 3: Broke ground in April 2025. It will focus on next-generation 2nm and A16 processes, but is not slated for volume production until the end of the decade.39
If a Hormuz blockade cuts power to Taiwan in 2025 or 2026, TSMC Arizona provides exactly zero 3nm production capacity. While it can produce legacy 4nm chips for certain NVIDIA architectures and older Apple products, porting a highly optimized 3nm chip architecture backwards onto a 4nm node is physically impossible without years of complete silicon redesign and validation.
6.2 Kumamoto (Japan) and Dresden (Germany)
The geographic redundancy provided by Japan and Europe is similarly constrained by technological scope and target markets.
Kumamoto (JASM): The first fab in Kumamoto initiated mass production at the end of 2024. However, it is fundamentally geared toward mature and specialty technologies (12nm, 16nm, 22nm, and 28nm) to serve the Japanese automotive and image sensor markets.9 Construction of a second fab has begun, but it will not feature sub-5nm logic capabilities.
Dresden (ESMC): Fab construction is progressing, but the joint venture (involving Bosch, Infineon, and NXP) is explicitly targeted at automotive microcontrollers and industrial processes on highly mature nodes (28/22nm planar CMOS and 16/12nm FinFET).9
In summary, TSMC's corporate strategy purposefully retains absolute technological supremacy domestically. "Taiwan + 1" diversification efforts ensure that trailing-edge nodes for the automotive and industrial sectors have global redundancy, but the bleeding edge of artificial intelligence and consumer mobility (3nm, 5nm) remains entirely captive to the structural integrity of the Taipower grid.
Conclusion
The macroeconomic stability of the global technology sector is tethered to a highly fragile, multi-continental dependency chain. A sustained closure of the Strait of Hormuz represents an acute, systemic threat that transcends simple increases in the price of crude oil; it represents a direct physical threat to the operational viability of the digital economy.
With nearly 40% of its LNG imports transiting the Persian Gulf, and a structural reliance on volatile short-term spot markets due to insufficient long-term SPA coverage, Taiwan is exceptionally vulnerable to Middle Eastern maritime disruptions. Furthermore, by aggressively decommissioning its nuclear baseload in favor of gas, Taiwan has stripped its isolated power grid of critical dispatchable redundancy. The island's mandatory 11-day LNG storage buffer offers scant protection; mathematical modeling proves that even a 30% reduction in gas supplies would rapidly outstrip the emergency backfill capacity of legacy coal plants.
The ensuing rolling blackouts would instantly cripple TSMC's Fab 18, halting the production of up to 160,000 sub-5nm wafers per month. Because global technology giants operate on remarkably thin hardware inventories—with Apple possessing less than two weeks of supply, and NVIDIA roughly one fiscal quarter—the downstream contagion would be devastating and immediate. Finally, because overseas semiconductor initiatives in Arizona and Europe will lack 3nm production capabilities until at least 2027, the global economy has no immediate fallback. The security of the Strait of Hormuz is therefore inexorably linked to the viability of the modern digital economy, and any disruption there will immediately cascade into a global technological supply shock.
