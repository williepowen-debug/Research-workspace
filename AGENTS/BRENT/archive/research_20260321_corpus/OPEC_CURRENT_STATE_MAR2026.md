Global Energy Security in Crisis: OPEC+ Production, Spare Capacity, and Geopolitical Rupture in March 2026
The global macroeconomic landscape and international energy architecture are currently experiencing the most severe and synchronous systemic shock since the 1970s oil embargoes. Following the launch of the joint United States and Israeli military campaign against the Islamic Republic of Iran—codenamed "Operation Epic Fury"—on February 28, 2026, the foundational pillars of global crude oil logistics have been critically compromised.1 The immediate retaliation by Iranian forces and the subsequent declaration of the closure of the Strait of Hormuz have effectively trapped approximately 20 million barrels per day (mb/d) of crude oil, refined products, and liquefied natural gas (LNG) inside the Persian Gulf.4 This volume represents roughly 20% of total global petroleum consumption and a quarter of all seaborne oil trade, creating an unprecedented supply void.4
Compounding this Middle Eastern logistical paralysis is a simultaneous, severe constriction of Russian hydrocarbon exports driven by the newly enacted 20th European Union sanctions package, which has actively disabled a significant portion of Moscow's "shadow fleet".9 As global benchmark Brent crude futures surge past $90 per barrel in highly volatile trading, market participants are desperately attempting to quantify the true buffering capacity of the international system.11
This exhaustive analysis evaluates the state of the global oil market in early March 2026. It dissects the current Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and its allies (OPEC+) quota structure, actual production compliance, and the stark reality of the alliance's spare capacity. Furthermore, it assesses the absolute physical limits of strategic bypass infrastructure in Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Iraq, detailing why alternative routing cannot absorb the Hormuz shortfall. Finally, the analysis explores the deteriorating production reality in Russia and evaluates the efficacy of emergency mitigation scenarios, including the deployment of Strategic Petroleum Reserves (SPR).
Part 1: Current Quota Structure and Compliance Realities
The OPEC+ alliance entered the first quarter of 2026 operating under a highly structured, multi-tiered framework of baseline production targets and overlapping voluntary adjustments. Prior to the outbreak of regional hostilities, the primary macroeconomic concern dominating the cartel's strategy was the specter of a looming global supply glut.13 Rapid production growth from non-OPEC nations in the Americas—specifically the United States, Guyana, Brazil, and Canada—coupled with macroeconomic headwinds in China, had led the International Energy Agency (IEA) to forecast an impending oversupply of up to 3.7 to 4.0 mb/d for the year.15
To defend market stability and establish a floor for crude prices, the core eight members of the alliance—often referred to as the "Voluntary Eight" (V8), comprising Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman—had previously committed to withholding 1.65 mb/d of crude from the market.18 In late 2025, these nations formally agreed to pause the unwinding of these voluntary cuts through the first quarter (January, February, and March) of 2026, citing seasonal demand weakness.18

An analysis of the most recent production data from the IEA Oil Market Report and validated by OPEC secondary sources for January and February 2026 reveals critical divergences between the theoretical quotas assigned to member states and the physical barrels reaching the market.22 The alliance's production behavior reflects a complex mixture of strategic overproduction by well-capitalized Gulf states and involuntary underproduction by nations suffering from chronic infrastructure deficits or stringent international sanctions.

Granular Member Compliance and Structural Constraints
Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates (UAE): Prior to the late-February geopolitical rupture, both Saudi Arabia and the UAE were operating above their implied targets. The UAE exceeded its 3.40 mb/d quota by approximately 200,000 bpd, pumping 3.60 mb/d.22 This sustained overproduction reflects Abu Dhabi's long-standing strategic imperative to monetize its massive upstream capital investments and establish a higher baseline production level commensurate with its expanding capacity.24 Saudi Arabia similarly produced 10.28 mb/d against a target of 10.10 mb/d, a variance of 180,000 bpd.22 Market intelligence suggests that Riyadh systematically accelerated oil production and exports in the weeks preceding the US-Israeli strikes, pre-positioning crude inventory in anticipation of the conflict.25
Iraq and Kazakhstan: Iraq reported exact compliance in January at 4.34 mb/d.22 However, Baghdad has historically struggled with quota adherence due to the financial imperatives of the federal budget and the decentralized nature of production in the semi-autonomous Kurdistan region.27 Kazakhstan posted a minor overproduction of 20,000 bpd, pumping 1.31 mb/d against a 1.29 mb/d target, though its output remained constrained by prolonged power outages and weather-related terminal disruptions at the Caspian Pipeline Consortium (CPC) network.22
Nigeria: Africa's largest producer continues to exhibit chronic, structural underproduction. With an implied target of 1.50 mb/d, Nigeria achieved only 1.40 mb/d in early 2026, representing a 100,000 bpd shortfall.22 Despite recent improvements in foreign exchange reserves reported by the Central Bank of Nigeria, the upstream sector remains crippled by years of severe underinvestment, persistent pipeline vandalism, and widespread crude theft in the Niger Delta.29 This operational degradation prevents Nigeria from capitalizing on elevated price environments.
The Exempt Producers: Iran, Venezuela, and Libya:
Three key OPEC members operate entirely outside the quota system due to extraordinary domestic or international circumstances.
Iran: Prior to the outbreak of war, Iranian production had steadily climbed to 3.45 mb/d, recovering significantly from the depths of the "maximum pressure" sanctions era.22 The Islamic Republic achieved this by heavily discounting its crude and utilizing a vast network of "dark" vessels to export primarily to independent refiners in China.32
Venezuela: Plagued by decades of mismanagement and a severe lack of capital expenditure in its heavy oil fields, Venezuela's production remains anemic. In early 2026, output languished at 0.78 mb/d.22 Even with the geopolitical shifts following recent US military actions and regime changes in Caracas, the systemic degradation of Venezuelan infrastructure dictates that any meaningful production recovery will require years of sustained foreign investment.13
Libya: Operating at 1.33 mb/d, Libyan output remains highly volatile, subject to sudden blockades by competing domestic political factions and severe weather incidents affecting Mediterranean loading terminals.22

As Iranian retaliatory strikes targeted Gulf infrastructure and maritime insurance providers suspended coverage for vessels traversing the Strait of Hormuz, the global market pivoted from managing a glut to confronting an acute shortage.4
Recognizing the severity of the supply shock, the OPEC+ V8 convened an emergency virtual meeting on Sunday, March 1, 2026.35 Abandoning their prior commitment to pause output increases through March, the group authorized an accelerated "production adjustment" of 206,000 bpd, scheduled to take effect in April 2026.37 The official communique cited "a steady global economic outlook and current healthy market fundamentals," carefully avoiding direct reference to the kinetic warfare enveloping their primary export routes.36

Part 2: Spare Capacity—The 5-6M bpd Myth vs. Engineering Reality
A dangerous and pervasive heuristic within global energy markets and mainstream financial modeling assumes that the OPEC+ alliance maintains a highly elastic spare capacity buffer of between 5.0 and 6.0 million barrels per day. Rigorous examination of current upstream field data, pipeline throughput constraints, and independent auditing metrics flatly denies this assumption.42
As of early March 2026, the true effective spare capacity of the OPEC+ alliance stands at 4.35 million bpd.22 Of this total, the OPEC-9 members hold approximately 3.39 mb/d, with non-OPEC allies (primarily Russia and Kazakhstan) theoretically holding the remainder.22
The Illusion of Rapid Deployment: 90 Days vs. 6 Months
Understanding the utility of spare capacity requires differentiating between the theoretical definition and the engineering reality of wellhead physics. OPEC officially defines Maximum Sustainable Capacity (MSC) as the average maximum number of barrels per day of crude oil that can be brought online within 90 days and sustained continuously for one full year.43
However, energy economists and reservoir engineers note a critical distinction in deployment timelines:
Within 90 Days (Rapid Deployment): Reactivating idled capacity is not akin to turning a faucet. It requires the repressurization of dormant reservoirs, the recalibration of surface separation facilities, and the careful management of associated gas and water cuts. Of the total 4.35 mb/d buffer, an estimated maximum of 2.0 to 2.5 mb/d can realistically be surged to market within a 30-to-90-day window without risking permanent damage to the geological formations.45
6+ Months (Capital Intensive Expansion): The remainder of the capacity requires extensive lead times. Unlocking these volumes involves mobilizing drilling rigs, completing new lateral wells, and significantly upgrading pipeline gathering networks. Similarly, price-responsive non-OPEC production, such as the United States shale sector, typically exhibits a 6-to-12-month lag from initial capital deployment and rig activation to sustained flow increases at the export terminal.47



Country Breakdown: Stated Nameplate vs. True Spare Capacity
The distribution of this spare capacity is heavily skewed, creating immense systemic vulnerability. Over 65% of the world's immediate surge capacity is concentrated in two nations: Saudi Arabia and the UAE.49
Saudi Arabia: Saudi Aramco represents the absolute bedrock of global energy security. The Kingdom maintains a stated Maximum Sustainable Capacity (nameplate) of 12.11 mb/d.22 Against an actual January 2026 production level of 10.28 mb/d, Saudi Arabia holds a verified effective spare capacity of 1.84 mb/d.22 This is "true spare"—mechanically proven, verified by prior production peaks, and highly reliable. However, in the context of the March 2026 crisis, this theoretical cushion is practically useless; if the crude cannot safely transit the Arabian Peninsula to international waters, the spare capacity is functionally zero.41
United Arab Emirates: Driven by an aggressive, multi-billion-dollar upstream capital expenditure campaign led by ADNOC, the UAE has successfully elevated its sustainable nameplate capacity to 4.28 mb/d.22 With current production running at 3.60 mb/d, Abu Dhabi controls approximately 0.67 mb/d of effective spare capacity.22 Like the Saudi volumes, this capacity is highly reliable but profoundly vulnerable to maritime chokepoints.
Iraq, Kuwait, and Russia: The remainder of the cartel possesses negligible rapid-response capability. Kuwait holds a marginal, localized buffer of 0.30 mb/d.22 Iraq, despite possessing vast reserves, currently holds absolute zero effective spare capacity.22 Its infrastructure was operating at its maximum threshold limits prior to the war-induced pipeline and field shut-ins.22 Russia, theoretically holding a vast production base, is currently experiencing negative spare capacity. Due to targeted sanctions and a lack of export vectors, Russian capacity is not "spare" but rather "stranded," with forced shut-ins occurring across its Siberian fields.52
To address historical discrepancies in self-reported production data, OPEC+ authorized a landmark capacity review mechanism in late 2025. Between January and September 2026, a consortium of independent, U.S.-based auditing firms will rigorously evaluate the true MSC of 19 non-sanctioned members. This independently verified data will serve as the unalterable foundation for the 2027 production baselines, fundamentally reshaping how the cartel measures its own market power.43
Part 3: The Saudi Arabia Hormuz Bypass and Regional Infrastructure
The eruption of the US-Iran war and the subsequent de facto blockade of the Strait of Hormuz has transformed the global energy security paradigm from a question of resource availability into a pure logistics equation. Under normal conditions, approximately 20 million barrels per day—encompassing roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids and a vast proportion of Qatari LNG—transit this 21-mile-wide maritime corridor.4 With the waterway rendered impassable due to severe kinetic risks and the complete withdrawal of standard maritime Protection and Indemnity (P&I) insurance, Gulf producers are entirely reliant on overland bypass infrastructure.4
An assessment of these alternative routes reveals a stark reality: combined bypass systems can theoretically transport only 6.5 to 7.0 mb/d under optimal, untested conditions.56 This leaves an insurmountable net supply shortfall of approximately 13 to 14 million bpd that simply cannot reach global markets.40
Bypass Infrastructure Capabilities (March 2026)




The East-West Pipeline (Petroline) - Saudi Arabia
Saudi Aramco's primary geopolitical shield is the East-West Pipeline (Petroline). This massive, 1,200-kilometer engineering marvel connects the kingdom's eastern processing hubs at Abqaiq directly to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, entirely bypassing the Persian Gulf.58
Capacity Limits: Petroline possesses a core nameplate capacity of 5.0 mb/d.58 During extreme emergencies, Aramco has demonstrated the technical capability to temporarily boost throughput to roughly 7.0 mb/d by converting adjacent, parallel Natural Gas Liquids (NGL) pipelines to transport crude oil.63
Operational Reality and Market Share: Prior to the February crisis, the Red Sea route was highly underutilized. West coast exports averaged between 800,000 and 2.0 million bpd, representing roughly 12% to 28% of total Saudi seaborne exports, with the vast majority flowing from the eastern Ras Tanura terminal through Hormuz.58 However, as the Hormuz threat materialized, Aramco executed a radical logistical pivot. In the first four days of March 2026 alone, Saudi Arabia loaded 10 million barrels onto five Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) at Yanbu, effectively tripling the port's daily export rate to 2.5 mb/d.59
The Realistic Export Ceiling: Despite Petroline's 7.0 mb/d burst capacity, the ultimate chokepoint lies at the waterfront. The Yanbu marine terminal complex (including the Al-Muajjiz South Terminal) has a maximum sustainable loading limit of 4.3 to 4.5 mb/d.59 Furthermore, the pipeline must constantly supply domestic refineries clustered in Yanbu (including SAMREF and YSREF), which consume approximately 1.0 mb/d of the incoming crude.62 Accounting for these domestic obligations and marine loading limits, the realistic, absolute ceiling for non-Hormuz Saudi crude exports is firmly capped at 3.3 to 3.5 mb/d.
UAE Habshan-Fujairah Pipeline (ADCOP)
The United Arab Emirates relies on the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline (ADCOP), a 400-kilometer overland route designed specifically to mitigate Hormuz vulnerability. ADCOP transports premium Murban crude from the inland Habshan fields across the Hajar Mountains to the deepwater port of Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman.58
Capacity and Status: ADCOP holds an original nameplate capacity of 1.5 mb/d, which has been stretched to a reported peak operational capacity of 1.8 mb/d.58 Before the war, the pipeline was running at a healthy 71% utilization rate (approx. 1.1 mb/d).65 However, unlike Yanbu, which remains relatively insulated on the Red Sea, Fujairah's proximity to the conflict zone has proven disastrous. On March 3, 2026, drone strikes directly impacted Fujairah's vast storage tank farms, triggering fires and forcing a severe slowdown of loading operations.51 Consequently, ADCOP's current operational status is classified as highly intermittent, rendering it an unreliable failsafe for global markets.51
The Northern Route: Iraq-Turkey Pipeline (Kirkuk-Ceyhan)
For Iraq, OPEC's second-largest producer, the crisis poses an existential economic threat. While over 90% of its 4.3 mb/d output flows from southern fields (such as Rumaila) and is totally dependent on maritime transit out of Basra via Hormuz, the nation possesses one northern land route: the Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline flowing to the Mediterranean coast of Turkey.27
Capacity and Status: The Iraq-Turkey pipeline possesses a historical design capacity of 1.6 mb/d.71 However, years of infrastructure degradation, geopolitical sabotage, and protracted legal arbitration between Baghdad and Ankara have reduced its usable throughput to a mere 300,000 to 400,000 bpd.72
The March 2026 Collapse: In a devastating blow to bypass efforts, Iraq completely suspended all crude production in the northern region on March 3, 2026. Facing intense aerial bombardments and drone activity in the semi-autonomous Kurdistan region related to the broader war, authorities instituted a total precautionary shut-in. This removed 220,000 bpd of federal state production and 125,000 bpd of regional output (including flows from the Shaikan and Atrush fields).61 Furthermore, the rapid filling of the 1.2 million barrel storage tanks at the Ceyhan terminus created insurmountable backpressure.61 Consequently, the sole northern bypass route is entirely dormant, operating at 0 mb/d.60 In the south, the lack of tankers forced Baghdad to shut down operations at the massive 1.5 mb/d Rumaila field, sending shockwaves through the nation's oil-dependent economy.51
Part 4: Russia's Production Reality and the Shadow Fleet Collapse
While the Persian Gulf states grapple with a logistical crisis born of intense kinetic warfare, the Russian Federation is enduring a systemic export collapse engineered by comprehensive regulatory suffocation. This collapse operates independently of the Middle East conflict, creating a synchronous, dual-front supply shock to the global economy. In January and February 2026, Russian crude production averaged roughly 9.3 mb/d against an OPEC+ quota of 9.57 mb/d.22 This structural underperformance of 270,000 bpd is not an act of cartel compliance, but a manifestation of deep logistical paralysis and rapidly exhaustible storage capacity.22
The 20th Sanctions Package and the Shadow Fleet
The primary mechanism driving Russia's export collapse is the European Union's formidable 20th sanctions package, formally proposed and enacted in early 2026.9 This legislation represents a definitive escalation in Western economic warfare. It abandons the previous, highly porous "price cap" regime in favor of a total, absolute ban on the provision of all European maritime services—including vital Protection and Indemnity (P&I) insurance, classification, ship management, and port access—to any vessel transporting Russian hydrocarbons, regardless of the sale price.76
To circumvent previous restrictions, Moscow had methodically assembled a "shadow fleet"—a covert network of aging, poorly maintained tankers operating under opaque ownership structures and flags of convenience (such as Sierra Leone, Cameroon, or Gabon), frequently utilizing dangerous dark ship-to-ship (STS) transfers at sea.77 However, the new EU package specifically designated 43 additional vessels, bringing the total number of strictly banned shadow fleet tankers to 640.9 The European Union also explicitly lists 598 vessels completely banned from European ports and services, while the United States asserts extraterritorial rights against 183 vessels.77
This regulatory dragnet has proven highly effective. Coordinated kinetic and legal interdictions by the US, UK, and EU—highlighted by the French Navy's interception of the Arctic crude tanker Grinch in the Mediterranean—have generated a massive chilling effect across illicit maritime logistics.77 Naval intelligence and satellite tracking data confirm that at least 20% of Russia's shadow fleet has been forced to permanently halt operations due to an inability to secure even secondary-market insurance or find safe harbor.10 This paralysis translates to an estimated 150 million barrels of Russian crude currently stranded at sea in floating storage, effectively functioning as a massive freeze on the Kremlin's capital.52 Furthermore, major buyers in India and China, fearful of secondary sanctions and logistical delays, have sharply curtailed Urals purchases, severing Moscow from its primary Asian revenue streams.52
The Onshore Storage Crisis and Impending Shut-ins
The severe restriction of seaborne export vectors has triggered a catastrophic physical bottleneck within Russia's domestic energy infrastructure. Seaborne exports have plummeted below 3.0 mb/d, while domestic production remains around 9.0 mb/d, resulting in a staggering daily surplus of approximately 2.5 million barrels that can neither be refined nor exported.52 Furthermore, sustained Ukrainian drone strikes against deep-interior Russian refineries (such as the major facilities in Ryazan and Volgograd) have eliminated the domestic refining buffer that might otherwise absorb this excess crude.85
Consequently, Russia's physical storage capacity has reached critical operational limits:
Onshore Tanks: The Russian Federation possesses only about 32 million barrels of commercial onshore storage capacity. At the current surplus rate, this provides a mere three to four days of national production cover.86
Pipeline Network: Transneft, the state-owned pipeline monopoly, operates a vast network that can hold an additional 100 million barrels, acting as a secondary reserve.86
Utilization: Satellite tracking platforms indicate that roughly half of the commercial onshore capacity and vast stretches of the pipeline network are already at maximum utilization.86
If export flows do not normalize within weeks, Russian producers face a dire physical reality: forced, large-scale well shut-ins. Energy analysts project that Russia will be compelled to physically shut in up to 300,000 bpd of production by April 2026.83 Unlike the highly flexible shale basins of North America, the thermodynamic characteristics of mature Russian oilfields in Western Siberia dictate that shutting down extraction in sub-zero environments results in severe waxing and freezing of the wellbores. These forced shut-ins are partially irreversible, meaning the loss of this capacity will permanently degrade Russia's baseline production capability, threatening the long-term viability of the sector.52
Part 5: Systemic Shock and Emergency Scenario Assessment
The convergence of the Hormuz maritime blockade and the systemic collapse of Russian export logistics has generated an environment ripe for extreme, uncontrolled price volatility. In the week following the initiation of Operation Epic Fury, Brent crude futures spiked by nearly 35%, violently breaching the $90 per barrel threshold as traders priced in a worst-case supply destruction scenario.11
Historical Context of OPEC+ Emergency Meetings
The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries historically convenes emergency summits only when extraordinary macroeconomic or geopolitical shocks threaten to permanently destabilize the physical balance of the market. Notable historical precedents include the 1990 Iraqi invasion of Kuwait, the 2008 Global Financial Crisis, the demand collapse during the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, and the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine. The virtual summit held on Sunday, March 1, 2026, was convened under similar existential duress.36
However, the strategic options available to the alliance today are severely constrained by geographic reality. During the 1990 Gulf War, a sudden 4.0 mb/d supply loss from Iraq and Kuwait was rapidly and effectively offset by Saudi Arabia ramping up its massive idle capacity. In 2026, while Saudi Arabia holds 1.84 mb/d of verified spare capacity, over 90% of the cartel's total spare volume is geographically locked inside the Persian Gulf.22
Member states have publicly acknowledged this structural trap. Following Iran's aggressive radio broadcasts warning commercial vessels away from the Strait, and subsequent drone attacks on the UAE's Fujairah port and Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG facilities, Gulf leaders realize that above-quota production is meaningless.51 As long as commercial shipping titans (such as Maersk, MSC, Hapag-Lloyd, and CMA-CGM) flatly refuse to transit the Strait due to extreme kinetic risk and voided insurance, excess Gulf production cannot reach the Asian or European markets that desperately require it.4
Realistic Mitigation Options: An Emergency Assessment
With OPEC+ effectively paralyzed by maritime logistics, the burden of market stabilization shifts entirely to international response mechanisms, alternative production, and inevitable demand destruction.
1. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) Releases: The most immediate and viable mechanism to temper the price shock is a massive, coordinated release of strategic reserves orchestrated by the International Energy Agency (IEA). Currently, IEA member nations hold approximately 1.2 billion barrels in public emergency stocks, supported by an additional 600 million barrels in government-mandated industry stocks.8
Duration and Efficacy: These strategic reserves are meticulously engineered to provide approximately 90 days of import coverage in the event of a severe disruption.92 While an aggressive SPR release can effectively dampen panic-driven price spikes and reduce the geopolitical "war premium," historical data (such as the early phases of the 2022 Ukraine conflict) demonstrates that it is a finite, short-term blunt instrument.48 It cannot permanently replace a sustained, structural deficit of 15 to 20 mb/d.
2. Non-OPEC Production Acceleration: A sustained high-price environment (exceeding $90/bbl) will heavily incentivize rapid production increases outside the Middle Eastern conflict zone. This supply response will be driven primarily by the Americas, specifically the United States, Guyana, Brazil, and Canada.13 The U.S. shale sector, currently operating near record highs of 13.7 mb/d, is highly price-elastic.17 However, the mobilization of capital, securing of drilling rigs, and completion of fracking operations entails an unavoidable 60-to-90-day lag before new crude physically enters the market, offering zero immediate relief to the current crisis.47
3. Total Route Redirection and Geoeconomic Costs: For the limited volumes that can bypass Hormuz via the Saudi Petroline or the UAE's ADCOP, the geoeconomic cost of transport is rising exponentially. Cargoes loading from Yanbu on the Red Sea face exorbitant freight premiums and must navigate secondary threat vectors, including the lingering risk of Houthi disruptions in the Bab el-Mandeb strait.93 For major Asian importers—particularly China, India, Japan, and South Korea, which rely on the Persian Gulf for over 60% of their crude—this disruption translates immediately into compressed refinery margins, severe industrial shortages, and massive inflationary pressure on their broader economies.11
Strategic Conclusions
The global oil market in early March 2026 is defined not by a geological lack of hydrocarbons, but by the sudden, violent severing of the critical logistical arteries required to distribute them. OPEC+ production quotas, compliance metrics, and spare capacity declarations are mathematically irrelevant when the primary physical conduit for 20% of the world's daily consumption is barricaded by kinetic warfare.
Simultaneously, the West's highly successful regulatory suppression of the Russian shadow fleet removes a vital secondary supply source precisely at the moment Middle Eastern barrels vanish from the market. Unless aggressive diplomatic intervention or decisive military action rapidly restores safe commercial transit through the Strait of Hormuz, the global economy faces a sustained period of severe, triple-digit energy prices. The ensuing inflationary shock will inevitably force rapid demand destruction across both OECD and emerging markets, likely precipitating a severe global economic contraction long before new, non-OPEC supplies can be mobilized to rebalance the international system.
