Historical Demand Destruction Timelines: An Analysis of US Gasoline and Global Crude Markets During Major Price Shocks
1. Introduction: The Mechanics of Demand Destruction in Global Energy Markets
The relationship between crude oil price shocks and petroleum demand destruction is one of the most rigorously scrutinized dynamics in global macroeconomic analysis. Demand destruction—defined strictly as the permanent or semi-permanent downward shift in the consumption curve of a commodity due to prohibitively high prices—does not occur instantaneously.1 Because modern industrialized economies are highly petroleum-intensive and short-term demand for transportation fuels is notoriously inelastic, consumers and industrial actors initially absorb price spikes by reallocating disposable income, depleting savings, or absorbing corporate margin compression.3 Only when prices remain elevated past a specific duration and threshold does behavioral adaptation, operational curtailment, and structural substitution force an unambiguous contraction in physical consumption.5
Historically, severe crude oil price shocks have served as critical catalysts for global recessions, a phenomenon extensively documented in macroeconomic literature tracking the events of 1973, 1979, and 1990.7 To comprehend the velocity and magnitude of demand destruction, it is necessary to examine the precise timelines from the onset of a geopolitical supply shock to the inflection point of verifiable demand contraction.1 This analysis investigates three highly comparable systemic shocks: the 1990 Persian Gulf War, the 2011–2012 Iranian Sanctions and Arab Spring, and the 2022 Russia-Ukraine conflict.10
By deconstructing the weekly trajectories of Brent crude prices alongside high-frequency United States Energy Information Administration (EIA) implied gasoline demand data and International Energy Agency (IEA) global market reports, this report identifies the temporal lag between price spikes and demand capitulation.14 Furthermore, it dissects the varied catalysts for eventual price reversals—ranging from organic demand destruction to coordinated supply responses and Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) interventions—while identifying early warning indicators in auxiliary sectors such as aviation and commercial freight.17 Through this rigorous empirical examination, a predictive framework is established to assess future vulnerabilities, culminating in an analysis of a hypothetical 2026 Strait of Hormuz closure scenario.20
2. Theoretical Framework: Price Elasticity, Market Transmission, and Macroeconomic Impact
Before dissecting the historical case studies, it is imperative to establish the transmission mechanisms through which a crude oil supply shock permeates the real economy and induces demand destruction. The global oil market operates on a delicate equilibrium characterized by low spare capacity and highly inelastic short-term demand.1 When a geopolitical event abruptly removes physical barrels from the market, prices must spike violently to force the market back into balance.23 This rebalancing is achieved either by incentivizing latent supply to come online or by forcing marginal consumers to exit the market entirely through price rationing.24
2.1 The Lagged Response of Retail Gasoline and the Permanent Income Hypothesis
The transmission of global crude oil prices to local retail gasoline prices involves an inherent temporal lag. Empirical analysis indicates that a $10 per barrel increase in the spot price of crude oil typically translates into an approximate 24-cent per gallon increase in retail gasoline prices, with the full pass-through taking roughly four to eight weeks depending on refinery configurations and regional distribution bottlenecks.26 Consequently, implied gasoline demand—measured by the EIA as "product supplied," which is a proxy derived from refinery production, net imports, and primary inventory changes—rarely shows an immediate reaction to a crude spike.28
Consumers initially absorb the higher costs, reducing discretionary spending in other economic sectors before altering their commuting or travel behaviors.4 This delayed behavioral shift is heavily influenced by the Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH). Under the PIH framework, consumers base their spending on their expected long-term average income rather than current income.4 When a price shock hits, consumers initially perceive it as a transient anomaly and maintain their consumption patterns by utilizing savings or credit. It is only when the price elevation proves to be sticky and structural that consumers adjust their permanent expectations and materially reduce their volumetric consumption of motor fuels.2



2.2 Secondary Inventories and Data Obfuscation
A secondary factor complicating the immediate measurement of demand destruction is the behavior of wholesale and secondary inventories.2 When a major supply shock occurs, wholesale distributors, regional jobbers, and large commercial consumers often engage in aggressive precautionary stockpiling to hedge against future physical shortages or further price escalation.31 Because the EIA's "product supplied" metric measures the disappearance of product from primary storage facilities (refineries and major bulk terminals), a surge in secondary stockpiling artificially inflates the implied demand data.28
This creates a paradoxical scenario wherein apparent demand remains robust or even increases during the initial weeks of a price crisis, masking the underlying reality that end-user retail consumption is beginning to falter.30 It is only after these secondary storage capacities are exhausted and wholesale buyers step back from the market that the true magnitude of demand destruction becomes visible in the primary data sets.2
2.3 Early Warning Indicators: The Industrial Canary in the Coal Mine
Because aggregate gasoline demand data often lags and is subject to statistical noise, analysts rely on early warning indicators in highly sensitive, margin-constrained industrial sectors to identify the onset of demand destruction before it registers in broad macroeconomic prints.33
The commercial aviation sector serves as a premier leading indicator. Aviation is highly sensitive to jet fuel prices, which track crude closely but are subject to their own intense refining constraints.35 Unlike retail automobile consumers, commercial airlines operate on razor-thin margins and cannot easily absorb sudden, unhedged cost escalations.19 Their primary operational defense mechanism is to rapidly ground older, fuel-inefficient aircraft and aggressively slash route capacity.19 This immediate capacity reduction serves as an unambiguous, early indicator of industrial demand destruction, often preceding retail gasoline pullbacks by several weeks.35
The commercial trucking and logistics sector provides another vital early warning metric. The freight sector relies exclusively on middle distillates (diesel) and is similarly margin-compressed.18 While major trucking firms utilize contractual fuel surcharges to pass volatile fuel costs onto shippers, there is typically a 45- to 60-day systemic lag between the incurrence of higher fuel costs and the collection of the surcharge revenue.38 During severe, rapid price spikes, this lag severely strains the working capital of smaller operators and owner-operators, leading to immediate capacity constraints, bankruptcies, and a measurable deceleration in domestic trucking tonnage indexes.18
With this macroeconomic and theoretical framework established, the analysis proceeds to the empirical evidence and precise chronologies of the three defining historical case studies.
3. Case Study 1: The 1990 Persian Gulf War Oil Shock
The 1990 oil price shock serves as the archetypal short-duration, high-intensity geopolitical supply disruption. Triggered by the Republic of Iraq's military invasion of the State of Kuwait on August 2, 1990, the event immediately removed approximately 4.3 million barrels per day (mbpd) of combined Iraqi and Kuwaiti crude production from the global market.13 This massive volume represented a critical fraction of global supply at a time when the broader macroeconomic environment was already exhibiting signs of severe fragility.39
3.1 Price Timeline and Trajectory
In the weeks immediately preceding the invasion, the global oil market was relatively well-supplied, and Brent crude was trading in a stable, tight range. During the week of July 30, 1990, Brent averaged $18.98 per barrel.14 Following the invasion on August 2, panic gripped both the paper derivatives and physical markets. By August 3, Brent had surged to $24.13, and the upward momentum accelerated relentlessly throughout the month, pushing prices to $32.35 by August 23.13
The market traded heavily on the profound geopolitical fear that Saddam Hussein's armored forces might not stop at Kuwait but could cross the border into Saudi Arabia.13 Such an event would have jeopardized the absolute core of the world's remaining spare production capacity. Driven by this extreme risk premium, the price of Brent crude reached its absolute peak of $41.45 per barrel on September 27, 1990—exactly eight weeks post-invasion.14 A secondary, slightly lower peak of $41.15 was recorded on October 11, solidifying the autumn of 1990 as a period of unprecedented price severity.14
3.2 Demand Inflection and Empirical Evidence
The macroeconomic environment of 1990 was uniquely vulnerable to an energy shock. The United States economy had already entered a mild recession in July 1990, mere weeks before the invasion, driven by restrictive monetary policy implemented by the Federal Reserve to cool late-cycle inflation.39 The sudden doubling of global oil prices acted as a severe, highly regressive tax on the global consumer, violently accelerating the economic contraction.39
Determining the exact week United States gasoline demand showed a measurable decline (greater than 3% year-over-year) during this period requires carefully navigating statistical artifacts in the EIA's historical data.28 Initial "product supplied" data for motor gasoline in August and September 1990 appeared artificially resilient. During the week ending August 3, 1990, EIA data recorded total finished motor gasoline product supplied at a robust 7.605 mbpd, and by the week ending September 7, it remained elevated at 7.326 mbpd.41 This resilience was heavily driven by secondary marketers and wholesale distributors aggressively replenishing and hoarding stocks in anticipation of military escalation, physical rationing, or further explosive price hikes.30
However, adjusting for this secondary stock build, true underlying consumption began to plummet as the U.S. economy rapidly shed jobs and vehicle-miles traveled (VMT) contracted sharply.30 By October 1990—roughly 10 to 12 weeks after the initial price spike and lagging the ultimate price peak by two to four weeks—gasoline demand destruction became unambiguously visible.30
The EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook archives reveal that domestic petroleum demand declined structurally, with peak-to-trough demand destruction measuring approximately 200,000 to 400,000 barrels per day in annualized terms for 1991.30 On a weekly volatility basis, the peak-to-trough swing was much more severe; weekly gasoline product supplied fell from highs of roughly 7.6 mbpd in the late summer of 1990 down to an abysmal 6.433 mbpd by February 15, 1991, representing a catastrophic short-term drop in physical deliveries exceeding 1.0 mbpd before stabilizing.41 This structural demand destruction ultimately contributed to a peak-to-trough Gross Domestic Product (GDP) decline of 2.6% for the broader U.S. economy during the recessionary period.39
3.3 Early Warning Indicators
The most glaring and immediate early warning indicator of demand destruction in the autumn of 1990 emerged from the commercial aviation sector. Jet fuel prices skyrocketed synchronously with crude oil, devastating the heavily leveraged and fiercely competitive U.S. airline industry.19
Airlines that lacked the capital liquidity to prepay for fuel at lower prices or maintain robust hedging programs were immediately insolvent.19 Between late 1990 and early 1991, major legacy carriers including Pan American World Airways (Pan Am), Eastern Air Lines, Trans World Airlines (TWA), and Continental Airlines either filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection or underwent severe liquidation.19 The rapid grounding of older, inefficient fleet assets resulted in a sharp, immediate contraction in jet fuel demand that predated the broader retail gasoline pullback, serving as a flawless leading indicator of the broader macroeconomic damage.19
3.4 Catalysts for Price Reversal
The reversal of the 1990 price spike was as violent and sudden as its ascent. Following the peak in late September, prices began a volatile, uneven descent, dropping to $27.45 by October 22 as the market realized an immediate Iraqi invasion of Saudi Arabia was unlikely.14 However, the ultimate capitulation of the oil market occurred in mid-January 1991. On January 16, Brent traded at $30.28; by January 17—the launch of the U.S.-led coalition's Operation Desert Storm—prices crashed a staggering 37% in 48 hours to $21.10.14
While profound demand destruction undoubtedly softened the market's fundamental balance over the autumn months, the rapid price reversal was primarily dictated by a massive, coordinated supply response and unprecedented political intervention.44 Saudi Arabia surged its production by an astonishing 3.2 mbpd, fully utilizing its latent spare capacity to backfill the lost Iraqi and Kuwaiti barrels.44
Concurrently, United States President George H.W. Bush announced the authorization of a 1.125 mbpd emergency drawdown from the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR), which was supplemented by pledges of an additional 0.8 mbpd from allied governments.44 Academic models analyzing this specific intervention estimate that the SPR releases after the invasion of Kuwait resulted in a sustained $2-per-barrel reduction in the real price of oil after seven months.17 Ultimately, the psychological impact of overwhelming military success, combined with the Saudi physical supply ramp, were the dominant forces that shattered the price shock.13
4. Case Study 2: The 2011–2012 Iran Sanctions and Arab Spring Super-Cycle
Unlike the sharp, transient, military-resolved shock of 1990, the 2011–2012 oil market experienced a prolonged, structural elevation in prices that fundamentally reshaped global consumption patterns. Driven by the geopolitical upheaval of the Arab Spring—which systematically removed 1.5 mbpd of highly prized light, sweet crude from Libya—and subsequent stringent U.S. and European sanctions targeting the Islamic Republic of Iran's nuclear program, global markets were severely constrained.11
4.1 Price Timeline and Trajectory
The global macroeconomic backdrop in 2011 was characterized by an aggressive, energy-intensive recovery in emerging markets—notably China and India—which collided violently with a stagnant supply picture.22 Brent crude breached the psychological $100 per barrel threshold in February 2011 as the Libyan civil war intensified.22
With global spare production capacity at critically low levels, the market entered a sustained period of deep backwardation. Prices escalated rapidly, spiking to $126.46 by April 11, 2011.14 Although prices experienced a brief seasonal dip during the late summer, the implementation of severe European embargoes on Iranian crude catalyzed a secondary, sustained surge, pushing Brent to an annual peak of $128.14 on March 13, 2012.11 For roughly 18 consecutive months across 2011 and 2012, Brent crude averaged over $111 per barrel, marking the first time in financial history that the global benchmark averaged above $100 for a full calendar year.22

4.2 Demand Inflection and Empirical Evidence
Tracking demand destruction during the 2011–2012 super-cycle presents a fascinating analytical challenge due to profound methodological complexities in domestic energy data reporting. Throughout the first half of 2011, EIA weekly implied gasoline demand data appeared to show catastrophic, unprecedented declines in U.S. consumption, leading many market participants to prematurely declare massive domestic demand destruction.15
However, this initial data was deeply flawed by an archaic statistical model. In August 2011, the EIA publicly acknowledged that its weekly predictive model for petroleum product exports was failing to capture a historic, structural shift in U.S. refining economics.15 U.S. refiners, benefiting from deeply discounted domestic West Texas Intermediate (WTI) crude oil stranded in the midwest, were running at maximum utilization rates and exporting record volumes of finished gasoline and diesel to global markets.11 Because the EIA's weekly "product supplied" metric is a calculated balance that subtracts exports from total domestic supply, the systemic undercounting of these massive export volumes resulted in an artificial, severe deflation of implied domestic demand.15
Once the EIA corrected this methodology in late 2011, the revised data revealed that U.S. gasoline demand was indeed declining, but at a more moderate, structural pace.15 This decline was driven by consistently high retail prices averaging $3.56 per gallon 47 and the accelerating penetration of highly efficient vehicles mandated by improving Corporate Average Fuel Economy (CAFE) standards.48 It took approximately 20 to 24 weeks of sustained $100+ Brent crude for actual, undeniable year-over-year demand destruction to materialize in the United States, far longer than the immediate crash seen in 1990.15
Globally, the International Energy Agency's monthly reports tracked a similar, delayed timeline. In September 2011, the IEA officially began cutting its global oil demand growth forecasts by hundreds of thousands of barrels, citing mounting financial headwinds and high absolute prices.49 By the publication of the 2012 Medium-Term Oil Market Report, the IEA noted that demand destruction in mature OECD economies was "unambiguous".16 The high price environment forced a permanent substitution effect in Europe and North America, though this structural decline was largely masked on an aggregate global scale by relentless, price-inelastic demand growth from China and the Middle East.22



4.3 Early Warning Indicators
The commercial trucking sector provided a highly accurate early warning signal during this prolonged, grinding price cycle. The American Trucking Associations' (ATA) Truck Tonnage Index, which acts as a bellwether for the physical domestic economy, had grown at a robust 5.8% in both 2010 and 2011.18 However, as diesel prices surged synchronously with crude, the sector's growth effectively halted.
By March 2012, the ATA index was up a mere 2.7% year-over-year, the smallest increase since the depths of the financial crisis in December 2009.18 The Ceridian-UCLA Pulse of Commerce Index mirrored this dramatic slowdown, recording a 4.9% decline in the first quarter of 2012 compared to the fourth quarter of 2011.18 Trucking firms, squeezed violently by the lag in fuel surcharge recoveries and high base costs, reduced fleet utilization and deferred capital expenditures.18 This signaled broad macroeconomic deceleration and industrial fuel demand destruction well before retail passenger gasoline data finally capitulated.
4.4 Catalysts for Price Reversal
The easing of the 2011–2012 super-cycle was a complex amalgamation of international policy and revolutionary technological shifts. While the IEA successfully coordinated a 60 million barrel emergency stock release in June 2011 (with 30 million barrels originating from the U.S. SPR), its long-term impact on the structural market deficit was negligible.23 Market analysis indicated that prices had actually begun softening weeks prior to the IEA announcement due to deteriorating global macroeconomic conditions and fears of a European sovereign debt crisis.23
The true, permanent catalyst for the bearish reversal—which ultimately saw prices fall sharply from $128 in March 2012 to $88.69 by June 25, 2012 14—was entirely supply-driven.11 The structural global deficit was overwhelmed by the geometric, unprecedented growth of the U.S. shale oil revolution.46 Utilizing hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling, U.S. crude production surged past 6 million barrels per day in early 2012, logging an unprecedented 780,000 bpd annual increase.11 This massive influx of light tight oil, combined with unambiguous demand destruction in the OECD and heightened Saudi output, finally broke the back of the sustained $100+ price regime.16
5. Case Study 3: The 2022 Russia-Ukraine & Post-COVID Supply Shock
The 2022 oil shock presented a unique historical anomaly: a massive geopolitical supply disruption layered directly on top of a highly distorted, inflation-prone post-pandemic macroeconomic recovery.12 Following the Russian Federation's invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022, sweeping western financial sanctions and voluntary self-embargoing by global commodity trading houses threatened to strand millions of barrels of Russian crude and refined products, throwing an already tight market into chaos.12
5.1 Price Timeline and Trajectory
The physical and paper market reaction was explosive. On February 28, just four days after the invasion commenced, Brent crude decisively crossed the $100 per barrel mark.53 Panic buying, logistical rerouting, and physical supply chain seizures drove the front-month Brent contract to a staggering, historic peak of $133.18 on March 8, 2022—merely two weeks post-invasion.14
The market remained extraordinarily volatile throughout the spring, exhibiting extreme backwardation that severely penalized commercial inventory storage. Prices briefly retreated to $104.61 by March 16 as initial panic subsided, but surged aggressively again into the peak Northern Hemisphere summer driving season, reaching a secondary peak of $129.20 on June 8, 2022.14 Following this summer peak, crude finally succumbed to a protracted downward trend, heavily influenced by aggressive monetary tightening from global central banks, bringing prices back into the $80 range by the fourth quarter of the year.14
5.2 Demand Inflection and Empirical Evidence
In 2022, the transmission of global crude prices to domestic retail gasoline was violently exacerbated by a critical bottleneck in global refining capacity.37 The permanent closure of multiple refineries during the 2020 COVID-19 demand crash meant that the global market was structurally short of finished refined products.37 Consequently, the "crack spread"—the margin refiners make by processing crude into gasoline and diesel—blew out to unprecedented historical highs.52 This resulted in retail gasoline and diesel prices disconnecting from their historical crude ratios; pump prices rose much faster and higher than the $130 crude price strictly implied.37
At what price level did U.S. gasoline demand begin falling year-over-year? The data indicates that profound demand resistance materialized rapidly as retail pump prices breached $4.00 per gallon nationwide, roughly correlating to the $110-$120 per barrel Brent level in this distorted refining environment.54 However, demonstrating a clean, sustained -5% year-over-year decline in EIA weekly data took considerable time.54 Throughout the first half of 2022, U.S. consumers, heavily buoyed by accumulated pandemic-era fiscal savings and exhibiting a desperate, inelastic demand for post-lockdown mobility, showed remarkably low price elasticity.54
It was not until August 2022—approximately 24 weeks after Brent first crossed $100—that the cumulative, grinding weight of broad-based inflation broke consumer resilience.54 EIA weekly data for August 2022 showed implied U.S. gasoline demand averaging 8.84 mb/d, a stark, undeniable decline of roughly 6.7% compared to the 9.48 mb/d averaged in August 2021.54 This 24-week timeline highlights how massive fiscal stimulus can temporarily override fundamental price elasticity, delaying the onset of traditional demand destruction.54
5.3 Early Warning Indicators
The defining early warning indicator of the 2022 shock was the violent behavior of the diesel and middle distillate markets.37 Because the Russian Federation was a primary supplier of finished diesel and vacuum gas oil to Europe, the conflict and subsequent sanctions disproportionately squeezed global diesel supplies.37
The Producer Price Index (PPI) for diesel skyrocketed an astronomical 109% year-over-year by June 2022, severely outpacing the 85% increase in wholesale gasoline.37 This acute distillate shock battered the commercial freight, agriculture, and logistics sectors immediately, signaling severe upstream constraints and operational demand destruction weeks before retail passenger gasoline demand formally contracted.37
5.4 Catalysts for Price Reversal
The reversal of the 2022 price spike from the summer highs down to the $80s in the fourth quarter was driven by a triad of powerful macroeconomic forces. First, organic, price-driven demand destruction became fully realized across the OECD as record retail prices curtailed marginal consumption and industrial output.54 Second, stringent, unyielding COVID-19 lockdown policies in the People's Republic of China drastically suppressed Asian demand, artificially taking an estimated 2 to 3 mbpd of expected consumption off the global ledger, providing massive relief to the tight market.56
Third, and perhaps most critically for the short-term physical balance and market psychology, was unprecedented political intervention. In March 2022, the Biden administration authorized the largest drawdown in the history of the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, releasing 180 million barrels over six months (effectively adding 1 mbpd of steady physical supply to the domestic market).57 A subsequent U.S. Treasury Department analysis estimated that this massive, sustained injection of physical supply lowered retail gasoline prices by 13 to 31 cents per gallon.57 This intervention significantly dampened the absolute price peak, smoothed out physical shortages, and accelerated the bearish reversal in the crude markets alongside the unfolding demand destruction.57
6. Comparative Analysis: The Velocity of Demand Destruction
The historical record across these three distinct eras reveals a profound reality regarding global petroleum markets: the velocity of price spikes is virtually instantaneous, driven by algorithmic trading and panic in the paper derivatives markets, whereas the manifestation of physical demand destruction operates on a delayed, grinding timeline.1
In 1990, the U.S. economy was already teetering on the edge of a recession; consequently, the timeline from the price spike to demand destruction was relatively compressed (roughly 10 to 12 weeks).13 The consumer had zero macroeconomic buffer. Conversely, in 2011 and 2022, despite dramatically higher absolute nominal prices, the timeline to verifiable demand destruction stretched significantly to 20–24 weeks.15 In 2011, this delay was masked by aggressive emerging market growth.22 In 2022, the delay was financed by pandemic-era stimulus and a pent-up demand for mobility, proving that robust consumer balance sheets can temporarily override the standard price elasticity of demand.54

7. Forward-Looking Synthesis: A 2026 Strait of Hormuz Scenario
The historical timelines extracted from 1990, 2011, and 2022 provide a critical predictive framework for assessing future geopolitical supply shocks. Consider a hypothetical scenario in 2026 wherein the Strait of Hormuz—a highly vulnerable maritime chokepoint through which approximately 20 million barrels per day (roughly 20% of global petroleum liquids) transit—is closed due to an escalating military conflict.20 If Brent crude is already sitting at an elevated baseline of $90 per barrel as the crisis begins, the historical data implies a highly asymmetric and dangerous market reaction.
Unlike 1990 or 2011, global refining capacity in 2026 remains structurally tight, and the "easy" demand destruction has already occurred.52 The penetration of electric vehicles (EVs) and advanced hybrid technologies has structurally removed the most price-sensitive, low-income marginal drivers from the gasoline consumption pool.59 Furthermore, corporate logistics and trucking fleets have highly optimized their routing and efficiency.61 Therefore, the remaining baseline petroleum demand is vastly more inelastic.
If a Hormuz closure instantly removes 20 mbpd from the market, algorithmic trading will ensure prices violently spike within days, easily eclipsing the historical absolute peaks of 2008 and 2022. However, because remaining demand is highly inelastic and essential for basic economic function, the historical 20- to 24-week timeline required to force a 5% year-over-year contraction in consumption will likely stretch even longer. Consumers and industrial sectors will be forced to absorb catastrophic costs for months before structural alternatives can be scaled or deployed.
Crucially, in this scenario, the traditional historical catalysts for price reversals are heavily muted. A Hormuz closure would completely trap the spare production capacity of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, and Iraq, neutralizing the very supply response that saved the market in 1990.21 Furthermore, the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, significantly depleted following the historic 180-million-barrel drawdown in 2022, lacks the volumetric firepower to unilaterally balance a 20 mbpd deficit for an extended duration.57 Consequently, absent a swift military resolution, a 2026 Hormuz shock would subject the global economy to an unprecedented period of sustained triple-digit crude prices, forcing a brutal, prolonged cycle of demand destruction that would almost certainly precipitate a deep, systemic global macroeconomic depression.
