Executive Summary
“Can you find about like what scam or thing they done in past like spoofing calls or somthing [sic],” the threat actor typed. The conversation was with one of several AI assistants that the actor was using to get advice on planning and executing a variety of cybercrime activities.
Our visibility into this specific actor's operational environment gives us unique insights into exactly how threat actors are building, testing and executing agentic attacks across multiple campaign types.
Unit 42’s investigation into the actor’s operational environment and activities makes two things clear:
The attacker used basic tooling and relied on AI-generated scripts and reconnaissance reports, suggesting they didn’t have the skills these activities would have required before the advent of readily available AI tools.
Despite some apparent bumbling, the actor had an impact, attacking a number of targets in India, including universities. The actor recovered thousands of records revealing personal information and mapped multiple organizations’ attack surfaces.
The attacker’s conversations with jailbroken AI tools included getting step by step advice on how to set up a fraudulent Voice over IP (VoIP) operation. One assistant “helpfully” generated a document including a “NEXT EXPLOIT OPPORTUNITIES” section, outlining planned followup attacks.
Unit 42 identified and investigated this threat actor's operational environment. There, we determined that they had weaponized an AI assistant across several types of cybercrime, including:
Credential theft and data exfiltration
VoIP telecommunications fraud
Wi-Fi password cracking
Cryptocurrency reconnaissance
Command-and-control (C2) operations
An actor operating under the handle BUGSTER configured the Hermes Agent framework with DeepSeek V4 as its reasoning engine and Cursor integrated development environment (IDE) as a secondary tool, both with safety controls disabled. The actor ran an AI-generated brute-force campaign against an Indian university’s website. The actor also achieved a confirmed intrusion against a second university, where they exfiltrated a database of more than 5,200 records, including personally identifiable information (PII) on students.
The actor also built specialized AI bot profiles to acquire VoIP fraud expertise through conversation and maintained automated pipelines for Wi-Fi password cracking and cryptocurrency whale reconnaissance (i.e., identifying large cryptocurrency holders). Notably, the Wi-Fi cracking capability requires close physical proximity to the target, indicating the actor had local access to the targeted locations.
Multiple, unrelated actors are independently adopting the same
Hermes + DeepSeek
combination. This suggests we may be witnessing an increasingly prevalent toolchain of choice for cybercriminals adopting AI-enabled capabilities. We assess a key driver is low safety friction at low cost: DeepSeek models are inexpensive and easy to access, and readily jailbroken with a simple system-prompt override.

Technical Analysis
We identified evidence that the threat actor leveraged AI systems for a wide range of criminal activity against multiple target networks, primarily in India. The criminal activity included:
Planning for VoIP fraud
Credential theft targeting several organizations
Wi-Fi password cracking
Cryptocurrency reconnaissance
C2 payloads
Credential Theft and Data Exfiltration
WordPress Brute Force
The actor used AI-generated reconnaissance to conduct a methodical attack against an Indian university's online education platform. The actor’s enumeration reports exhibit structured formatting (executive summaries, severity ratings, systematic endpoint categorization) consistent with AI-generated output. The attack progressed through four stages:
Subdomain enumeration
: The actor identified 12 subdomains including the main WordPress site, a learning management system (LMS), student portal and administration dashboard
WordPress REST API enumeration
: The actor extracted 13 active user IDs with real names via post author fields and author sitemaps, bypassing the Cloudflare Web Application Firewall (WAF) that blocked direct user endpoint access
Targeted brute force
: A generator script produced a brute force tool with 117 usernames (derived from the enumeration) and 28 common passwords
Credential brute force
: The actor’s tool flagged one WordPress login as valid

University Database Exfiltration
The actor exfiltrated a MongoDB database from a second Indian public university. The exfiltration tooling wrote a manifest alongside the dumped collections, recording the following data:
Admin credential obtained
: An administrator account with a weak password
Exfiltrated records
: Thousands of student records (diploma, undergraduate, postgraduate) and faculty records containing PII
Infrastructure exposed
: Cloud load balancing, edge routing, database services, and a third-party cloud storage service account
E-Commerce Enumeration
The actor enumerated an Indian site on a popular e-commerce platform, extracting:
A storefront API token
Three admin email addresses
Two phone numbers
Payment merchant IDs
The attack report is a suspected large language model (LLM)-generated document, including a NEXT EXPLOIT OPPORTUNITIES section outlining planned follow-up attacks.
VoIP Telecommunications Fraud
We also identified evidence that the actor was developing VoIP (Voice over IP) fraud infrastructure, using the
alice
Hermes profile as an AI-powered advisor. Evidence included:
Advisory conversations
A detailed technical knowledge base
Carrier research
However, the absence of call logs, Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) traces or active call routing suggested the operation was still in a planning or early setup phase.
With DeepSeek V4 as the underlying model,
alice
provided detailed, actionable criminal methodology through conversations and a comprehensive fraud knowledge base ingested into the bot. The actor developed a planned approach following an established VoIP fraud model:
Establish a legitimate front
: Register a US limited liability company (LLC) with an employer identification number (EIN) to qualify as a telecommunications provider. The knowledge base includes detailed guidance on:
Forming LLCs in Wyoming or Delaware as a non-US resident
Obtaining an EIN without a Social Security number via the US Internal Revenue Service (IRS) fax
Completing Federal Communications Commission (FCC) registration
Obtain carrier access
: Contract with legitimate VoIP carriers to purchase call routing capacity (SIP trunks).
Traffic mixing
: Route approximately 70% legitimate call traffic alongside 30% fraudulent calls to stay below carrier compliance audit thresholds.
Bypass caller ID authentication
: Circumvent
STIR/SHAKEN
, the FCC-mandated caller identity verification framework. In the advisory conversations,
alice
covers bypass techniques and Calling Line Identification (CLI) spoofing.
Monetize
: Generate revenue with the 30% fraudulent traffic through toll fraud and other non-compliant routes. Least-cost routing (LCR) optimizes per-minute costs across carrier tiers.

Other Operations
Command and Control
The threat actor’s infrastructure maintained a listening port for a Python-based C2 listener supporting Windows reverse shell connections. Supporting payloads include:
A Base64-encoded PowerShell reverse shell delivered via VBScript
A QubesOS VM setup script with SSH and
ncat
Multiple Windows shell variants
Wi-Fi Password Cracking
The actor developed an automated Wi-Fi password cracking process. A Linux (Kali) device the actor labeled
Pi
placed within range of target Wi-Fi networks captured Wi-Fi Protected Access (WPA) authentication material (PMKID or EAPOL handshakes) from nearby access points.
The server collected these hashes over an SSH tunnel and dispatched them to
Vast.ai
cloud GPU instances for cracking, with results automatically reported back to the actor via Discord webhooks. This technique requires physical proximity to the target networks, indicating the actor had local access to the targeted locations.
Credential Scanning Toolkit
We also identified a large Go-based credential-scanning toolkit. This toolkit scrapes webpages and extracts exposed credentials, including:
.env
values
S3 signed URLs
Google Cloud service account keys
Content delivery network (CDN) API keys
Private keys in log files
A companion Python script scrapes domain lists for scanning.
Cryptocurrency Reconnaissance
The actor scraped the top 500 ETH holders and major token holders via the
DeBank
API, producing intelligence files that documented 45 cryptocurrency whales with net worth figures. We assess this was likely for future targeting, but we were unable to identify any follow-on activity during this research.
Tool Design and Infrastructure
The threat actor used a single US-based server (
162.243.3[.]92
) to host multiple core services, including:
The Hermes Agent framework
A C2 listener
A Cursor integrated development environment (IDE) proxy
Several supporting components
Our analysis of the actor’s logs and test scripts indicates that they experimented with various LLMs, including
GLM 5.2
by Zhipu AI and other models accessed through
OpenRouter
. Ultimately, they chose DeepSeek V4 as the backend for operations.
The actor's test scripts show repeated attempts to defeat model safety controls with a prompt-level override. Although GLM 5.2 is an
open-weight model
that has been reported as weakly guarded, the actor built a dedicated script to send the override to GLM 5.2 and flag refusals. This is evidence of resistance the actor worked to overcome.
The actor ultimately operationalized DeepSeek V4, consistent with the minimal safety controls observed in the
Chinese-speaking activity we previously reported
. Figure 5 shows an example of the system prompt the actor used to override model safety controls (from the actor's model-testing scripts, e.g.,

).

Conclusion
This case illustrates how AI lowers the skill barrier for cybercrime. The actor used basic tooling and relied on AI-generated scripts and reconnaissance reports, but still achieved notable impact. They recovered more than 5,200 PII records and mapped multiple organizations' attack surfaces. AI enabled a single actor to operate across diverse attack types (credential theft, VoIP fraud, Wi-Fi cracking, cryptocurrency targeting) that would traditionally require distinct skill sets.
The VoIP fraud planning demonstrates a particularly notable use of AI as a domain expert. With DeepSeek V4 as the underlying model, the actor's bot profiles provided detailed, actionable criminal guidance.
This guidance included specific fraud-to-clean traffic ratios, carrier audit thresholds to stay below and operational security measures to avoid detection. The model's minimal safety controls allowed it to function as an on-demand criminal advisor, enabling the actor to acquire operational expertise through conversation rather than experience.
Combining these findings with our previous reporting on a
Chinese-Speaking threat actor leveraging the Hermes + DeepSeek
toolchain points to growing uptake of this combination among cybercriminals.
