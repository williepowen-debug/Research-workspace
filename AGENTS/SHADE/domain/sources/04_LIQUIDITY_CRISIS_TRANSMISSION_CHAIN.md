Systemic Fragility in the Convergence of Private Equity and Life Insurance: Mapping the Transmission of Liquidity Shocks and the Hierarchy of Loss
The structural transformation of the life insurance industry over the past two decades has been defined by the aggressive entry of private equity firms into the sector. This convergence has fundamentally altered the liability profile and asset composition of insurers, shifting away from traditional, highly liquid government and investment-grade corporate bonds toward complex, illiquid private credit and structured securities.1 While this model seeks to exploit the illiquidity premium to satisfy long-term obligations in a volatile interest rate environment, it introduces novel systemic risks. If a private equity-owned life insurer were to face a liquidity crisis—characterized by a rapid withdrawal of institutional deposits and policy surrenders—the resulting transmission chain would penetrate deep into the global financial system, affecting stakeholders from retail policyholders to the broader private credit markets.4
The Anatomy of the Modern PE-Insurance Model
The contemporary private equity (PE) influence in life insurance is not merely a change in ownership but a radical shift in the intermediation chain. PE firms typically acquire or establish insurers to serve as a captive source of long-term capital, which is then funneled into private credit assets originated or managed by the PE parent's own asset management arms.2 This creates a closed-loop system where the insurer provides the funding, and the asset manager provides the investment products, often utilizing complex structures such as Collateralized Loan Obligations (CLOs), Business Development Companies (BDCs), and Joint Venture Loan Funds (JVLFs).3
This model relies on the assumption that life insurance liabilities are "sticky" and long-dated, providing a stable base for illiquid investments. However, the rise of institutional products such as Guaranteed Investment Contracts (GICs) and Funding Agreement-Backed Securities (FABS) has introduced "runnable" wholesale funding into the insurance balance sheet.3 Unlike traditional death benefits, these products are often held by sophisticated institutional investors who monitor credit ratings and financial stability with the same rigor as bank depositors.
The systemic risk is further obscured by regulatory arbitrage. Many PE-owned insurers utilize offshore reinsurance captives, particularly in jurisdictions like Bermuda, to move liabilities off the primary balance sheet, taking advantage of less stringent capital and disclosure requirements.3 This creates a "liquidity illusion" where the insurer appears well-capitalized on a statutory basis while holding significant exposure to Level 3 assets—those with unobservable inputs and no active secondary market.12
The Transmission Chain of a Liquidity Crisis
A liquidity crisis in a PE-owned insurer would likely follow a multi-stage progression, beginning with a trigger event that undermines confidence and culminating in systemic contagion. The initial catalyst for a run is rarely a single default but rather a loss of confidence in the insurer’s valuation or solvency. This could stem from a major credit rating downgrade, a public warning from regulators regarding "creative" marks in the private credit portfolio, or an investigation into affiliate transactions and management fees.7
Once a trigger occurs, the first entities to move are institutional depositors. Holders of GICs and funding agreements often have downgrade surrender or event of default provisions that allow them to withdraw funds at book value if the insurer's credit rating falls below a certain threshold.9 Sophisticated investors like Vanguard or large pension funds, acting as fiduciaries, are compelled to exit to preserve capital. This mirrors a bank run, where the largest and most informed creditors flee first to avoid being trapped in a subsequent moratorium or liquidation.9
As institutional cash leaves the door, the insurer must raise liquidity. Because the core of the PE-insurance strategy involves holding illiquid private credit, the insurer is forced to sell its most liquid, high-quality assets first—typically public corporate bonds and government securities.18 This process, observed in the 1991 failure of Executive Life, leaves the remaining policyholders and creditors with an increasingly concentrated and illiquid portfolio of "junk" or distressed assets.18
If the liquid assets are exhausted, the insurer must attempt to sell its private credit holdings. This is where the transmission chain hits the private credit feedback loop. Since there is no robust secondary market for these bespoke loans, any forced sale occurs at a deep discount, known as a fire sale.6 Under ASC 820 fair value accounting, these fire-sale prices can serve as observable inputs that force other market participants to mark down their own identical or similar holdings.13
The contagion spreads to the broader private credit market through several channels. Other PE-owned insurers or BDCs holding the same credits must recognize losses, potentially triggering their own liquidity pressures.13 Banks providing credit facilities to the insurer or its affiliated funds may trigger Material Adverse Change (MAC) clauses, freezing further draws and accelerating repayments.23 Finally, stress at the insurer level may trigger cross-default provisions in the PE parent’s other investment vehicles, creating a "hell and damnation" scenario for the GP's reputation and capital-raising ability.6
Hierarchy of Loss: Who Loses Money and in What Order?
The distribution of losses in an insurance insolvency is governed by state law, which generally prioritizes individual policyholders over institutional and general creditors. However, the unique structure of PE-owned insurers creates a more complex priority stack than traditional firms.
The PE Parent and Fund Investors
In a typical PE structure, the equity of the insurer is the first to be wiped out. This means the limited partners (LPs) in the PE parent's insurance-focused funds lose their entire principal investment.27 Furthermore, the General Partner (GP) may face clawback or giveback requirements. If the GP has received carried interest or management fees from the insurer while it was technically insolvent or through questionable accounting, a state receiver or the SEC may seek to recover these funds for the benefit of policyholders.7
General Unsecured Creditors and Subordinated Debt
Below the equity holders are the providers of subordinated debt and general unsecured creditors. This includes vendors and potentially banks that have provided unsecured credit lines to the insurer's holding company.10 These stakeholders rarely recover significant value in a liquidation.
Derivative Counterparties
A critical and often overlooked group is the derivative counterparties. Most life insurers use derivatives—interest rate swaps, caps, and duration hedges—to manage the mismatch between long-term liabilities and shorter-term assets.4 Under federal law and many modern state insurance receivership laws, derivative counterparties enjoy super-priority via close-out netting and collateral liquidation rights.17 If an insurer enters rehabilitation or liquidation, these counterparties can immediately terminate their contracts, net out what is owed, and seize any collateral—cash or high-quality bonds—held in margin accounts.17 While some regimes impose a 24-to-48-hour stay to allow for a transfer of the contracts, the inability of a receiver to provide immediate collateral can lead to a massive drain of the insurer’s most liquid assets at the very start of the crisis.33
Institutional Depositors: GIC and Funding Agreement Holders
The status of institutional products like GICs is a major point of legal contention. In most states, GICs and funding agreements are classified as policyholder claims or group annuities, giving them the same priority as individual life insurance policies.9 However, some state statutes or court rulings have historically attempted to subordinate Muni-GICs or non-traditional products to individual death benefits.21 If they are treated as pari passu with policyholders, institutional investors like Vanguard or pension funds will share in the distribution of the estate's remaining assets. If the assets are insufficient, these institutional holders will face significant haircuts on their principal.38 Importantly, many institutional GICs are excluded from state guaranty association coverage because they are unallocated contracts or exceed residency-based limits.9
Individual Policyholders and State Guaranty Associations
Individual policyholders are the most protected but still face tail risk. When an insurer is liquidated, state guaranty associations (GAs) are triggered. These associations assess other healthy insurers in the state to raise funds to pay claims up to statutory limits—typically $300,000 for death benefits and $250,000 for annuity values.38 Policyholders with values above these limits—common in high-net-worth universal life or large structured settlements—become general creditors for the excess amount. In the PHL Variable liquidation, over-the-cap policyholders are projected to lose over $120 million in benefits despite continuing to pay premiums during the rehabilitation phase.40
Banks and Credit Providers
Banks providing secured credit facilities to the insurer or its subsidiaries are generally protected by their collateral. However, if that collateral consists of private credit loans that have been marked down, the banks may face collateral shortfalls. In the event of a covenant violation, banks are less likely to provide waivers if the insurer’s health is deteriorating, leading to a liquidity freeze that accelerates the insurer's path to insolvency.23
Historical Precedents: Lessons from Previous Crashes
The current PE-insurance risks are not without precedent. Analyzing past failures reveals how runs on insurance companies manifest and the limitations of regulatory intervention.
Executive Life Insurance Company (1991)
Executive Life (ELIC) serves as the primary archetype for a junk bond fueled insurance collapse. Like modern PE insurers, ELIC's CEO Fred Carr used a high-yield investment strategy to offer industry-leading crediting rates on annuities and GICs.18 Following the collapse of the junk bond market and the indictment of its primary broker, Drexel Burnham Lambert, ELIC experienced a massive run. In early 1990 alone, the firm was forced to liquidate nearly $4 billion in assets to meet surrender requests.18 California regulators were aware of insolvency as early as 1983 but allowed the company to use surplus relief reinsurance and other accounting gimmicks to appear solvent.18 When the state finally seized ELIC in 1991, its junk bond portfolio was sold to a French consortium at a fraction of its face value.21 Annuitants initially saw their benefits cut to 70% of promised levels.18
Confederation Life (1994)
Confederation Life was one of Canada’s largest insurers, and its failure highlighted the risks of cross-border complexity and illiquid real estate investments. A failed attempt to raise capital from other Canadian insurers triggered a loss of confidence. Unlike ELIC, which was a slow burn, Confederation Life's collapse was a rapid liquidity freeze as US and UK regulators moved to ring-fence assets for their local policyholders.48 The failure left 250,000 policyholders in a state of uncertainty, leading to the creation of more robust cross-border cooperation frameworks.48
PHL Variable (2024-2026)
The ongoing liquidation of PHL Variable Insurance Company represents the modern version of this crisis, driven by a mismatch in Universal Life (UL) policies.40 Regulators found a $900 million deficit in 2024, which ballooned to $2.2 billion by early 2026.40 Recognizing that a standard liquidation would leave policyholders with massive losses, regulators proposed an enhanced liquidation that combines state guaranty fund support with a sale of profitable blocks to a third-party buyer to provide benefits above the GA caps.40 To prevent a run during the rehabilitation phase, a court-ordered moratorium was placed on surrenders and withdrawals, trapping policyholders while the company’s condition continued to deteriorate.40
The Private Credit Contagion and the Role of Creative Marks
The most significant transmission risk in a modern crisis is the impact on the $1.7 trillion private credit market.13 Because PE-owned insurers hold a disproportionate share of these assets, their distress can force a realization of losses that have been hidden by management judgment. Private credit valuations are traditionally based on Level 3 unobservable inputs, allowing managers to maintain stable Net Asset Values (NAVs) even when the economy slows.13 This practice is often referred to by critics as extend and pretend—lenders relax terms or allow Payment-in-Kind (PIK) interest to avoid declaring a default.8
In a liquidity crisis, this valuation bridge collapses. If a distressed insurer is forced to sell a unitranche loan to raise cash, the transaction price becomes a hard mark. Other insurers or funds holding the same loan must mark it down to the fire-sale price.13 Even if they don't hold the same loan, auditors and LPs may use that price to triangulate the value of similar loans in the same industry.14 As marks fall, retail-oriented private credit funds may face a surge in redemption requests, leading to gating and further panic.1
The interconnectedness of PE firms, insurers, and banks creates a multi-layered leverage problem. An insurer might own the equity tranche of a CLO managed by its parent, while the parent’s credit fund holds the senior debt of the same company, and a bank provides a warehouse credit facility to the whole structure.3 This interconnectedness means that a liquidity shock at the insurance level is not ring-fenced. It flows through to the leverage providers and the capital providers. With a $215 billion refinancing wall approaching, the inability of distressed PE-insurers to participate in new lending or refinancings could lead to a broader contraction in middle-market credit.6
The Role of Safety Nets: Guaranty Associations and Their Limits
While the state guaranty system is designed to prevent retail catastrophic loss, it is not built to handle the systemic failure of a massive PE-owned insurer with significant institutional liabilities. Guaranty associations do not maintain pre-funded reserves. Instead, they operate on an ex post assessment basis, charging healthy insurers in the state a percentage of their premiums to cover the failure.38 If a large insurer fails, the assessments required could be so massive that they threaten the profitability of the remaining healthy insurers. In such cases, state law allows GA boards to request haircuts from the court—meaning they are legally permitted to pay out less than the statutory guarantee if paying the full amount would jeopardize the rest of the industry.38
Many of the products that characterize the PE-insurance model are explicitly excluded from GA coverage. These include unallocated GICs that fund pension plans but are not allocated to individual participants.9 Coverage is also generally tied to the residency of the policyholder. Institutional contracts with non-US addresses or complex corporate shells may fall through the cracks.42 Finally, most GAs only cover a minimum guaranteed interest rate. Any excess returns promised through complex PE investment strategies are not protected.55
Actionable Insights for Risk Management and Oversight
The transmission of a liquidity crisis in a PE-owned insurer is a function of complexity, illiquidity, and interconnectedness. To mitigate these risks, market participants and regulators must look beyond traditional statutory solvency metrics. Regulators must increase scrutiny of Investment Management Agreements (IMAs) and management fees paid to PE parents. If these fees act as unauthorized dividends, they drain the capital buffer intended to protect policyholders during a stress event.7
Wholesale funding in insurance should be treated with the same macroprudential caution as uninsured bank deposits. The industry’s reliance on these products is near 2007 levels, and their potential for rapid exit during a rating event is a primary driver of fire-sale risk.3 The discrepancy between Level 3 private marks and public market reality is the hidden fuse in the transmission chain. Encouraging the use of independent third-party valuation agents and implementing more frequent stress testing of private credit portfolios is essential.12
Finally, while close-out netting is intended to reduce systemic risk for banks, in the context of an insurance insolvency, it creates an immediate liquidity drain that can make rehabilitation impossible. Policy discussions around extending stays or harmonizing state and federal rules are critical to ensuring an orderly resolution rather than a chaotic collapse.35 The transformation of life insurers into private-credit-heavy entities has fundamentally linked the insurance sector to the shadow banking system. In a liquidity crisis, the transmission chain moves rapidly from institutional withdrawals to fire sales, mark-to-market contagion in private credit, and a cascading hierarchy of loss.
The historical precedents of Executive Life and Confederation Life, coupled with the modern difficulties faced by PHL Variable, demonstrate that when sticky liabilities become runnable and assets become illiquid, the regulatory safety net is stretched to its breaking point. As the private credit market continues to grow and the refinancing wall approaches, the resilience of this PE-insurance nexus will be tested. The stability of the broader financial system may ultimately depend on the accuracy of today's private valuations and the willingness of regulators to intervene before a liquidity wobble turns into a systemic fire sale.
Deep-Dive: State Insolvency Regimes and Institutional Vulnerabilities
The failure of an insurance company is uniquely governed by state law, a deviation from the federal bankruptcy process that applies to almost every other sector of the American economy. This state-centric model, solidified by the McCarran-Ferguson Act, ensures that insurance commissioners maintain primary jurisdiction over rehabilitation and liquidation.10 For PE-owned firms, this creates a fragmented landscape where a single parent holding company may oversee subsidiaries across dozens of jurisdictions, each with slightly different priority rules.
The Conflict of Priorities: State vs. Federal
A significant tension exists between state insurance priority statutes and the Federal Priority Act (FPA). Under the FPA, claims of the United States government—such as unpaid taxes or federal loan guarantees—must be paid before any other creditor.57 This creates a high-stakes conflict when state laws prioritize policyholder benefits. The Supreme Court addressed this in U.S. Dept. of Treasury v. Fabe, holding that state statutes take precedence over the FPA only to the extent they protect policyholders. General creditors and other institutional claimants, however, remain subordinate to federal interests.57 For institutional depositors like pension funds holding GICs, this means that any federal claim against the insurer could further erode their potential recovery from the estate.
The Mechanism of Institutional Withdrawal: The Contractual Run
Institutional products such as GICs and Funding Agreements are often documented with provisions that explicitly anticipate financial distress. Unlike a retail life insurance policy, which might have a fixed surrender charge, an institutional contract may include rating-based triggers. If the insurer's credit rating falls below investment grade (e.g., Baa3/BBB-), the contract holder often gains the right to demand immediate repayment at book value.9
This creates a first-mover advantage. In the lead-up to the 1999 run on General American Life, sophisticated contract holders monitored the firm's capital position and exited at the first sign of trouble.20 Because the insurer must pay these sophisticated actors 100 cents on the dollar, the remaining pool of assets for retail policyholders is depleted. This effectively turns the "policyholder priority" class into a race where those who exit first preserve their capital, and those who remain bear the burden of the illiquid assets.9
The Role of Investment Management Agreements (IMAs) in Liquidity Drain
The NAIC has expressed growing concern over the IMAs between PE-owned insurers and their affiliated managers. These agreements often grant the manager significant discretion over the insurer’s investment strategy, sometimes with provisions that make the IMA difficult or expensive to terminate even if performance suffers.7 In a liquidity crisis, an affiliated manager may be incentivized to hold onto the insurer’s illiquid assets (from which they earn fees) while selling off the insurer’s more liquid, non-affiliated holdings. This "capture" of the insurance general account serves the PE parent’s interest in maintaining its assets under management (AUM) but directly conflicts with the insurer's need for liquidity.
Forced Sales and the "Broken" CLO Mechanism
A major component of the PE-insurance asset base is the investment in CLO tranches. Insurers typically hold the "mezzanine" or "equity" tranches, which offer high yields but are the first to absorb losses if the underlying corporate loans default.3 In a forced sale scenario, these tranches are notoriously difficult to price. If an insurer is forced to dump its CLO holdings to meet GIC redemptions, it doesn't just lose capital; it destroys the market price for those instruments.
Because many CLOs have over-collateralization (OC) and interest coverage (IC) triggers, a significant markdown in the value of the underlying loans (caused by the insurer's fire sale) can lead to a diversion of cash flows. In recent stress tests, some private credit CLOs have breached these tests, forcing cash flows to be diverted to senior tranches and leaving the insurance-held equity tranches with zero income.15 This creates a secondary liquidity shock: the insurer loses the very investment income it needs to fund its ongoing operations.
The Broader Private Credit Contagion: Mechanisms and Ripples
The private credit market is not a monolith, but its interconnectedness with insurance creates several distinct pathways for contagion.
The Psychological Contagion: Shaken Confidence
When a large financial institution fails, the impact is often as much psychological as it is financial. In the private credit space, where transparency is minimal, any crack in the armor of a major manager can lead to a broad risk-aversion. Lenders who previously competed aggressively for deals may suddenly scramble to verify their own collateral. This leaching of confidence into public markets can lead to a widening of spreads in high-yield bonds, even if the underlying fundamentals of the public companies remain sound.16
The $215 Billion Refinancing Wall
The timing of a potential liquidity crisis is critical. The middle-market corporate sector is facing a massive "refinancing wall" in 2026-2028. Many companies that took out private loans during the low-rate environment of 2020-2021 will need to refinance at much higher rates.6 If the insurance companies—who are now the marginal providers of corporate credit—are embroiled in their own liquidity crises, they will be unable to provide this refinancing capital. This could lead to a wave of corporate defaults, which in turn further devalues the insurers' remaining loan portfolios, creating a classic feedback loop of financial distress.
Regulatory and Supervisory Responses
Supervisors have begun to respond to these risks by tightening disclosure and risk-based capital (RBC) requirements for private and offshore assets.12 The NAIC’s "13 Private Equity Considerations" list highlights areas such as ownership control, short-term focus, and the unwillingness of owners to support troubled insurers.7 However, the speed of financial innovation often outpaces regulatory cycles. The shift to "asset-intensive" reinsurance and "funded re" structures in the UK and US demonstrates that PE firms are constantly finding new ways to optimize capital, often by moving it further away from the direct reach of traditional regulators.52
Conclusion: Synthesizing the Threat
The mapping of a liquidity crisis in a PE-owned life insurer reveals a system that has traded liquidity and transparency for yield and capital efficiency. In a stable environment, the model functions effectively, providing long-term capital to the economy and attractive returns to investors. However, the transmission chain of a run—from institutional withdrawals to derivative collateral drains and the marking down of private credit assets—exposes a fragile architecture.
The order of loss clearly places the highest risk on the PE parent and its fund investors, followed by institutional depositors who lack the protection of state guaranty funds. Individual policyholders remain the most shielded, yet even they are not immune to the consequences of a systemic shortfall, as seen in the PHL Variable and Executive Life precedents. As we approach a period of increased corporate refinancing needs and continued interest rate volatility, the "stickiness" of insurance liabilities will be put to its ultimate test. The stability of the broader global financial system may very well depend on whether the insurance industry can manage its transition from a holder of liquid bonds to a major player in the opaque world of private credit without triggering a cascade of fire sales that its current safety nets were never designed to catch.
