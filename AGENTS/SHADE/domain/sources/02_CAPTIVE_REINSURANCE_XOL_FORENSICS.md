Structural Deficiencies in Life Insurance Solvency: A Forensic Analysis of Captive Reinsurance, XOL Accounting, and the Regulatory Response
The contemporary landscape of the United States life insurance and annuity industry is increasingly defined by a divergence between traditional statutory accounting frameworks and the complex, often opaque world of captive reinsurance. Forensic investigations into the financial integrity of major carriers have revealed a systemic reliance on accounting mechanisms that artificially inflate surplus while masking significant liability exposure. Central to this phenomenon is the work of forensic accountant Tom Gober, whose examinations of captive financials have uncovered staggering imbalances between reported liabilities and tangible backing assets. This report analyzes the mechanics of Excess of Loss (XOL) assets, the role of state-sanctioned permitted practices in jurisdictions such as Vermont, South Carolina, Delaware, Arizona, and Iowa, and the evolving regulatory response of the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC). Furthermore, it provides a forensic autopsy of the collapse of PHL Variable Insurance Company, a case study in the consequences of aggressive reserve financing and the eventual exposure of multi-billion-dollar deficiencies.
The Forensic Investigations of Tom Gober and the Seven Billion Dollar Imbalance
The forensic work of Tom Gober, a Certified Fraud Examiner with over four decades of experience specializing in complex reinsurance transactions, has brought to light a critical vulnerability in how large life insurers manage their balance sheets. Gober’s investigations, often presented to regulatory bodies such as the Department of Labor’s Employee Benefits Security Administration (EBSA), highlight a growing trend where insurance companies leverage their balance sheets in ways that escape the full scrutiny of ratings agencies and state regulators.1 A primary focus of Gober’s work involves the identification of circular related-party transactions within major insurance holding company systems. In one significant forensic review of public filings as of year-end 2023, Gober identified a dangerous level of interdependence among myriad affiliates that reinsure each other, invest in each other, and pay dividends to each other.3
A specific and alarming finding in his examination of a captive reinsurer’s financials revealed approximately $7 billion in liabilities backed by a mere $200 million in real, tangible assets.1 The remaining gap in the balance sheet was filled by what are classified as XOL assets—essentially accounting entries based on Excess of Loss reinsurance agreements with affiliated entities.1 These findings suggest that the traditional metrics of solvency may be deeply misleading when applied to companies utilizing these aggressive reserve financing strategies.
To quantify these risks, Gober developed the Transparency, Surplus, and Risky-assets Ratio (TSR Ratio). This metric is designed to show the relationship between a company’s higher-risk, off-balance sheet assets and its reported statutory surplus.5 The TSR Ratio serves as an early-warning benchmark: while a company with a low TSR could survive a significant write-down of its risky assets, a company with an elevated ratio would see its entire surplus wiped out by a minor impairment of those same assets. The Accounting Mechanics of Excess of Loss Assets
In the specialized world of insurance accounting, the term "XOL assets" refers to the practice of recording an Excess of Loss reinsurance agreement as an admitted asset on a balance sheet, rather than treating it merely as a contract for future indemnification. In standard insurance accounting, reinsurance is typically reflected on the liability side as a reserve credit, which reduces the total liabilities a company must report.4 However, under certain state-permitted practices, these agreements are transformed into assets. The use of XOL assets is a form of accounting arbitrage designed to bridge the gap between economic reserves and the more conservative statutory reserves required by the NAIC’s Statutory Accounting Principles (SAP).
Under SAP, insurers are often required to hold reserves that exceed their best estimates of future claims, most notably for term life policies (Regulation XXX) and universal life with secondary guarantees (Guideline AXXX).8 To avoid the capital strain of holding these redundant reserves, a ceding insurer establishes a special purpose captive affiliate. This captive then executes an XOL agreement with another reinsurer, often a third party or an offshore affiliate. State regulators in specific domiciles permit the captive to claim the notional value or the security provided by these XOL agreements as an admitted asset on the captive's books.4 Once the captive appears asset-rich due to these XOL entries, the parent insurer cedes its risks to the captive and takes a reserve credit, effectively lowering its own liabilities and increasing its reported surplus.4
The forensic concern regarding XOL assets stems from their lack of liquidity and their contingent nature. Unlike real assets such as cash or investment-grade bonds, an XOL asset is only as valuable as the counterparty’s ability to perform under the contract in a tail-risk scenario.6 Furthermore, these arrangements often involve indemnification of the XOL counterparties by the parent insurer, creating a circular risk profile where the entity supposedly offloading the risk is ultimately responsible for the security backing that risk.4 This interdependence means that in a systemic crisis, the assets intended to protect policyholders may prove to be illusory.
The Landscape of Permitted Practices and State Regulatory Divergence
While the NAIC provides the Accounting Practices and Procedures Manual as the national standard for Statutory Accounting Principles, individual state laws grant insurance commissioners the authority to allow deviations. These deviations are categorized as either prescribed practices, which are state-wide laws or rules, or permitted practices, which are specific approvals granted to individual companies.12 The use of these practices has created a fragmented regulatory environment where the reported financial health of an insurer depends heavily on its choice of domicile.
Vermont: The Captive Epicenter
Vermont has long established itself as the leading domestic domicile for captive insurance, largely by offering a more flexible regulatory environment than traditional states. Vermont statute, specifically 8 V.S.A. § 15 and § 3634a, empowers the Commissioner to adopt rules that may depart from NAIC SAP.14 In the realm of life insurance, Vermont has frequently permitted captives to follow U.S. GAAP or other specific bases of accounting that result in higher reported capital and surplus than would be allowed under SAP.13 For example, the Vermont captive Fairwind was granted permission to follow GAAP for certain recognitions, such as deferred tax assets, resulting in a surplus nearly $300 million higher than under NAIC standards.13 More critically, Vermont has specifically permitted captives like River Lake IX and X to carry XOL reinsurance agreements as admitted assets, a practice that is essential to their licensing and operational viability.11
Iowa and the Brookfield Transactions
Iowa has also emerged as a key participant in allowing aggressive reserve financing maneuvers. Forensic reports on Iowa-domiciled insurers, such as American Equity Investment Life Insurance Company (AEILIC) and its affiliates under Brookfield Wealth Solutions, reveal billions of dollars in XOL arrangements.4 AEILIC reportedly took $3.6 billion in reserve credits for risk ceded to its Vermont captives, which in turn recorded XOL assets of $1.6 billion and $934 million respectively.4 Without these permitted practices, these captives would have had deeply negative surpluses, but with the XOL agreements recorded as assets, they appeared solvent.4 The result was AEILIC reporting a surplus of $3.7 billion, of which 97.2% was directly attributable to the reserve credit derived from its captives.4
Delaware and Regulation 1004
Delaware maintains a rigorous standard through its Insurance Department, including Regulation 1004, which establishes uniform standards for reserve financing of XXX and AXXX policies.16 Delaware typically requires that assuming insurers not licensed as captives or special purpose vehicles maintain at least 500% of the Authorized Control Level RBC.16 However, Delaware also allows for regulatory discretion. Financial statements from major carriers like MetLife and Brighthouse Financial acknowledge that their statutory capital and surplus are significantly impacted by permitted practices in their domiciliary states, which can differ from the NAIC Manual.17 The purpose and intent of Regulation 1004 are to ensure that reinsurance ceded for reserve financing purposes is backed by appropriate primary security, yet the availability of regulatory discretion remains a critical variable in the solvency equation.16
South Carolina and the Oversight of Risk Retention Groups
South Carolina has been identified as a state that monitors the impact of captives through narrow-scope examinations and has been part of the broader discussion on the risks of shadow insurance.20 The state has focused on the regulatory treatment of risk retention groups (RRGs) that are organized as captives, examining opportunities to improve uniformity and oversight in this area.8 While some state regulators in South Carolina defend the use of captives as economically sensible when transparent, critics argue that the lack of public access to captive financials remains a barrier to effective oversight.7 The Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC) identified variable annuity and long-term care captive transactions as areas of particular concern, and South Carolina regulators have contributed to these national dialogues.8
Arizona and the XXX/AXXX Reserve Environment
Arizona is frequently utilized as a domicile for captives reinsuring XXX and AXXX business. Companies such as American Equity have explicitly stated in SEC filings that their capital positions depend on the continuation of these domiciliary permitted practices.5 Arizona captive regulation is deemed to satisfy national standards if the applicable reinsurance transaction satisfies the NAIC XXX/AXXX Reinsurance Framework.23 However, the state has also been noted for allowing the use of affiliated reinsurers in jurisdictions where financial information is statutorily made confidential, letting them fund liabilities with far fewer assets than the ceding carrier itself would need in its own state.5

National Association of Insurance Commissioners Evolution and Regulatory Stance
The NAIC has spent the last decade attempting to harmonize the regulation of captive reinsurers to prevent a competitive decline in state standards. The primary concern has been the use of captives to finance redundant reserves under Regulation XXX and Guideline AXXX.8 In 2014, the NAIC adopted Actuarial Guideline XLVIII (AG 48), which established the first national standards for the types of assets that could be used to back these reserves. AG 48 required a split in security: primary security, consisting of cash and SVO-listed securities, must cover the economic portion of the reserve, while other security, such as letters of credit or XOL agreements, could be used to back the remaining statutory portion.8
This framework was later codified into the Term and Universal Life Insurance Reserve Financing Model Regulation (#787), which became an accreditation standard for states in the early 2020s.8 As of August 2025, 42 jurisdictions have implemented Model #787, forcing more transparency and uniformity in how reserve financing is structured.8 Furthermore, the move to Principle-Based Reserving (PBR), which became mandatory for accredited states on January 1, 2020, has significantly reduced the incentives for new captive transactions by aligning statutory reserves more closely with economic reality.8
The transition to PBR marks a paradigm shift from formulaic, one-size-fits-all reserves to a regime that permits substantially more flexibility based on the actual risks of a company's portfolio. The PBR Implementation Task Force has coordinated technical groups to ensure that the Actuarial Method used for reserve calculation reflects conservative assumptions about mortality and investment income.15 The ultimate goal is to ensure consistent reporting and assist state insurance departments in regulating for financial solvency, ensuring policyholders' obligations are met when due.12
Recent Transparency Initiatives and the Implementation of Actuarial Guideline 55
Despite the progress of Model #787, the NAIC remains concerned about asset-intensive reinsurance transactions, particularly those involving variable annuities and long-term care, which were not fully covered by the initial XXX/AXXX framework.8 Recent efforts have focused on increasing the visibility of offshore reinsurance, where U.S. insurers cede billions in reserves to offshore captives that are then managed by private equity firms.6 This practice has raised concerns about counterparty risk and the potential for contagion in times of stress.6
In August 2025, the NAIC Executive Committee and Plenary adopted Actuarial Guideline LV (AG 55), also known as Actuarial Guideline 55.28 This new framework is designed to bring asset-intensive reinsurance—specifically coinsurance and modified coinsurance (ModCo) arrangements—fully within the scope of asset adequacy oversight.28 AG 55 requires U.S. ceding companies to demonstrate that the liabilities transferred offshore remain fully backed by assets capable of withstanding moderately adverse conditions.27
Scope and Materiality Thresholds of AG 55
AG 55 represents a fundamental shift in regulatory philosophy, moving from simple disclosure to a requirement for rigorous actuarial proof of solvency for ceded blocks. Effective for asset adequacy analysis reported in December 31, 2025 annual statements, AG 55 addresses a critical gap where certain reinsurance structures could materially reduce reported reserves without a clear demonstration that the backing assets would perform under stress.28 The guideline applies to life insurers with asset-intensive reinsurance treaties ceded to counterparties that are not required to submit a VM-30 memorandum to U.S. state regulators.28 The materiality thresholds are specific and tiered:
Transactions with reserve credit in excess of $5 billion.28
Combined reserve credit in excess of $1 billion that also represents more than 5% of the ceding company’s life and annuity reserves.29
Transactions where the reserve credit exceeds $500 million and 10% of total reserves.29
Transactions over $100 million that represent 20% of total reserves.29
Key Requirements and Attribution Analysis
Insurers in scope must conduct Asset Adequacy Testing (AAT) that specifically evaluates the reinsured block. This includes defining a Starting Asset Amount that must equal the post-reinsurance reserve and subjecting that asset base to various interest rate scenarios.29 Crucially, AG 55 introduces the Attribution Analysis, a step-by-step estimate designed to make reserve movements tied to offshore or captive reinsurance observable, traceable, and explainable.27 The analysis must bridge the gap between the pre-reinsurance statutory reserve and the post-reinsurance reserve, identifying exactly how much of the decrease is attributable to differences in assumptions, asset yields, or structural features of the treaty.28 If the ceding insurer lacks transparency into the actual asset portfolio of the reinsurer, they are mandated to apply a conservative proxy asset portfolio in their modeling.29
Forensic Autopsy of PHL Variable Insurance (Phoenix Life)
The rehabilitation of PHL Variable Insurance Company, formerly known as Phoenix Life, serves as a stark warning of the risks inherent in the aggressive use of captives and private equity-backed "Bermuda Triangle" strategies. Owned by the private equity firm Golden Gate Capital, PHL Variable was placed into rehabilitation by the Connecticut Department of Insurance in May 2024.6 Upon seizing control, regulators and the rehabilitator discovered a "hole much bigger than anticipated"—a deficiency in the company’s accounts totaling $2.2 billion.6 This deficiency had a profound impact on policyholders, with payouts limited and approximately $400 million in "nest eggs" effectively withheld from retirees.6
The Discontinuance of Permitted Practices
Forensic analysis revealed that the $2.2 billion hole was significantly exacerbated by the discontinuance of permitted practices. According to the Rehabilitator's First Accounting and Status Report, the company had relied heavily on opaque and complex intercompany transactions, including XOL arrangements, to artificially inflate its financial statements.4 When the rehabilitator moved to report the company’s condition under standard statutory accounting without these permitted deviations, the capital and surplus deficiency increased by a material amount.4 This suggests that the company was essentially insolvent for years, but its true condition was masked by regulatory concessions granted by its domicile.
The Bermuda Triangle Strategy and Contagion Risk
PHL Variable, along with other firms such as 777 Partners, utilized a strategy where life insurers generated steady funding from U.S. retirees through annuity sales, allocated those funds to high-yield alternative assets managed by an affiliated private equity firm, and then reinsured the downside risk with an offshore reinsurer in Bermuda or the Cayman Islands.6 The failure of this strategy at PHL and the subsequent license revocation of 777 Partners' offshore reinsurer by the Bermuda Monetary Authority in late 2024 highlight the contagion risk warned about by the FSOC.6 The FSOC’s 2025 Annual Report noted that reserves ceded to offshore jurisdictions more than doubled to over $1.1 trillion between 2019 and 2024, creating a systemic vulnerability where U.S. policyholders' security is dependent on foreign jurisdictions with less stringent reserve requirements.6
Systemic Risk and the Role of Insurance Guaranty Funds
The proliferation of captive reinsurance and the reliance on synthetic assets like XOL entries pose a significant threat not only to policyholders but also to the taxpayers and other insurers who fund state guaranty associations. Forensic experts like Tom Gober point out that when an insurer fails, state regulators must often navigate foreign courts to recover assets, potentially repatriating only "pennies on the dollar" for U.S. policyholders.6 Furthermore, there is a conflict in the accounting standards that apply to the insurance guaranty funds themselves. These funds, which are the final safety net for policyholders, often prepare their financial statements using accounting principles that differ from U.S. GAAP, which some auditors warn present only the General Operating Account and may not fairly present the full financial position of the fund.1 This lack of transparency at the guaranty fund level suggests that the true capacity of the industry to absorb a major failure may be overstated.
The Rise of Shadow Insurance
The term "shadow insurance" has been adopted by state regulators and international bodies like the IMF to describe these arrangements.1 The growth of affiliated reinsurance primarily through captives represents a shift of risk to entities that are subject to less stringent regulatory rules than traditional insurers.7 As of year-end 2024, U.S. life carriers had ceded 38% of their $2.4 trillion in reserves to Bermuda-based reinsurers, while more than $50 billion in new capital entered the Bermuda market since 2016, much of it from large alternative asset managers.27 The absence of meaningful transparency in reporting means that a Bermuda reinsurer could be taking on dangerous risks without the policyholder or U.S. state regulator being aware of it.6
Conclusion and Future Regulatory Outlook
The forensic evidence and regulatory trends analyzed in this report lead to several nuanced conclusions regarding the future of life insurance solvency in the United States. The current system of state-permitted practices has created a fragmented regulatory environment where the reported surplus of a major insurer can be fundamentally changed by the stroke of a domiciliary commissioner’s pen. The admission of XOL agreements as assets, while providing temporary capital relief, introduces a high degree of synthetic surplus that may not be available to pay claims during a systemic downturn.
The implementation of AG 55 is a necessary and significant step toward reclaiming oversight of asset-intensive reinsurance. By requiring attribution analysis and demonstrating the adequacy of assets under adverse conditions, the NAIC is forcing a level of transparency that was previously absent. However, the effectiveness of AG 55 will depend on the willingness of state regulators to enforce these standards and challenge the proxy portfolios used by insurers who lack transparency into their offshore affiliates. The collapse of PHL Variable Insurance underscores the urgent need for a more holistic view of insurance holding company systems. The "Bermuda Triangle" strategy demonstrates that risks are not being eliminated but are instead being transformed from insurance risk into counterparty and investment risk.
Future regulatory efforts should focus on consolidated group capital requirements and more frequent onsite examinations to ensure that the interdependence of affiliates does not lead to a domino effect of insolvencies. Ensuring that insurance remains a reliable utility for retirees requires a move away from the shadow practices of the last two decades and a return to the conservative statutory principles that once defined the industry. The ongoing work of forensic accountants and the continued pressure from federal oversight bodies like the FSOC will be critical in maintaining the integrity of the U.S. insurance market.
