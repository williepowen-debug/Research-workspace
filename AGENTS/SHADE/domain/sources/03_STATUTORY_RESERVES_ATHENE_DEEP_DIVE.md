Structural Arbitrage and Capital Optimization: A Comprehensive Analysis of Captive Reinsurance, Statutory Reserves, and Institutional Liquidity in the Life Insurance Industry
The evolution of the life insurance industry over the past three decades has been defined by a sophisticated transition from traditional risk-pooling to advanced capital management and structural optimization. As interest rates remained suppressed for much of the early 21st century, life insurers faced increasing pressure to generate yield while navigating a regulatory framework designed for a simpler, more conservative era. This pressure culminated in the widespread adoption of captive reinsurance and the strategic use of regulatory "permitted practices" to manage what the industry perceived as excessive and redundant statutory reserve requirements. The following report provides an exhaustive examination of these mechanisms, the actuarial foundations that necessitate them, the offshore structures that facilitate them—specifically through the lens of Athene and the Bermuda-US nexus—and the emerging liquidity risks associated with institutional deposit-type contracts.
The Foundations of Actuarial Solvency: Statutory Reserves and the Regulatory Mandate
To understand how life insurers optimize their balance sheets, one must first grasp the concept of statutory reserves. Unlike corporate accounting for most industries, which is governed by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) to provide investors with a "going concern" view of a company's profitability, insurance regulation in the United States is governed by Statutory Accounting Principles (SAP). SAP is fundamentally focused on solvency and the protection of policyholders, adopting a "liquidation" perspective that prioritizes the insurer's ability to pay claims even under adverse conditions.1
The Philosophy and Legal Genesis of Reserving
Statutory reserves are the legal liabilities an insurance company must maintain on its balance sheet to represent its unmatured obligations—essentially, the funds set aside today to pay for death benefits, annuity payments, or health claims that may not come due for decades.3 The legal authority for these requirements is rooted in the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, which delegated the primary responsibility for insurance regulation to the individual states.1 Consequently, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) serves as a standard-setting body, but the actual force of law resides with state insurance departments, which adopt the NAIC’s Accounting Practices and Procedures Manual (AP&P Manual) and its Statements of Statutory Accounting Principles (SSAP).2
Reserves exist because insurance cash flows are inherently mismatched. In many life insurance products, such as level-premium whole life or long-term disability, the premiums collected in the early years of the policy are significantly higher than the expected claims. Conversely, in the later years, the mortality or morbidity risk increases exponentially, and the premiums collected in those years alone would be insufficient to cover the claims. Statutory reserves act as a financial bridge, accumulating the "excess" premiums from early years to ensure the insurer is prepared for the inevitable "hump" of future obligations.1
Mathematical and Actuarial Methodologies
The calculation of these reserves has traditionally relied on a formulaic, rule-based approach. The standard method for life insurance is the Commissioner’s Reserve Valuation Method (CRVM), while annuities are governed by the Commissioner’s Annuity Reserve Valuation Method (CARVM).3 These methods utilize prescribed mortality tables and interest rate assumptions that are often intentionally conservative.1

This occurs when the statutory formula requires an insurer to hold significantly more capital than their own internal, realistic "economic" models suggest is necessary based on modern mortality data and actual investment yields.4 This perceived redundancy became the primary catalyst for the development of captive reinsurance and the industry's shift toward Principle-Based Reserving (PBR).

Permitted Practices: The Mechanism for Regulatory Deviation
While the NAIC provides a unified framework, the state-based nature of U.S. regulation allows for individual state insurance departments to grant "permitted practices." These are accounting treatments specifically requested by an insurer that depart from the NAIC SAP or state-prescribed rules.2 A permitted practice essentially allows a domestic insurer to report its financial condition differently than the standard rules would require, often resulting in higher reported surplus or lower required reserves.5
The Logic of the Deviation
Insurers typically seek permitted practices to address anomalies where standard accounting rules would penalize a company for a risk that its regulator agrees is overstated or where the standard treatment does not reflect the economic reality of a specific transaction.5 These deviations are not universal; they are specific to a single company and must be approved by its domiciliary regulator at least 30 days before filing a financial statement reflecting the practice.5
For example, the Iowa Insurance Division (IID), which oversees Athene’s primary U.S. operating company, has historically allowed certain permitted practices related to derivative accounting and indexed annuity reserve calculations.13 Specifically, Athene was permitted to use an amortized cost method for call options used to hedge interest growth in indexed products, rather than the fair value approach mandated by NAIC SSAPs.13 This smoothing of derivative values prevents volatility in reported statutory surplus that could be triggered by short-term market fluctuations, even if those fluctuations have no impact on the long-term solvency of the company.
The Transparency Gap
The use of permitted practices has been a point of contention among regulators and industry analysts. When a state approves a deviation, it can make it difficult to compare the capital strength of that insurer against a peer in a different state that does not benefit from such a practice.12 Furthermore, these practices can sometimes be used to recognize assets that would otherwise be "non-admitted" (valued at zero) under standard SAP rules.2
A notable example occurred in Vermont, where Athene’s captive, Athene Re USA IV, was granted a permitted practice to include the face amount of letters of credit (LOCs) as admitted assets to fund its reinsurance obligations to its parent.13 Without this specific accounting deviation, the captive would have failed to meet its authorized control level risk-based capital (RBC) requirements, highlighting how permitted practices can serve as a critical, albeit opaque, pillar of an insurer’s capital structure.13
XXX/AXXX Reserve Financing: Managing the "Humpback" Reserve Crisis
The most pervasive use of captive reinsurance in the life industry arose from two specific regulatory developments: Regulation XXX and Actuarial Guideline AXXX (AG 38). These regulations were enacted to ensure insurers held adequate reserves for term life policies with long-duration level premiums and universal life policies with secondary guarantees (ULSG).8
The Mechanics of Regulation XXX and AXXX
Regulation XXX was introduced in the late 1990s to address term policies where premiums remained level for 20 or 30 years. Because the mortality tables used by regulators (such as the 1980 CSO) did not reflect the significant improvements in longevity, the statutory formulas required a "segmented" reserve approach.8 This meant that reserves would "hump" in the middle of the policy term, often reaching levels that insurers argued were five to ten times higher than the actual economic risk of the policy.8
AXXX applied a similar logic to ULSG products, which provide a guaranteed death benefit even if the policy’s cash value drops to zero, provided the policyholder pays a specified minimum premium. Regulators viewed these as "shadow term" policies and required reserves based on a funding ratio that often resulted in massive statutory liabilities.8
The Securitization and Captive Solution
To manage these "redundant" reserves without tying up billions of dollars in high-quality capital, insurers developed a financing model using special purpose vehicles (SPVs) or captive reinsurers.9 The process typically involves the following steps:
Cession to Captive: The primary operating company cedes a block of term or ULSG business to an affiliated captive reinsurer, typically located in a state with flexible captive laws (e.g., Vermont, South Carolina) or offshore.9
Tiered Collateralization: The captive must "fund" the reserves it assumes to allow the primary insurer to take a "reserve credit" (removing the liability from its own books).9 The funding is split into two tiers:
Primary Security: This equals the "economic reserve"—the amount the insurer realistically expects to pay out. It must be backed by cash and SVO-listed securities.14
Other Security: This covers the "redundant" portion—the gap between the economic reserve and the much higher statutory reserve. This is typically backed by "hollow assets" such as letters of credit (LOCs) or parental guarantees.14
Third-Party Financing: In many XXX/AXXX securitizations, the captive issues notes to third-party investors to raise the cash for the primary security, or it obtains an LOC from a bank to serve as the "Other Security".9
This structure allows the insurer to maintain its statutory RBC ratios while deploying the "redundant" capital into more productive investments or returning it to shareholders. However, it creates a "shadow" liability within the holding company that is often invisible to traditional statutory reporting.15

Shadow Insurance and the Offshore Migration: The Bermuda-US Nexus
The practice of moving liabilities from regulated operating companies to less-regulated, unrated affiliates—often labeled "shadow insurance" by critics—has grown from $11 billion in 2002 to over $364 billion by 2012.16 While the XXX/AXXX captives were primarily domestic, the modern frontier of reserve optimization has moved offshore, with Bermuda emerging as the global hub for "asset-intensive reinsurance" (AIR).7
The Bermuda Regulatory Divergence
Bermuda is not merely a tax-advantaged jurisdiction; it has developed a sophisticated principles-based regulatory regime that is recognized as equivalent to Solvency II by the European Union and as a "reciprocal jurisdiction" by the NAIC.7 The core of the Bermuda advantage lies in the Bermuda Monetary Authority’s (BMA) Scenario-Based Approach (SBA) for calculating technical provisions.7
Under the SBA, a reinsurer can discount its liabilities using the projected yields of the specific asset portfolio supporting those liabilities—including the illiquidity premiums found in private credit and alternative investments.20 In contrast, U.S. statutory accounting for annuities (CARVM) often requires much more conservative discounting and includes a "cash surrender floor," which forces insurers to hold enough assets to cover a 100% mass-lapse scenario at all times.7
This allows Bermuda reinsurers to hold a "Total Asset Requirement" (TAR) that is often lower than the U.S. requirement for the same block of business, not because they are under-capitalized, but because their model accounts for the higher yield of the underlying assets.7 This is particularly attractive for private equity-backed insurers that specialize in direct origination of loans and other high-yielding, illiquid assets.
Hollow Assets and Parental Guarantees
A significant concern regarding offshore affiliates is the use of non-admitted assets to back reserves. In many "shadow" transactions, a captive may record an undrawn letter of credit with a parental guarantee as an asset on its books.15 While New York and other strict jurisdictions do not allow such "hollow assets" to count toward reserves, many captives in lenient jurisdictions rely on them to bridge the gap between their available capital and their required liabilities.15
The danger of this practice is that in a systemic downturn, the parent company may be unable to honor its guarantee, or the bank issuing the LOC may face its own liquidity crisis.15 If the assets backing the policies are "contingent loans" rather than cash or bonds, the "cushion" intended to protect policyholders essentially evaporates when it is most needed.15
Case Study: Athene and the Apollo Partnership
Athene Holding Ltd. represents the archetype of the modern, asset-intensive life insurer. Founded in 2009 and now a subsidiary of Apollo Global Management, Athene has pioneered a model that combines aggressive annuity origination with sophisticated offshore reinsurance and alternative asset management.25
The Athene Corporate Architecture
Athene’s structure is a meticulously designed nexus of U.S. and Bermuda entities. It conducts the majority of its organic business (retail annuities, pension risk transfers) through U.S. subsidiaries like Athene Annuity and Life Company (AAIA), domiciled in Iowa.25 However, the vast majority of its regulatory capital is held offshore.
25
Athene leverages its Bermuda subsidiary, Athene Life Re (ALRe), to act as a central hub for pooling risk across its various units.25 This is often done through "Funds Withheld" or "Modified Coinsurance" (ModCo) agreements, where the assets remain in the U.S. for regulatory purposes, but the economic exposure and the "excess" capital requirements are managed in Bermuda under the BMA’s more flexible regime.25
The ACRA Sidecar Model: ADIP
A unique feature of Athene’s capital strategy is the Athene Co-Invest Reinsurance Affiliate (ACRA).25 ACRA is an on-demand capital vehicle funded roughly two-thirds by third-party institutional investors (via the Athene Dedicated Investment Program, or ADIP) and one-third by Athene itself.26 This allows Athene to pursue massive deals—such as the $83.65 billion in gross premiums and deposits recorded in 2025—without needing to raise dilutive equity at the parent level.27
Through ACRA, Athene can "rent" the capital of pension funds and sovereign wealth funds to support its reserves. As of December 31, 2025, ACRA 1 and ACRA 2 combined had $142.1 billion of retroceded reserve liabilities.27 This structure transforms Athene from a traditional insurer into a "capital-light" spread business, where Apollo manages the assets, Athene manages the liabilities, and third-party investors provide the "buffer" capital.27
Institutional Liabilities: Deposit-Type Contracts and the Physics of a "Run"
While the industry has focused heavily on mortality and longevity risk, the rapid growth of "deposit-type contracts" has introduced a different species of vulnerability: institutional liquidity risk.24
Defining Deposit-Type Contracts
Under SSAP No. 50, deposit-type contracts are those in which the insurer does not assume any mortality or morbidity risk.30 They act exclusively as investment vehicles and are more comparable to financial instruments issued by banks than to traditional insurance policies.30 The category includes:
Guaranteed Interest Contracts (GICs): Fixed-term deposits typically used by pension plans.33
Funding Agreements: Contracts that guarantee a return of principal and a fixed or floating rate of interest.30
Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (FABNs): A sophisticated structure where an SPV issues tradable notes to institutional investors, backed by a funding agreement from the insurer.24
The Anatomy of Run Risk: Retail vs. Institutional
The "run risk"—the danger of a mass withdrawal of funds—is fundamentally different for retail policyholders versus institutional investors.
1. Retail Policyholder Surrenders: Individual annuity owners are subject to significant "friction" that discourages withdrawals. Most annuities have a surrender charge period (often 7 to 10 years) where withdrawing funds costs the owner 5-10% of their principal.33 Furthermore, many Athene products include Market Value Adjustments (MVAs); if interest rates rise, the insurer reduces the payout to the surrendering policyholder to reflect the decline in the value of the underlying bonds.33 This "locks in" the capital and protects the insurer's liquidity.
2. Institutional Deposit Withdrawals: Institutional products like FABNs do not have surrender charges or MVAs in the traditional sense, but they are governed by fixed maturity dates.24 The "run" in this context is not a sudden withdrawal, but a "lumpy" maturity wall where billions of dollars in obligations come due in a short window.24 If the market for new notes is closed (a "roll risk") and the underlying assets—such as the private credit and CLOs favored by Apollo—are illiquid, the insurer could face a liquidity crisis.24
Athene’s "Athene Global Funding" program, for instance, has a $35 billion capacity for issuing FABNs.34 These notes are senior unsecured obligations that rank equal to policyholders in the event of liquidation, but they are explicitly not covered by state insurance guaranty funds.24

The NISA Debate and Credit Spreads
The risk profile of these institutional deposits is reflected in the market price. The "ELB" (Economic Loss to Beneficiaries) metric developed by NISA uses the credit spreads on an insurer’s FABNs as a proxy for its default risk.36 In 2023, Athene’s FABNs showed significantly higher credit spreads than traditional insurers like New York Life, suggesting that institutional bond markets perceive a higher level of risk in Athene’s asset-intensive, private-equity-linked model.36 While Athene argues that these spreads are a measure of illiquidity and market volatility rather than true default risk, the divergence highlights the market's awareness of the unique risks associated with institutional funding agreements.36
Strategic Synthesis: The Future of the Captive Model
The life insurance industry's reliance on captive reinsurers and offshore structures is a response to the "capital-heavy" nature of traditional statutory regulation. By ceding liabilities to affiliates in Bermuda or Vermont and utilizing permitted practices to recognize "Other Security" like LOCs, insurers have created a more flexible, yield-sensitive financial architecture.
However, this architecture relies on several critical assumptions:
Jurisdictional Equivalence: The model assumes that Bermuda’s SBA and the BMA’s oversight will continue to be viewed as equivalent to U.S. and EU standards. Any regulatory "crackdown" on the SBA's discounting methods could trigger a massive capital call for the industry.19
Asset Performance: The use of illiquidity premiums to justify lower reserves assumes that private credit and alternative assets will perform as expected. A systemic spike in defaults in the middle-market loan sector would hit asset-intensive insurers like Athene disproportionately.20
Liquidity Access: For institutional spread businesses, the ability to "roll" funding agreements is vital. A freeze in the credit markets—similar to the 2008 financial crisis—would test the liquidity of these new-age insurers in a way that traditional retail-focused companies are shielded from by their surrender charges.24
As the NAIC continues to implement VM-20 and VM-22 (principles-based reserving for life and annuities), the "redundancy" that drove the original captive movement may diminish.22 Yet, as long as there is a divergence between "economic" and "statutory" reality, the industry will continue to seek the most efficient shell for its liabilities, ensuring that the shadow insurance sector remains a central, if complex, pillar of global retirement security.
