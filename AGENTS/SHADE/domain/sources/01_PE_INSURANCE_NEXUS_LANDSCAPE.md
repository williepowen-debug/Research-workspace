Institutional Integration and the Private Credit Frontier: Mapping the Life Insurance and Private Equity Nexus
The landscape of the United States life insurance and annuity sector has undergone a fundamental structural transformation over the last fifteen years, evolving from a industry characterized by traditional asset-liability management toward a highly integrated nexus with the alternative asset management (AAM) and private equity (PE) sectors. This convergence is not merely a tactical shift in investment strategy but a wholesale reorganization of the insurance value chain, where the liabilities generated by insurance premiums serve as a reservoir of permanent capital for the private credit and asset-backed finance (ABF) origination engines of global investment firms.1 As of year-end 2024, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) Capital Markets Bureau identified 137 PE-owned U.S. insurers, controlling approximately $704.3 billion in cash and invested assets—a scale that has more than doubled since 2018.3 This report provides an exhaustive mapping of this nexus, detailing the specific relationships between leading private equity firms and their insurance subsidiaries, the jurisdictional frameworks enabling their growth, and the strategic implications of their asset allocation models.
The Macroeconomic Catalyst: From Negative Yields to Private Credit
The origins of the private equity-insurance nexus can be traced to the protracted period of low and even negative interest rates following the 2008 financial crisis. During this era, traditional life insurers, who predominantly invested in high-quality, publicly traded corporate bonds, struggled to generate the yields necessary to meet long-term policyholder guarantees.1 By 2020, as much as $15 trillion of negative-yielding assets existed globally, creating an existential challenge for the spread-based business models of life and annuity providers.5 This environment compelled insurance Chief Investment Officers (CIOs) to look beyond liquid credit markets toward illiquid, non-traditional assets that offered an "illiquidity premium"—excess yield generated by the complexity and long duration of private investments.6
Private equity firms stepped into this vacuum by offering specialized origination capabilities. Unlike traditional asset managers, firms such as Apollo, KKR, and Blackstone have built vast ecosystems for originating private loans, infrastructure debt, and asset-backed securities (ABS) that typically yield 50 to 250 basis points over comparable public corporate bonds.1 This yield advantage has enabled PE-controlled insurers to price their annuity products more aggressively, capturing a massive share of the retirement savings market. By 2024, PE-backed platforms accounted for 35% of new sales in fixed and fixed-indexed annuities in the U.S., a staggering increase from just 7% in 2011.1
The Scale of the PE-Owned Insurance Market
The growth of PE involvement in the insurance sector is underscored by the rapid accumulation of assets under management (AUM) within these specialized entities. The NAIC reports that PE-owned insurers now account for 7.8% of the total U.S. insurance industry’s $9 trillion in assets.3

This concentration in the life insurance segment is logical; life and annuity products generate long-duration, predictable liabilities that are ideally suited for the long-dated, illiquid private credit portfolios managed by private equity sponsors.1 Furthermore, 28 of these PE-owned insurers have become members of the Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB), utilizing nearly $26 billion in outstanding advances to further leverage their spread-generating activities.3
Apollo Global Management and Athene: The Archetypal Integration
The partnership between Apollo Global Management and Athene Holding Ltd. serves as the foundational paradigm for the integrated insurance-asset management model. Founded in 2009 and fully merged in 2022, the Apollo-Athene nexus demonstrates the power of combining a retirement services platform with a scaled investment origination engine.4 Athene’s primary function within the ecosystem is to generate "float"—low-cost, long-term capital from annuity premiums—which Apollo then deploys into a diversified array of private credit and alternative assets.4
Asset Scale and Financial Performance
Athene has consistently reported record-breaking inflows, driven by strength in retail annuity sales and funding agreement issuances. By year-end 2024, Athene’s total GAAP assets reached $362.8 billion, reflecting its position as one of the largest annuity providers in the United States.11 The firm’s "Net Invested Assets"—a management view that removes noncontrolling interests from its Apollo/Athene Dedicated Investment Program (ADIP) and other consolidations—stood at $248.6 billion as of December 31, 2024, and grew further to $262.4 billion by the end of the first quarter of 2025.12

Asset Allocation and the Strategy for "Excess Spread"
The investment philosophy of the Athene portfolio is centered on generating "excess return per unit of risk" through origination.13 While 97% of Athene’s fixed income portfolio is comprised of investment-grade assets, the firm achieves higher yields by targeting asset classes that generate structuring premiums, such as CLOs and ABS, rather than taking on incremental credit risk.10
A critical component of this strategy is the allocation to alternative investments. As of March 31, 2025, Athene’s net alternative invested asset value was $13.0 billion.12 Approximately 28% of these alternatives are invested in strategic origination platforms managed by Apollo, including Wheels, Redding Ridge, and MidCap Financial.12 This "origination" model allows the insurer to capture the fees and spreads that would otherwise go to commercial banks. Apollo’s broader credit business, which supports Athene, reached over $616 billion in AUM in late 2024, with private credit accounting for essentially all of the firm’s net growth in recent quarters.10
Reinsurance Structure and Jurisdictional Strategy
Athene utilizes a sophisticated reinsurance framework to optimize its capital structure, primarily leveraging its Bermuda-domiciled Class E reinsurer, Athene Annuity Re Ltd..11 Bermuda is a favored jurisdiction for such entities due to its sophisticated regulatory environment and capital treatment for long-duration insurance blocks. Furthermore, Athene employs a strategic third-party capital vehicle known as the Apollo/Athene Dedicated Investment Program (ADIP).15 ADIP acts as a reinsurance sidecar, allowing external institutional investors to provide the equity capital necessary to support the growth of Athene’s spread liabilities.15 As of mid-2024, the ADIP program managed more than $69 billion in invested assets, highlighting the scale of non-balance sheet capital supporting the nexus.16
KKR and Global Atlantic: The Re-domestication and ABF Strategy
The 2021 acquisition of Global Atlantic Financial Group by KKR represented a significant expansion of the PE-insurance nexus, bringing another major insurer under the control of a leading alternative manager. Global Atlantic, which originated within Goldman Sachs, has since become the centerpiece of KKR’s insurance strategy, providing a stable stream of liabilities that KKR manages through its global credit and real assets platforms.17
General Account Assets and Investment Composition
As of December 31, 2023, Global Atlantic reported total assets of $207.2 billion, a significant increase from $171.7 billion in 2022.18 The insurer’s general account is heavily weighted toward credit-related instruments that align with KKR’s expertise in asset-based finance and private corporate lending.

The allocation to mortgage and other loan receivables (27%) is particularly noteworthy, as it reflects the firm's strategic focus on private lending.18 Furthermore, Global Atlantic holds approximately $3.57 billion in CLOs and $2.81 billion in CBOs, assets that are frequently originated or structured by KKR’s debt capital markets team.18
Jurisdictional Evolution: Bermuda to Delaware
One of the most distinctive aspects of the Global Atlantic strategy is its 2023 re-domestication. Following the merger with KKR, the company was re-domesticated from Bermuda—an offshore hub—to Delaware, an onshore jurisdiction.18 This move reflects a broader trend among some PE-backed insurers to align more closely with U.S. domestic regulatory frameworks, potentially to facilitate greater market acceptance and transparency while still benefiting from specialized reinsurance structures.
Despite the move to Delaware, the group maintains offshore entities, such as Global Atlantic Assurance Limited, a Bermuda Class 3A reinsurer with $11.2 billion in total assets as of year-end 2024.19 This entity acts as an internal capital management tool, assuming risks from other Global Atlantic subsidiaries to optimize the group's overall capital efficiency.19
Brookfield and American Equity: The AEL 2.0 Transformation
Brookfield Reinsurance’s $4.3 billion acquisition of American Equity Investment Life Holding Company (AEL), completed in May 2024, demonstrates a different pathway for the PE-insurance nexus: the acquisition of a leading independent, publicly traded insurer by an alternative manager to accelerate a strategic pivot toward private credit.20
Scaling Assets and the AEL 2.0 Model
AEL was a pioneer in the fixed index annuity (FIA) market, but its historical focus was on a traditional return-on-equity (ROE) model supported by liquid corporate bonds.22 Under Brookfield’s leadership, the company has transitioned to an "AEL 2.0" strategy, focusing on a return-on-assets (ROA) model that leverages Brookfield’s global alternative investment platform.22 This integration immediately increased Brookfield Reinsurance's insurance assets under management to over $100 billion.20
Asset Allocation and Direct Lending
Brookfield has wasted no time in rotating AEL’s portfolio toward its own originated assets. Following the acquisition, AEL committed and loaned $415.3 million to Brookfield Securitization CFO Inc. and $165 million to BIP REU Holdings (2016) LP.23 These direct loans are emblematic of the "originate-to-hold" model, where the insurer provides the capital for the parent company’s credit vehicles. By December 31, 2023, AEL had already reported over $451 million in proceeds from "Other Invested Assets," a category that includes private equity and private credit fund commitments.23
Captive Reinsurance: The Vermont and Bermuda Nexus
Brookfield/AEL utilizes one of the most complex and transparently domestic captive structures in the industry, focused heavily on the state of Vermont.

The use of Vermont is significant; it is a leading domestic captive jurisdiction that allows insurers to utilize specialized accounting treatments for certain reserves while remaining under the supervision of a U.S. state regulator.23 This "domestic captive" strategy allows Brookfield to manage the high capital charges associated with AEL’s lifetime income benefit reserves more efficiently than would be possible under standard Iowa statutory accounting alone.23
Blackstone and Resolution Life: Global Consolidation and Sub-Advisory
Blackstone’s involvement in the insurance sector is characterized by its strategic partnership with Resolution Life, a global specialist in the acquisition and management of in-force life insurance portfolios.25 In 2023, the two firms closed a $3 billion equity capital raise, establishing Resolution Life as Blackstone’s primary partner for the life and annuity consolidation market globally.27
Asset Scale and the "Funds Withheld" Model
As of year-end 2024, Resolution Life managed approximately $85 billion in assets.25 Its balance sheet is notable for the significant use of "Funds Withheld" assets, which accounted for $18.9 billion (21% of total assets) in its 2024 Successor financial statement.28
Sources: 28
The "Funds Withheld" mechanism is a common feature in global reinsurance transactions, where the assets backing a reinsured block of policies remain on the books of the original ceding insurer, while the investment management authority is transferred to the reinsurer (Resolution Life) and its sub-advisor (Blackstone).28 This structure mitigates counterparty risk for the ceding insurer while allowing Blackstone to execute its investment strategy.
Blackstone’s Role as Investment Manager
Blackstone serves as the investment manager for Resolution Life across several key asset classes, including directly originated private credit, real estate, and asset-based finance.26 This relationship ensures that Resolution Life has access to the high-yielding, investment-grade credit necessary to maintain the profitability of the closed books it acquires.26 In late 2024, it was announced that Nippon Life would acquire 100% of Resolution Life for $10.6 billion, although the investment management relationship with Blackstone will remain in place, demonstrating the enduring value of the origination platform.26
Global Reinsurance Hubs
Resolution Life operates as a global entity with major hubs in:
Bermuda: Resolution Re, a Class E reinsurer focused on institutional business and global portfolio management.25
Singapore: A regional office established in 2024 to target the Asian consolidation market, which recently supported a $2 billion flow deal with a Japanese insurer.25
United States: Regional offices in West Chester and New York focused on flow and PRT reinsurance.25
Ares and Aspida: The Agile New Entrant
Ares Management’s entry into the insurance sector via Aspida, founded in 2020, highlights the speed with which a major AAM can build a scaled insurance platform from the ground up.31 Aspida is positioned as a tech-forward annuity and reinsurance company that leverages the "Ares Insurance Solutions" (AIS) team for investment management and capital optimization.31
Rapid Growth and Asset Composition
By September 2024, Aspida had grown to over $19 billion in assets, and by September 2025, that figure reached $27.4 billion.31 This rapid growth has been supported by $2.3 billion in equity capital raised by Ares Management.32

The portfolio is highly diversified across structured credit (ABS, CLO, MBS), which together account for 51% of total assets.33 This heavy weighting toward structured products is a hallmark of the Ares model, which utilizes the firm’s deep expertise in credit structuring to find attractive risk-adjusted yields. Furthermore, over 93% of the total portfolio is investment-grade, reflecting the group’s focus on asset quality.33
Offshore and Institutional Structures
Aspida utilizes two primary reinsurance entities to manage its global operations:
Aspida Life Re Ltd. (Bermuda): A Bermuda Class E life and annuity reinsurer.33
Aspida Re Cayman Ltd. (Cayman Islands): A Cayman-based reinsurer that contributes to the group’s $2.2 billion in total regulatory capital.33
These entities allow Aspida to provide efficient reinsurance solutions to third-party clients globally while also managing the internal risk from its domestic U.S. annuity platform, Aspida Life Insurance Company, which is based in Durham, North Carolina.31
The Apollo-MassMutual Strategic Alliance
The relationship between Apollo Global Management and Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Company (MassMutual) represents a more complex, partnership-oriented facet of the PE-insurance nexus. MassMutual is a traditional mutual insurer, owned by its policyholders, yet it has become a major capital partner to the private equity industry.13
Minority Equity in ATLAS SP and ABF Franchises
In April 2024, Apollo and MassMutual announced a strategic transaction in which MassMutual became a minority equity owner in ATLAS SP Partners, Apollo’s warehouse finance and securitized products business.13 As part of this multibillion-dollar commitment, MassMutual also agreed to invest in Apollo’s Asset-Backed Finance (ABF) franchise.13
This transaction allows MassMutual to access investment-grade, asset-backed credit originated by ATLAS SP and the broader Apollo ecosystem.13 For MassMutual, this provides a "scaled origination ecosystem" that complements its wholly owned asset manager, Barings, without requiring the insurer to build out a massive internal credit structuring team for every asset class.13 For Apollo, the partnership provides a major source of stable, institutional capital to fuel its $20 trillion ABF opportunity.13
The Creation of Martello Re
MassMutual further deepened its involvement in the PE-insurance nexus by spearheading the launch of Martello Re Limited in 2022.38 Martello Re is a Bermuda-domiciled Class E reinsurer launched with $1.65 billion in initial equity from a group including MassMutual, Centerbridge Partners, and Brown Brothers Harriman (BBH).38
Martello Re was created to provide reinsurance capacity primarily for MassMutual’s current product offerings, with MassMutual initially reinsuring $14 billion of general account liabilities to the new entity.38 The investment portfolio is managed jointly by Barings and Centerbridge, focusing on both public and private asset origination.38 By mid-2024, Martello Re had grown to over $23 billion in assets under management.42 This model represents a "hybrid" approach, where a traditional insurer creates a startup reinsurer in partnership with private equity managers to manage its own spread-based liabilities more efficiently.
The Jurisdictional Map: Domestic and Offshore Hubs
A critical enabler of the PE-insurance nexus is the use of specialized jurisdictions that offer flexible regulatory and accounting frameworks. These jurisdictions are categorized into offshore hubs and what critics often refer to as domestic "secrecy jurisdictions" within the United States.24
Offshore Hubs: Bermuda and Cayman
Bermuda is the preeminent global hub for the PE-insurance nexus. It offers a sophisticated "Solvency II-equivalent" regulatory regime that provides significant capital credit for high-quality private credit investments and structured products.11 Most major PE firms, including Apollo (Athene), KKR (Global Atlantic), and Ares (Aspida), maintain their largest reinsurance entities in Bermuda. The Cayman Islands serve as a secondary hub, often favored for institutional pension risk transfer (PRT) and specialized funded reinsurance deals.25
Domestic Hubs: Vermont, South Carolina, Iowa, and Others
Within the U.S., five states have emerged as preferred locations for captive insurance companies used by PE-owned life insurers: Vermont, Iowa, South Carolina, Delaware, and Arizona.24
Vermont: The clear leader in the domestic captive market. It is used by Brookfield/AEL to house massive blocks of annuity business.23 Vermont’s captive laws allow for more flexible reserve financing arrangements than many other states.24
Iowa: As a major insurance hub, Iowa has updated its laws to support the competitive position of its domestic firms, such as AEL.22
South Carolina and Delaware: These states are increasingly used to host captives that assume risk from primary insurers, often employing accounting principles that differ from standard National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) SAP.24
These jurisdictions allow insurers to "offload" 80% to 90% of their assets and liabilities into affiliated reinsurance captives, shifting the risk from the primary, highly regulated entity to a lighter regulated venue.24
Systemic Shifts in Asset Allocation and Risk Management
The transition of $700 billion into PE-owned insurers has significantly altered the aggregate investment profile of the U.S. insurance industry. Data from the NAIC and academic researchers indicate that PE-owned insurers are the primary drivers of the shift toward private placements and structured credit.3
Bonds, Mortgages, and Private Placements
For PE-owned insurers, bonds remain the largest asset class but have declined as a percentage of total investments, while mortgages have grown significantly.
Sources: 3
A substantial part of this growth stems from "private credit extension to financial borrowers and to privately placed asset-backed securities".7 Private placements accounted for 14% of life insurers’ general account assets in 2024, up from 10% in 2014, with PE-owned firms showing a private placement share expansion 7 percentage points higher than non-PE-owned peers.7
The Liquidity and Credit Question
The fundamental trade-off in the PE-insurance nexus is between yield and liquidity. Private placements are, on average, only half as likely to be traded as public bonds in a given quarter, reflecting their inherent illiquidity.7 However, proponents argue that because insurance liabilities (annuities) are themselves illiquid—often carrying surrender charges that discourage early withdrawal—there is a natural matching between illiquid assets and illiquid liabilities.1
From a credit perspective, the vast majority of these assets are rated investment-grade. For example, Athene and Aspida both report that over 93-97% of their fixed income portfolios are investment-grade.10 The risk, therefore, is not primarily "junk bond" credit risk, but rather the complexity of the underlying collateral in structured products and the potential for "spread compression" if interest rates fall or if origination markets become overcrowded.34
Conclusion: The Structural Permanence of the Nexus
The life insurance and private equity nexus has moved beyond its experimental phase to become a permanent structural feature of the global financial architecture. The scale is immense, with nearly $1 trillion of life and annuity products now managed by alternative asset managers.1 Firms like Apollo, KKR, and Brookfield have successfully transformed themselves from asset-light investment managers into capital-intensive, spread-based financial institutions that rival the largest commercial banks in their credit origination capacity.2
This integration provides a path to scalability and resilience for the insurance sector, offering policyholders higher yields and insurers more robust capitalization through the support of deep-pocketed private equity parents.2 However, it also introduces a new layer of organizational and regulatory complexity. The extensive use of captive reinsurance in Bermuda, Vermont, and the Cayman Islands, combined with a significant tilt toward private and structured credit, creates a system that is more efficient but also more interconnected.7 As these platforms continue to grow, the focus for both industry participants and regulators will shift toward ensuring that the "illiquidity premium" remains a source of strength rather than a point of vulnerability in the face of future market cycles.
