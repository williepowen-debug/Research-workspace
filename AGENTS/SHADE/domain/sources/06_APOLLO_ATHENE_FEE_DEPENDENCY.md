Institutional Analysis of the Structural and Economic Integration of Apollo Global Management and Athene Holding: Implications for Equity Valuation and Solvency Risk
The architectural fusion of Apollo Global Management (AGM) and Athene Holding Ltd (AHL) represents one of the most significant evolutions in the history of alternative asset management. Historically, the alternative investment landscape was defined by the cyclicality of private equity fundraising and the volatility of performance-linked incentive fees. However, through the systematic construction of Athene, Apollo has effectively transitioned into a diversified financial services powerhouse that operates as an integrated "flywheel" where retirement services liabilities provide the permanent capital necessary to fuel a global asset management and credit origination platform. As of the fiscal year ending December 31, 2025, and into the first quarter of 2026, Apollo’s beneficial ownership of 100% of Athene’s common stock has cemented an economic dependency that is both profound and structurally complex.1 For institutional investors and fiduciaries assessing the Apollo equity thesis, particularly those evaluating the sustainability of the current valuation against a potential $100 put option scenario, the primary analytical imperative lies in deconstructing the "Related Earnings" streams to determine the precise attribution of equity value to Athene’s balance sheet operations.
The Economic Engine: Quantifying Revenue and Earnings Dependency
The consolidated earnings profile of Apollo Global Management is now bifurcated into two primary operational pillars: Fee Related Earnings (FRE), derived from the asset management segment, and Spread Related Earnings (SRE), generated exclusively by Athene’s retirement services segment. To understand the economics from Apollo's perspective, one must observe the interplay between these two metrics. In 2025, Apollo reported record combined fee and spread-related earnings of $5.889 billion.2 Of this total, SRE accounted for $3.361 billion, representing approximately 57.1% of Apollo's core operating profitability.2 This indicates that more than half of the company’s bottom line is not "fee-based" in the traditional asset management sense, but is instead "spread-based," derived from the difference between the investment yield earned on Athene’s massive asset portfolio and the interest credited to its annuity policyholders.5
The "Asset Management" segment, which generated $2.378 billion in management fees in 2025, is itself a derivative of Athene’s scale.2 Athene constitutes the single largest component of Apollo’s Fee-Generating Assets Under Management (FGAUM), which reached $709 billion as of year-end 2025.2 The capital provided by Athene is categorized as "permanent," meaning it is not subject to the redemption risks typical of retail mutual funds or the fixed-term commitment periods of traditional private equity drawdown funds.8 This permanence allows Apollo to earn a high-margin, recurring fee stream that serves as the foundation for its FRE growth.

The Mechanics of the Asset Management Agreement (AMA)
The economic relationship between the two entities is governed by a series of sophisticated Investment Management Agreements (IMAs) and a master Fee Agreement. This framework has been amended nine times to reflect the scaling of the platform and to align incentives toward specialized asset origination. Under the Ninth Amended and Restated Fee Agreement, effective December 31, 2023, the fee structure is no longer a simple flat percentage of assets but is instead a tiered and modular system that rewards Apollo for "alpha-generating" activities.11
Tiered Base Management Fees
The "Base Fee" is intended to compensate Apollo for enterprise-wide services, including asset allocation, mergers and acquisitions diligence, and corporate support functions like risk management, tax, and legal.13 Following the 2019 revisions, the fee was structured to provide Athene with economies of scale while ensuring Apollo maintained a stable revenue floor. Apollo earns 22.5 basis points (0.225%) per year on the "Backbook Value" of Athene’s assets, defined as the $103.4 billion in assets held as of December 31, 2018.13 For all assets acquired after January 1, 2019, the base fee rate drops to 15 basis points (0.15%).13
Sub-allocation Fees and Alpha Generation
Beyond the base fee, Apollo earns "Sub-allocation Fees" that vary based on the complexity and specialized underwriting required for the asset class. This four-tiered structure is designed to incentivize Apollo to leverage its proprietary origination platforms (e.g., MidCap Financial, Atlas SP) to source private, investment-grade credit that offers higher yields than public markets.3

For assets acquired after January 1, 2019, the sub-allocation fee is generally subject to a cap of 10% of the market value of the assets, although the tiered percentages within that cap are meticulously calculated.14 Furthermore, a "Fee Reduction/Increase" mechanism exists where if more than 60% of Athene’s sub-allocated assets are invested in Core and Core Plus assets, Athene receives a 0.025% fee reduction on the "Incremental Value" of its portfolio.14 This ensures that Apollo is not over-compensated for managing a conservative portfolio that does not require its full "alpha" capabilities.
Incentive Fees and Subsidiary Profits
A critical second-order insight into Apollo's revenue is the profit interest held by key executives in the investment management entities. For instance, James Belardi, the CEO of Athene and a Director/Officer of Apollo, owns a profit interest in Apollo Insurance Solutions Group (ISG).15 Through this interest, he receives quarterly distributions equal to 3.35% of the base management fees and 4.5% of the sub-advisory fees paid by Athene.15 This alignment mechanism ensures that the leadership of the retirement services segment is directly incentivized to grow the fee-paying asset base of the parent company.
The Legal and Regulatory Trapdoor: Rehabilitation and Insolvency Risk
A fundamental component of the $100 put thesis is the risk that Athene could enter a state of regulatory "rehabilitation" or "liquidation," potentially resulting in the loss of Apollo’s fee stream. Because Athene is a regulated insurance carrier, its insolvency is governed by state law in Iowa (for its domestic subsidiaries) and by the Bermuda Insurance Act 1978 (for its reinsurance operations), rather than the federal U.S. Bankruptcy Code.17
Iowa Chapter 507C: The Commissioner's Powers
Under the Iowa Insurers Supervision, Rehabilitation, and Liquidation Act (Chapter 507C), the Iowa Insurance Commissioner has broad authority to petition the court for a rehabilitation order if an insurer is in a condition that would be "financially hazardous" to policyholders or the public.19 Upon such an order, the Commissioner (the "Receiver") takes possession of the insurer's assets and administers them under the court's supervision.18
In a rehabilitation scenario, the survival of Apollo’s fee stream is not guaranteed. While a Receiver typically seeks to maintain essential services to preserve the value of the asset portfolio, they have the power to reject or renegotiate "executory contracts." If the Receiver determines that the fees paid to Apollo are "excessive" or "unfair" to policyholders, the Investment Management Agreements could be terminated or the fee rates significantly reduced.20

However, if the Receiver decides to move the assets to a low-cost, passive index-tracking manager, Apollo’s high-alpha fee stream would be extinguished immediately.
Bermuda Regulatory Thresholds: The Target Capital Ratio
In Bermuda, the Bermuda Monetary Authority (BMA) monitors insurers against the "Enhanced Capital Requirement" (ECR). While the minimum legal coverage is 100%, the BMA mandates a "Target Capital Ratio" of 120%.22 If Athene’s capital falls below this threshold, the BMA can impose restrictions on dividends, mandate capital injections, or initiate a "Recovery Plan".22 As of late 2025, Athene maintained $2.9 billion in excess equity capital, providing a buffer against these regulatory triggers.6 However, a significant market shock to "illiquid" or "affiliated" assets—which the BMA assigns a "nil value" for certain stress-testing purposes—could rapidly erode this surplus.22
Structural Linkages and Cross-Default Vulnerabilities
The user’s query regarding cross-default provisions is critical to understanding the "contagion" risk between Athene and Apollo. Apollo Global Management, Inc. (the "Issuer") has issued several billion dollars in senior notes, including the 5.150% notes due 2035.23 These notes are guaranteed by Apollo’s asset management subsidiaries but are not guaranteed by Athene Holding Ltd. or its subsidiaries.23
Structural Subordination
Because Athene is a subsidiary of Apollo but not a guarantor of its debt, Apollo’s bondholders are "structurally subordinated" to all of Athene’s liabilities.24 This means that in a liquidation of Athene, policyholders and Athene’s own creditors must be paid in full before any value can flow up to Apollo to satisfy its senior notes or common stockholders. Athene’s subsidiaries had $347.2 billion in liabilities as of March 2025, including $5.3 billion of its own senior indebtedness.24
"Significant Subsidiary" Triggers
While the senior note indentures do not typically feature a "direct" cross-default where a downgrade of Athene triggers an Apollo default, the "Events of Default" clause includes bankruptcy or insolvency events involving the Issuer or any "Significant Subsidiary".23 A "Significant Subsidiary" is usually defined by the SEC as one that accounts for more than 10% of consolidated assets or income. Given that Athene provides 57% of Apollo’s earnings and the vast majority of its assets, it undeniably meets this definition.2 Consequently, if Athene were to enter a formal rehabilitation or receivership, it would likely constitute an Event of Default under Apollo’s senior note indentures, potentially leading to an acceleration of all outstanding debt.

Risk Factors: The Case for the $100 Put Thesis
The bearish thesis for Apollo, targeting a drop to $100, rests on several "fragility" points in the Athene relationship that the market may be mispricing.
The "Related Party" Asset Debate
Regulators have increasingly focused on the "affiliated" asset levels of private equity-owned insurers. In August 2025, Athene sparked a public debate by highlighting that its rivals (KKR’s Global Atlantic, Blackstone’s Everlake) hold higher proportions of related party assets.27 While Athene claims a 12% related party asset share, a statutory calculation by regulators puts it at 18%.27 The concern is that these assets—often loans to Apollo portfolio companies—may indirectly subsidize Apollo’s private equity funds at the expense of Athene’s policyholders. If the NAIC were to mandate higher capital charges for these assets, Athene’s ROE would drop, and its ability to pay dividends to Apollo would be curtailed.22
The "Lapse Risk" and Interest Rate Sensitivity
Athene’s portfolio is 72% annuity products.1 These products are sensitive to "lapse" risk—where policyholders surrender their contracts to seek higher yields elsewhere. The BMA has recently enhanced its "lapse shock" requirements, using Solvency II-style shocks for European business.22 If Athene experiences a "mass lapse" event, it would be forced to sell illiquid assets at a discount, potentially triggering the "rehabilitation" grounds in Iowa Code §507C.12(1)(a).19
The Termination Risk in the Bye-Laws
While the AMA seems permanent, Athene’s bye-laws allow for a termination election every two years (the next major date being June 4, 2023, and biennially thereafter).12 A termination notice must be given at least 30 days but no more than 90 days before the election date.12 For the termination to be valid, two-thirds of the independent directors must determine that Apollo's performance is "unsatisfactory" or its fees "unfair".20 While Apollo is currently the 100% owner, making a board-led revolt unlikely, a regulatory takeover would replace the board with a Receiver who would have no such loyalty and could trigger the "AHL Cause" termination provision immediately.12
Strategic Outlook and Growth Pillars for 2026
Despite these risks, Apollo management expresses "heightened confidence" in the platform's execution, projecting 20%+ FRE growth and $85 billion in inflows for 2026.3 This growth is predicated on three strategic pillars:
Origination: Scaling debt platforms to reach $300 billion+ in annual volume, with a focus on investment-grade "Single-A" average ratings.3
Global Wealth: Capturing $5 billion+ per quarter in retail inflows through semi-liquid products.29
Capital Solutions: Generating $800 million+ in annual fee revenue by providing "flexible capital" in volatile markets.3
The success of these pillars is entirely dependent on the "Athene Flywheel" remaining operational. If Athene cannot absorb the originated assets or provide the balance sheet stability to reassure retail investors, the entire Apollo growth narrative collapses.
Summary of Findings for the Equity Analyst
The architectural integration of Apollo and Athene has created a "permanent capital" vehicle of unprecedented scale, providing Apollo with a durable earnings stream (SRE) and a stable fee base (FRE). However, this integration has also created a binary risk profile. The economic dependency is absolute, with Athene driving over 60% of the parent’s equity value.
From a solvency perspective, Apollo has built significant "moats" through tiered fee agreements and structural subordination of its debt. However, the legal reality of insurance rehabilitation in Iowa and Bermuda creates a "trapdoor" effect. In a crisis, the Iowa Insurance Commissioner or the Bermuda Monetary Authority would prioritize policyholder protection over Apollo’s management fees, potentially stripping the parent of its primary revenue source.
The $100 put thesis finds its strongest support in the potential for regulatory "re-tiering" of capital charges on affiliated assets or a spike in surrender rates that forces a liquidity event. If Athene were forced into rehabilitation, Apollo would lose not only its $3.36 billion in SRE but also a $1 billion+ management fee stream, while likely triggering a technical default on its own $5.3 billion in senior debt. In such a scenario, the equity value of Apollo would be fundamentally compromised, as the "Asset Management" segment would be revealed as an engine with no fuel.
