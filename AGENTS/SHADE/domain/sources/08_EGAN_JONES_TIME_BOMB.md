Strategic Risk Assessment: The Egan-Jones Rating Crisis and Systemic Vulnerabilities in the Private Credit-Insurance Nexus
The financial architecture supporting the North American life insurance industry is currently facing a period of unprecedented structural instability, centered on the role of niche credit rating providers (CRPs) and the rapid proliferation of private credit. At the epicenter of this instability is the Egan-Jones Ratings Company (EJR), a firm whose methodologies, analyst capacity, and regulatory standing have come under intense scrutiny from both federal law enforcement and insurance regulators.1 The situation, often referred to by industry observers as the "Egan-Jones Time Bomb," represents a potential systemic failure where the revocation of credit ratings for thousands of private debt instruments could trigger an instantaneous and massive capital call across the insurance sector. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the operational triggers, regulatory shifts, and investigative status surrounding Egan-Jones, articulating the mechanics of the impending Risk-Based Capital (RBC) crisis.
The Convergence of Private Equity and Insurance Capital
The evolution of the insurance industry over the past decade has been characterized by a significant shift in asset allocation. Seeking higher yields in a prolonged low-interest-rate environment, insurance companies, particularly those owned or steered by private equity (PE) groups, have aggressively pivoted toward private credit. Currently, private equity groups direct approximately $700 billion of life insurance assets in the United States.3 This "Wall Street takeover" of the insurance sector has fundamentally altered the risk profile of insurer balance sheets, replacing publicly traded, liquid bonds with complex, illiquid, and often opaque structured-finance instruments.3
The attractiveness of these private credit assets for insurers is largely driven by their regulatory treatment. Under the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) framework, the capital an insurer must hold against an investment—the Risk-Based Capital (RBC) charge—is determined by the asset's credit rating. Historically, the NAIC has operated under a "Filing Exempt" (FE) process, which allows insurers to translate ratings from Nationally Recognized Statistical Rating Organizations (NRSROs) directly into NAIC designations.5 Egan-Jones, as an SEC-designated NRSRO, became a primary facilitator of this process for the private credit market.6
By securing an "investment-grade" rating from a firm like Egan-Jones, an insurer can classify a high-yield, high-risk private loan as a low-risk asset. This mechanism allows the insurer to minimize capital reserves, thereby freeing up capital for further investment or distributions to shareholders. However, the integrity of this system relies entirely on the accuracy and independence of the underlying credit ratings. Forensic accountant Tom Gober and other critics argue that this reliance has been exploited, creating a massive liquidity and solvency mismatch that is now being tested by regulatory and criminal interventions.1
The Egan-Jones Operational Model: Capacity and Conflict
Egan-Jones Ratings Company positions itself as a unique player in the credit rating ecosystem. Founded in 1995, it utilizes a primarily investor-supported model, which it claims minimizes the conflicts of interest inherent in the issuer-pay models used by the "Big Three" (S&P, Moody’s, and Fitch).7 Despite these claims, the firm’s expansion into private credit has raised alarms regarding its analytical capacity and the potential for "improper commercial influence".1
Analytical Disparity and "Ratings Shopping"
A central component of the investigation into Egan-Jones is the extreme disparity between the volume of its rated deals and the size of its analytical staff. In 2024, Egan-Jones reportedly graded over 3,000 investments in the private credit market with a team of only 20 analysts.1 This equates to an average of 150 complex private debt transactions per analyst per year. For professional peers in the credit risk domain, this ratio is considered highly anomalous, as the due diligence required for a single private structured-finance deal typically demands weeks of rigorous analysis.
The high volume of ratings produced by such a small team has led to allegations of "ratings shopping" and the use of "points systems" that prioritize sales and marketing over objective credit analysis.4 Senator Elizabeth Warren, in a formal inquiry to EJR CEO Sean Egan, highlighted reports that these systems may induce analysts to provide favorable ratings to attract and retain clients.4 This potential for inflated assessments is a primary driver of the systemic risk currently facing the insurance industry.

Identifying Affected PE-Insurer Assets
The reach of Egan-Jones ratings throughout the insurance industry is pervasive, extending into the portfolios of nearly all major life insurance groups in the United States. According to EJR’s own disclosures, all 25 of the top life insurance groups (measured by direct written premiums) hold instruments rated by the firm.6 These assets are not limited to traditional corporate bonds but include a wide array of complex private instruments.
Asset Classes and Structures
The typical issuers of securities rated by Egan-Jones include banks, asset managers, and private equity-backed financial institutions.7 The types of transactions involved are often illiquid and difficult to value, falling into the "Level 3" category of accounting standards where market prices are not readily available.1

The A-CAP and 777 Partners Exposure
One of the most prominent examples of EJR reliance involves the A-CAP insurance group and its relationship with 777 Partners. A-CAP utilized premiums from 250,000 policyholders to fund loans to 777 Partners, an investment firm with a history of complex and high-risk bets.1 To comply with risk-protection rules, these loans needed to be designated as "investment grade." Egan-Jones provided the ratings that made this possible, allowing A-CAP to funnel policyholder funds into these assets without triggering prohibitive RBC charges.1
Forensic accountant Tom Gober’s analysis of A-CAP insurers revealed a pattern of "affiliated paper and offshore reinsurance," where funds were moved to 777re, a Bermuda-based reinsurer.1 This structure created a lack of transparency and significant liquidity risks, which only became visible when federal investigators began probing the "improper commercial influence" on EJR’s rating procedures.1
Global Atlantic and KKR's Strategic Shift
While many firms remain deeply exposed to EJR ratings, some have begun taking preemptive measures to distance themselves. KKR’s insurance subsidiary, Global Atlantic, has publicly emphasized its reliance on major rating agencies like S&P, Moody’s, and Fitch, consciously moving away from smaller, niche raters like Egan-Jones.3 This shift underscores a growing recognition among sophisticated market participants that the regulatory and reputational risks associated with niche ratings are becoming untenable.
The Regulatory Pivot: The Bermuda Monetary Authority (BMA)
The first significant fracture in the EJR rating framework occurred in early 2026 when the Bermuda Monetary Authority (BMA) revoked its recognition of Egan-Jones for the purpose of determining solvency capital ratios.2 Bermuda is a global hub for the reinsurance industry, and many PE-backed insurers utilize Bermuda-based entities to manage their capital and tax exposure.
The Removal of Authorized Status
The BMA’s January 2026 capital and solvency handbook excluded Egan-Jones from the list of recognized agencies, which now includes only the "core four" (Moody’s, S&P, Fitch, A.M. Best) and a limited selection of optional agencies like Morningstar DBRS and KBRA.2 This decision prevents insurers from using EJR ratings to calculate their Bermuda Solvency Capital Requirement (BSCR).
The impact of this decision on insurers is immediate and binary. If an investment product is rated solely by Egan-Jones, it is now considered unrated by the BMA. Under Bermuda’s guidelines, such assets are assigned a BSCR rating of 8, which equates to a credit profile below triple-C-minus.2

For insurers with heavy EJR exposure, the BMA’s move forces them to either find a recognized agency to provide a new rating—which may be several notches lower—or hold massive amounts of additional capital. This has already led to a "windfall" for other rating companies as insurers scramble to replace EJR assessments before their next reporting cycle.2
NAIC Modernization: Ending "Blind Reliance"
Concurrent with the BMA’s actions, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) in the United States is overhaulng its own approach to credit ratings. For decades, the NAIC "blindly" relied on NRSRO ratings, a policy that regulators now admit provided no mechanism for due diligence or the ability to challenge unreasonable ratings.5
The Three-Notch Disparity and the SVO Backstop
The NAIC’s Securities Valuation Office (SVO) conducted a review of private letter ratings (PLRs) and discovered significant discrepancies. On average, PLRs from smaller agencies like Egan-Jones were three notches higher than the SVO’s own internal credit assessments.9 In approximately 7% of cases, the deviation was six notches or more, representing a total disconnect between the agency’s rating and the regulator’s view of risk.9
In response, the NAIC adopted a proposal to allow state regulators or the SVO to contest any rating that does not capture the full risk of an investment.10 If a rating is found to be three or more notches higher than the SVO’s assessment, the NAIC can override the rating and assign its own designation.9
The CRP Due Diligence Framework
The NAIC is currently developing a "Credit Rating Provider (CRP) Due Diligence Framework," which will establish clear quantitative and qualitative parameters for agencies whose ratings are used by insurers.5 This framework is designed to transition the SVO from a passive recipient of ratings to an active overseer of credit risk.
Quantitative Parameters: Evaluation of rating stability, default rates, and historical accuracy.5
Qualitative Parameters: Assessment of methodology, analyst qualifications, and internal controls against commercial influence.1
Regulatory Discretion: The SVO will maintain the ability to perform individualized credit assessments as a "backstop" to ensure capital consistency.5
This modernization, set for full implementation in 2026, represents a fundamental shift in the regulatory landscape. For Egan-Jones, whose ratings have been identified as the most frequent outliers, this framework effectively marks the end of its utility as a tool for capital optimization.9
Mechanics of the RBC "Time Bomb"
The "Time Bomb" refers to the mathematical reality of Risk-Based Capital requirements. RBC is designed to ensure that insurers have sufficient capital to absorb losses. The formula for the RBC charge on a specific asset is highly sensitive to its credit designation.
The Exponential Nature of Capital Charges
The NAIC uses a scale of 1 through 6 for designations, with 1 being the highest quality. Each designation is associated with an RBC factor. The shift from an NAIC 2 (BBB) to an NAIC 3 (BB) or NAIC 4 (B) represents an exponential increase in the capital requirement.

In a typical scenario, the move from investment-grade to speculative-grade can increase the capital charge by 300% to 500%. For an insurer with $10 billion in EJR-rated private credit, a wholesale downgrade could turn a $130 million capital requirement into a $500 million or $1 billion requirement overnight.
Forced Capital Calls and Liquidity Mismatches
When an insurer faces a sudden increase in its required capital (a "capital call"), it has three primary options, all of which are problematic in a distressed market.
Capital Injection: The parent company (often a PE firm) must provide fresh equity. However, if the PE firm itself is facing liquidity constraints or if the assets are underperforming, this may not be feasible.3
Asset Liquidation: The insurer can sell assets to reduce its required capital. Since these EJR-rated assets are largely illiquid private credit, selling them quickly would require significant "haircuts" on their value, further eroding the insurer’s capital surplus.1
Risk Transfer: The insurer can attempt to reinsure the risk. However, as the BMA has already shown, offshore reinsurers are facing their own regulatory crackdowns on EJR ratings, making this an unlikely escape route.2
This dynamic is exactly what occurred with Eurovita in Italy, where a liquidity mess triggered by withdrawal freezes required a massive rescue from multiple banks and insurers.3 Forensic accountant Tom Gober warns that the US insurance market is currently "setup for fire sale dynamics" if the EJR ratings are systematically revoked.3
The DOJ and SEC Investigation: Status and Implications
The "Time Bomb" is being accelerated by federal investigations into the internal operations of Egan-Jones. These investigations are not merely administrative; they are criminal and civil probes into the integrity of the US financial markets.
The SEC Probe into Commercial Influence
As of late 2025, SEC enforcement attorneys are actively investigating whether Egan-Jones executives "exerted improper commercial influence on its rating procedures".1 This probe specifically targets the period starting in 2019, when A-CAP began its significant lending to 777 Partners.1 The investigation focuses on whether the "investment grade" ratings provided by EJR were the result of analytical rigor or were bought through commercial relationships.
The DOJ Fraud Section and Criminal Investigations
The Department of Justice (DOJ) has significantly expanded its Criminal Fraud Section, which now includes 200 lawyers following a 2025 reorganization.12 The newly formed Market, Government, and Consumer Fraud (MGC) Unit is tasked with prosecuting large-scale financial crimes, including those involving securities fraud and the evasion of federal risk regulations.12
While the DOJ has not yet publicly confirmed the indictment of the "18-20 analysts" mentioned by Tom Gober, the broader investigation into 777 Partners and its associated entities has already produced high-level results. The former CFO of 777 Partners has pleaded guilty to fraud charges, and investigators are reportedly using this testimony to build cases against other participants in the scheme.3
The "Death Sentence" of Corporate Indictments
The DOJ has demonstrated a renewed willingness to indict corporate entities, ending a 15-year hiatus by charging three companies in 2025.14 This shift suggests that federal prosecutors are no longer swayed by the "death sentence" argument—that an indictment would destroy a financial firm. For Egan-Jones, a formal criminal indictment would lead to the immediate loss of its NRSRO status, effectively revoking every rating it has ever issued and detonating the RBC "Time Bomb" instantly.4
Market-Wide Consequences and Systemic Contagion
The implications of the Egan-Jones crisis extend beyond the firm itself and its immediate clients. The systemic nature of the risk arises from the concentration of these assets within the life insurance industry and the interconnectedness of the private credit market.
Fire Sale Dynamics and Policyholder Risk
If insurers are forced to liquidate $350 billion or more in EJR-rated assets 9, the resulting price collapse would create a "doom loop." Lower asset prices lead to further capital erosion, which leads to more forced selling. This "mark-to-market" reality is the greatest threat to policyholders. Tom Gober notes that if these companies were forced to value their assets at actual market prices rather than internal "mark-to-make-believe" models, their equity would be "wiped out".1
This scenario would leave insurers unable to pay claims for healthcare, home rebuilding, or life insurance benefits, potentially requiring a government bailout on a scale not seen since 2008.3 The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) has already noted that North American life insurers face a $150 billion shortfall in a severe downturn—a figure that would be exacerbated by an EJR-triggered crisis.3
Bank Lending and the Private Credit Ecosystem
The private credit market is also supported by significant bank lending, which has grown 145% in the past five years.4 Banks lend to the private debt funds that, in turn, sell the EJR-rated securities to insurers. A crisis in the insurance sector’s ability to hold these assets would lead to a withdrawal of bank credit, causing a systemic liquidity freeze across the broader economy.

Strategic Outlook and Recommendations for 2026
As the industry enters mid-2026, the resolution of the Egan-Jones crisis appears to be approaching its terminal phase. The combination of legislative pressure from figures like Senator Warren, the aggressive regulatory stance of the NAIC and BMA, and the criminal probes by the DOJ suggests a fundamental reordering of the niche credit rating market.
The Inevitability of Forced Deleveraging
Insurers must prepare for an environment where the "efficiency" of niche ratings is no longer a viable strategy. The NAIC SVO’s new "backstop" authority means that any asset whose rating appears out of line with market reality will be subjected to intense scrutiny and potential override.5
Immediate Action for Institutional Peers
For firms currently holding EJR-rated assets, the following steps are recommended:
Shadow Rating Analysis: Conduct internal or third-party assessments of EJR-rated portfolios using "Big Three" methodologies to identify potential capital "cliffs."
Contingency Capital Planning: Secure standby letters of credit or equity commitments to address potential RBC shortfalls.
Regulatory Communication: Proactively engage with state regulators to discuss the transition away from EJR ratings before the SVO initiates an override process.
The "Egan-Jones Time Bomb" is a consequence of years of regulatory arbitrage and a systemic underestimation of the risks associated with private credit. While the explosion of this bomb may be deferred through coordinated regulatory action, the underlying volatility remains. The shift from "blind reliance" to "rigorous due diligence" is a necessary, if painful, step toward restoring the long-term stability of the insurance industry.5 The upcoming implementation of the CRP Due Diligence Framework in late 2026 will be the final test of whether the industry can survive the loss of its favorite capital-optimization tool without a broader financial collapse.
