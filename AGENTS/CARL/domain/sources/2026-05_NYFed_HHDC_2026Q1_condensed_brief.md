## 1. Purpose and reading conventions

This brief reduces the 46-page report to the statistics and context most likely to matter for repeated machine ingestion. It removes repeated chart labels and most long-run visual history while retaining the latest-quarter figures, important directional changes, cross-product differences, age patterns, selected-state comparisons, and methodological cautions.

Two evidence labels are used:

- **Exact:** A value stated in the report text or summary.
- **Chart observation:** A relationship visible in a report chart. Numerical values are not treated as exact unless explicitly marked as approximate.

Page references use both the report's printed page number and the PDF page number where useful. The executive summary is on PDF page 2 and does not carry a printed report-page number.

## 3. Balances, originations, and available credit

### 3.1 Housing-related balances

- Mortgage balances increased by **$21 billion** to **$13.19 trillion**.
- HELOC balances increased by **$12 billion** to **$446 billion**.
- The HELOC increase was the **16th consecutive quarterly increase**.
- HELOC balances were **$129 billion above** their 2022 Q1 low.
- Mortgage originations were **$530 billion**, including both purchase and refinance originations as they first appeared on credit reports.
- HELOC limits increased by **$14 billion**, or **1.4%**.

**Source:** Summary, PDF p. 2.

### 3.2 Non-housing balances

- Total non-housing debt declined by **$15 billion**, or **0.3%**.
- Credit-card balances declined by **$25 billion** to **$1.25 trillion**; the report describes the Q1 decline as seasonal.
- Auto-loan balances increased by **$18 billion** to **$1.69 trillion**.
- Student-loan balances declined by **$6 billion** to **$1.66 trillion** and were described as essentially flat.
- Other balances declined by **$2 billion** to **$562 billion**. “Other” includes retail-card and consumer-finance loans.

**Source:** Summary, PDF p. 2.

### 3.3 New credit and credit limits

- New mortgage balances totaled **$530 billion**.
- New auto-loan balances totaled **$182 billion**.
- Aggregate credit-card limits increased by **$60 billion**, or **1.1%**.
- HELOC limits increased by **$14 billion**, or **1.4%**.
- The account chart shows credit-card accounts continuing to rise over the long run, while the number of mortgage, auto, and HE revolving accounts follows different historical paths.
- The new/closed-account chart shows inquiries below prior-cycle peaks and a gap between accounts opened and closed that varies over time.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report pp. 4-5 / PDF pp. 7-8; Report p. 10 / PDF p. 13.

### 3.4 Credit-score charts: comparability limitation

Beginning in 2026 Q1, the report changed the score used on the mortgage and auto origination charts from **Equifax Risk Score 3.0** to **VantageScore 4.0**. The 2026 Q1 score distributions therefore should not be treated as directly comparable with earlier quarters without accounting for the score-model change.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report pp. 6-9 / PDF pp. 9-12.

## 4. Delinquency and public-record measures

### 4.1 Aggregate delinquency

At the end of March 2026, **4.8% of outstanding debt** was in some stage of delinquency, approximately unchanged from 2025 Q4.

The stacked delinquency chart separates balances into 30 days late, 60 days late, 90 days late, 120+ days late, and severely derogatory. The current total remains well below the 2009-2010 peak shown in the historical series.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report p. 11 / PDF p. 14.

### 4.2 Transitions into early delinquency: 30+ days

The report uses annualized or four-quarter-moving-sum transition measures depending on the chart.

- Credit-card transition into early delinquency declined from **8.7% to 8.6%**.
- Mortgage transition into early delinquency declined from **3.9% to 3.8%**.
- Auto-loan transition into early delinquency was described as steady.
- The product chart shows credit cards and auto loans above mortgages and HELOCs at the latest point.
- Student-loan transition measures show a sharp recent increase after the unusually low readings visible during the pandemic-era reporting period.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report p. 13 / PDF p. 16.

### 4.3 Transitions into serious delinquency: 90+ days

- Mortgage transition into serious delinquency increased from **1.4% to 1.5%**.
- Auto-loan and credit-card serious-delinquency transitions were described as mostly unchanged.
- The product chart places credit-card serious-delinquency transitions above auto, mortgage, and HE revolving at the latest point.
- Student-loan serious-delinquency transitions show a sharp recent increase in the chart.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report p. 14 / PDF p. 17.

### 4.4 Stock of balances already 90+ days delinquent

- Student-loan balances 90+ days delinquent increased to **10.3%**, from **9.6%** in 2025 Q4.
- In the product chart, credit cards have the highest 90+ delinquency share among the non-student categories at the latest point.
- Auto loans are above mortgages and HE revolving.
- Mortgage and HE revolving 90+ delinquency shares remain the lowest categories in the latest chart.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report p. 12 / PDF p. 15.

### 4.5 Mortgage transition detail

The report provides two separate mortgage transition charts:

1. **Current mortgage accounts:** transitions to 30-60 days late and directly to 90+ days late.
2. **Mortgage accounts already 30-60 days late:** transitions back to current or forward to 90+ days late.

At the latest point, the charts show:

- Current-to-30/60-day-late transitions far below the 2008-2010 peak.
- Direct current-to-90+-day transitions also far below the prior-cycle peak.
- A larger share of 30-60-day-late mortgages returning to current than transitioning to 90+ days late.

**Sources:** Report pp. 15-16 / PDF pp. 18-19.

### 4.6 Foreclosures, bankruptcies, and collections

- **59,000** consumers had a new foreclosure notation in 2026 Q1, a slight increase from the prior quarter.
- **124,000** consumers had a new bankruptcy notation, unchanged from the prior quarter.
- The historical count chart shows both foreclosure and bankruptcy additions far below the peaks observed around the financial crisis.
- **5.0%** of consumers had a third-party collection account, a slight increase.
- The report's collection measure includes third-party collections recorded within the prior 12 months. The data dictionary notes that many collection actions are associated with medical and utility bills rather than conventional credit accounts.

**Sources:** Summary, PDF p. 2; Report pp. 17-18 / PDF pp. 20-21; Data Dictionary, report p. 43 / PDF p. 46.

## 5. Age-group findings

### 5.1 Distribution of total debt

- Aggregate debt is concentrated in the middle-age groups, especially ages **30-59**.
- The 18-29 group holds the smallest aggregate balance among the displayed age groups.
- Older age groups hold a growing share of aggregate debt over the long historical series.

**Source:** Report p. 20 / PDF p. 23.

### 5.2 Product mix by age

The 2026 Q1 composition chart shows:

- Mortgages are the largest debt category in every displayed age group.
- The 18-29 group has materially larger shares of student loans and auto loans than older groups.
- Student loans represent a declining share as age rises.
- HELOCs are a small share overall and are more visible among older age groups.
- Credit-card debt is a larger share of total debt for older groups than for the youngest group, although mortgage debt remains dominant.

**Source:** Report p. 21 / PDF p. 24.

### 5.3 Originations by age

- Auto-loan originations are distributed broadly across working-age groups, with 30-59 accounting for most dollar volume.
- Mortgage originations are concentrated in ages 30-59, with smaller contributions from 18-29 and 70+.
- Both origination charts show the pronounced 2020-2021 mortgage expansion and the later reduction from that peak.

**Sources:** Report pp. 22-23 / PDF pp. 25-26.

### 5.4 Serious delinquency by age

The all-product serious-delinquency chart shows the 18-29 group highest at the latest point, followed by 30-39, with older groups generally lower.

Product-specific charts show:

- **Mortgages:** age-group rates are tightly grouped and low at the latest point relative to the 2008-2010 peak.
- **Auto loans:** 18-29 is highest at the latest point; 30-39 is next.
- **Credit cards:** 18-29 is highest at the latest point; 30-39 is also above most older groups.
- **Student loans:** the recent increase appears across every age group. The 40-49 group is highest in the latest chart, with 30-39 and 50+ also elevated relative to 18-29.

**Sources:** Report pp. 24-28 / PDF pp. 27-31.

### 5.5 Foreclosures and bankruptcies by age

- New foreclosures remain low across every age group in the latest period compared with the financial-crisis peak.
- The largest number of foreclosures historically occurred among ages 30-59, reflecting the concentration of mortgage balances in those groups.
- New bankruptcies are also far below earlier peaks.
- The largest bankruptcy counts are concentrated in the middle-age groups rather than the youngest or oldest groups.

**Sources:** Report pp. 29-30 / PDF pp. 32-33.

## 7. Definitions and methodological cautions

### 7.1 Sample and population

- The Consumer Credit Panel is based on a nationally representative **5% random sample** of individuals with a Social Security number and a credit report, usually age 19 or older.
- Household members at the same address are also sampled, producing a panel of approximately **44 million individuals per quarter**.
- Most displayed results are produced from a 0.1% sample drawn from the larger panel for processing efficiency.
- The full 5% sample is used for foreclosure and bankruptcy calculations because those events are relatively infrequent.
- A 1% sample is used for student-loan data to address servicer discrepancies.

**Sources:** Data Dictionary, report pp. 41-42 / PDF pp. 44-45.

### 7.2 Active-account reporting

The panel primarily includes accounts reported by creditors within the prior three months. Accounts that are closed, dormant, transferred, paid off, or no longer actively reported may be excluded. Some derogatory accounts can stop updating, so payment-performance profiles may partly reflect creditor reporting practices.

**Sources:** Data Dictionary, report pp. 41-43 / PDF pp. 44-46.

### 7.3 Per-capita and joint-account treatment

- Per-capita figures divide sample totals by the number of people in the sample and therefore apply to people with credit reports.
- For individual-level balance calculations, 50% of a joint-account balance is attributed to each individual to reduce double counting.

**Source:** Data Dictionary, report p. 41 / PDF p. 44.

### 7.4 Key definitions

- **Total debt balance:** Balance across included accounts, excluding balances in bankruptcy.
- **90+ days late / seriously delinquent:** Balances 90 days late, 120+ days late, or severely derogatory.
- **Severely derogatory:** Includes reports such as repossession, charge-off, or foreclosure in combination with delinquency status.
- **New foreclosure:** An individual with a foreclosure notation first appearing during the prior three months. This is not the same as a count of foreclosed properties. Joint borrowers can both be counted.
- **New bankruptcy:** A bankruptcy first reported during the prior three months.
- **Transition rate:** Newly delinquent or seriously delinquent balance divided by the prior quarter's balance that was not already in that status.
- **Third-party collections:** Collections handled by an entity other than the original creditor and present within the prior 12 months.

**Sources:** Data Dictionary, report pp. 42-43 / PDF pp. 45-46.

### 7.5 What the report does not directly provide

The report does not directly provide:

- Household debt-service payments relative to income.
- Borrower income, employment status, liquid assets, or home equity.
- Bank charge-offs, provisions, recovery rates, or ultimate lender losses.
- Loan vintage, interest rate, monthly payment, or debt-to-income distribution by product.
- A complete all-state ranking or county-level results.
- A causal explanation for changes in balances or delinquency.

These items require other datasets and should not be inferred from this report alone.

## 8. LLM ingestion guardrails

1. Do not compare the 2026 Q1 mortgage and auto credit-score charts directly with prior quarters without acknowledging the switch to VantageScore 4.0.
2. Keep **balance levels**, **stock delinquency**, and **transition rates** as separate measures.
3. Keep quarterly event counts separate from charts reported as a **four-quarter moving sum**.
4. Treat Florida and other state values as selected-state chart comparisons, not comprehensive national rankings.
5. Treat chart-estimated values as approximate and preserve the original report as the authority for visual verification.
6. Do not equate a new foreclosure notation with a property foreclosure count.
7. Do not infer bank losses, debt-service burden, or household income from credit-report balances alone.
