Gasoline Prices and Expected Consumer Spending 
Joanne Hsu, PhD, Director   

October 9, 2026 

Over the last five or so years, the cost of living has been the dominant factor driving consumer views of the economy. The 
surge in fuel prices stemming from the ongoing conflict in the Middle East that began February 28, 2026 placed even more 
pressure on consumers’ pocketbooks. According to AAA, in the months leading into the conflict, gas prices averaged about 
$2.93 per gallon. They rose above $4.50 in May, followed by an an early-summer moderation. Gas prices then reversed 
course, approaching $4.50 again in September. Against the backdrop of elevated gas prices, the surveys asked consumers 
about any changes in their purchasing attitudes and their anticipated future spending, particularly on items experiencing 
large price increases, between June 23 and September 21. This report compares these results to readings from 2022 near the 
height of post-pandemic inflation and 2025 in the wake of escalating tariff policy. Some measures were also collected in 
1979 and 1981 which provide historical context, though by then consumers had already adjusted to multiple successive 
episodes of high inflation.

The recovery from the post-pandemic surge in inflation was interrupted by the dramatic change in trade policy in 2025, 
which  fueled  a  spike  in  inflation  expectations,  as  seen  in  Figure  1.  Moreover,  the  share  of  consumers  spontaneously 
mentioning  tariffs  during  interviews  grew  progressively  between  November  2024  and  May  2025,  just  after  the  April 
announcement of a new reciprocal tariff schedule, and moderated somewhat in the months since. In 2026 following the start 
of the Iran conflict, the decline in tariff mentions has coincided with an increase in gasoline mentions.   

Inflation expectations followed a similar pattern: a sharp rise through May 2025, and a softening for the rest of the year, 
though  remaining  well  above  readings from  earlier  in  2024.  The improvements in  inflation  expectations  that  continued 
through the start of 2026 reversed after the start of the Iran conflict at the end of February and have remained elevated; they 
anticipate higher inflation for the forseeable future.   

Only about 31% of consumers expect to spend as usual in the year ahead on items that have large price increases (Table 
1). The remaining consumers report that they would reduce their spending on such items, either by cutting back or stopping 
their spending on such items altogether. These results are in between readings from the 2025 period, a volatile period in 
tariff policy (May through July), when only 25% expected to spend as usual, and the 37% seen in the 2022 period (August 
through October). A possible explanation is that the shock and uncertainty of 2025 tariff policy led many to expect rapid 
increases in inflation and deterioration in the economy that ultimately did not come to pass, and they have calibrated their 
response to recent inflation trends with that experience in mind. Note that for historical comparability, the questions asked 
specifically about goods that have large price increases, without reference to what those goods might be. Consumers could 
potentially reduce spending on some items without necessarily reducing their overall expenditures. In the current period, 
consumers may focus on gas prices and may not have much latitude to reduce driving for their commute, for example.

As seen in Table 2, expected spending responses vary across the population. Higher income consumers are much more 
likely to continue spending as usual than their lower-income counterparts, with similar patterns by terciles of stock wealth. 
These consumers generally have the resources to buffer against some price or income shocks. Indeed, higher income and 
higher wealth consumers expect to be less responsive to inflation now than they reported in last year. About half of the 
wealthiest stockholders expect to spend as usual, up substantially from 39% in 2025.     

Across political identification, a clear majority of each political group anticipates adjusting their spending. Still, partisan 
differences are visible; Republicans are less likely to expect reducing their spending than independents or Democrats. These 
patterns are consistent with historical patterns in which consumers tend to hold more favorable economic views when their 
party is in the White House. Critically, research has shown that partisanship patterns are seen in actual spending behavior 
(as well as portfolio choice, entrepreneurship, patents, tax evasion, and other economic behaviors), so partisan differences 
in these survey responses cannot be dismissed as mere political posturing unrelated to behavior.     

When asked about what purchases consumers anticipate cutting back or delaying the most, the most common responses 
included food/groceries; household items including durables; car buying, eating out, and vacations. Note that for food and 
groceries, consumers are likely substituting from one good for another, such as trading down from brand names to generics, 
rather than cutting back on total expenditures for food as a whole.

Consumers who expect to scale back their spending have lower sentiment and higher inflation expectations. 

Differences in anticipated spending responses reflect variation in underlying views and expectations for the economy, and 
not just differences in demographic characteristics or circumstances, as seen in Table 3. For the majority of consumers who 
expect to cut back some of their spending, sentiment and inflation expectations are squarely in between those who plan 
major changes spending and those who plan to spend as usual. The 16% who expect to stop spending on more expensive 
items reported extremely low sentiment, extremely high inflation expectations, and poor unemployment expectations. These 
expectations are largely stagflationary, with both higher prices as well as weakening income prospects weighing on their 
personal finances. In contrast, the 31% of consumers who expect to spend as usual exhibit far more favorable sentiment, 
strong labor market expectations, and modest expectations for gas price inflation and inflation more generally. These results 
reaffirm that consumers’ spending intentions are influenced by their confidence and expectations for the economy. Notably, 
most consumers expect gas prices to continue rising over the next year; these consumers appear to be skeptical about the 
possibility of a quick resolution to energy supply constraints. 

Consumers continue to be more reluctant to borrow for major purchases than to dissave.   

Table 4 displays responses to two questions on consumers’ willingness to use savings or debt to finance major purchases. 
The distaste for debt now closely resembles readings from 2022 as well as 1981, despite the very different historical contexts 
for both contemporaneous inflation and consumer credit markets. Consumers are somewhat more willing to use savings for 
major purchases now relative to 2022, but less so than in 1979 and 1981 when consumers had adjusted to a long period of 
high inflation. Interviews also reveal substantial heterogeneity by income and wealth. About 20% of consumers in the top 
tercile of incomes believe it is OK to use credit for this purpose, compared with only 8% of those in the bottom tercile.

Advance buying motives fell from tariff-induced heights in 2025, and are currently comparable to readings in 
2022-2023. These motives are much less widespread than seen in the 1970s and 80s.     

While making plans to cut back on future spending may be one way to deal with an expected inflationary environment, 
another strategy could be to pre-emptively make large purchases in order to avoid future price increases. Such advance 
buying  motives  can  turn  high  inflation  expectations  into  a  self-fulfilling  phenomenon,  as  intertemporal  substitution  via 
accelerated buying would create further upward pressure on inflation. Thus, these motives are an important component of 
inflationary psychology.

Critically, history has shown that large increases in advance buying motives have preceded realized surges in inflation by a 
couple of years, a sign that the impact of inflationary psychology may have substantial lags. Since the 1960s, the surveys 
have tracked  spontaneous mentions  of advance  buying  motives  when  consumers  are asked  about  buying  conditions for 
various large purchases. Figure 2 plots the share of consumers spontaneously referencing that it is a favorable time to buy 
to  avoid  future  price  increases  for  durable  goods.  In  the  1960s  and  70s,  sharp  increases  in  advance  buying  motives 
consistently preceded each resurgence of inflation. The full history (left panel of Figure 2) shows that recent levels of buy-
in-advance motives spiked in 2025 and ticked up recently, but remain substantially lower than late 1970s levels. 

The right panel of Figure 2 focuses on the last 14 years. Advance buying motives climbed steeply immediately following 
the 2024 election; these views manifested almost immediately with a surge in spending on durables and vehicles. These 
motives remained high through the April 2025 reciprocal tariff announcement and partial pause, then eased through to the 
end of the year. Similar trends were seen following the 2016 election and 2018 tariff hikes, as discussed in last year’s report. 

The start of the Iran conflict on February 28, 2026 ended the decline in advance buying motives. They have climbed 
modestly and are currently comparable to readings seen in 2022 and 2023. While they remain well below 2025, 
consumers are clearly primed to think about intertemporal substitution with spending. Coupled with the fact that historical 
periods of rising inflation were preceded by spikes in advance buying motives, it is likely that the risks of inflationary 
psychology have yet to pass.

Question text 

Next, there are several questions related to your reactions to recent trends in gas prices.   

If there were a major purchase that you wanted to make, do you think that now is a time when it would be O.K., to use some 
of your savings or is now a time when you would be especially reluctant to use some of your savings? 

If there were something big that you wanted to buy, do you think that now is a time when it would be O.K. for you to buy 
on credit, or is now a time when you would be especially reluctant to take on new debt? 

Should people buy large things such as houses, cars, appliances, or furniture before they are really needed to avoid higher 
prices in the future? 

In the next 12 months, will you (and your family living there) stop buying things which have had particularly large price 
increases, cut down your buying, or continue to buy as usual? 

What purchases will you (and your family living there) cut back or delay the most? [Open ended question]
