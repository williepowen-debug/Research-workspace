## Part C: Existing Methodologies Tracking Pre-Crisis Populations

### 1\. Insurance Industry (Actuarial Risk & Adverse Selection)

**Identifying At-Risk (Pre-Claim) Policyholders:** Insurers employ actuarial models and predictive analytics to flag insureds who are likely to file claims in the future[\[1\]](https://www.genre.com/us/knowledge/publications/2018/may/iinapc1804-en#:~:text=According%20to%20a%20recent%20report%2C,become%20an%20industry%20best%20practice)[\[2\]](https://www.genre.com/us/knowledge/publications/2018/may/iinapc1804-en#:~:text=Reported%20uses%20of%20analytics%20in,claims%20departments%20include). Actuaries analyze *risk factors* (e.g. age, prior claims, credit score, location) and even real-time data (telematics in auto insurance, biometric health data) to estimate an individual’s claim propensity. For example, health and life insurers often compute *risk scores* based on biometric metrics – high BMI, hypertension, high blood sugar, tobacco use – which are **“early warning”** indicators of future medical claims[\[3\]](https://www.alloyemployer.com/blog/biometric-red-flags-what-health-and-wellness-metrics-predict-higher-workers-compensation-risk#:~:text=What%20doesn%E2%80%99t%20always%20get%20said,on%20the%20workers%E2%80%99%20comp%20side). Similarly, auto insurers may use driving behavior and credit-based insurance scores to predict who is *at-risk but has not yet filed a claim*. These predictive models help underwriters adjust premiums or intervene with loss-prevention programs for high-risk customers.

**Metrics Signaling Elevated Future Claims:** Actuaries monitor key metrics that correlate with future losses. In property/casualty lines, a **history of “near-miss” incidents** (e.g. frequent braking events for drivers) or small claims can signal a greater probability of a large claim later. Actuarial studies in workers’ compensation have found that biometric risk factors (obesity, blood pressure, etc.) predict not only higher health claims but also **slower recovery and costlier injury claims** if an accident occurs[\[3\]](https://www.alloyemployer.com/blog/biometric-red-flags-what-health-and-wellness-metrics-predict-higher-workers-compensation-risk#:~:text=What%20doesn%E2%80%99t%20always%20get%20said,on%20the%20workers%E2%80%99%20comp%20side)[\[4\]](https://www.alloyemployer.com/blog/biometric-red-flags-what-health-and-wellness-metrics-predict-higher-workers-compensation-risk#:~:text=,from%20work%20when%20injuries%20happen). In auto insurance, metrics like high annual mileage, prior at-fault accidents, or DUI citations portend elevated claim odds. Actuaries quantify these as *risk scores* or **“expected loss costs”** per policy. They also track **loss ratios** and claim frequency by segment; an increasing loss ratio in a segment of policyholders (even if they haven’t filed claims yet) can be a red flag. Modern techniques include machine learning models that continuously score policyholders on the likelihood of imminent claims (for example, flagging a homeowner insured who suddenly makes several small maintenance claims might indicate a larger claim is looming).

**Adverse Selection Identification:** *Adverse selection* refers to high-risk individuals disproportionately obtaining coverage (and low-risk opting out). Insurers combat this by analyzing the composition of their risk pool versus the general population. Actuaries look for *imbalances* – e.g. if significantly more sick individuals are buying a health plan than expected, or if an auto insurer finds an influx of drivers from high-accident neighborhoods without offsetting premium, it suggests adverse selection. They use **experience studies** to compare actual claims to expected claims for cohorts. If certain cohorts consistently exceed expected losses, the insurer investigates whether underwriting criteria missed a risk factor. For instance, life insurers observed that smokers had much worse mortality experience, leading to smoker-distinct rates to prevent adverse selection[\[5\]](https://www.mutual-assurance.com/post/underinsured-homes#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20Insurance%20Information,by%20an%20average%20of%2027). In practice, companies maintain databases (like the Medical Information Bureau for life/health insurance) to spot applicants who shop for policies only when health is declining – a classic adverse selection behavior. Actuaries also compute an **“anti-selection cost”** – effectively the extra claims costs attributable to policyholder behaviors like waiting until needs are high to buy coverage[\[5\]](https://www.mutual-assurance.com/post/underinsured-homes#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20Insurance%20Information,by%20an%20average%20of%2027). To monitor this on an ongoing basis, insurers regularly review metrics like the ratio of claims incidence between those who recently purchased coverage and longer-tenured policyholders. A spike in early claims among new entrants could indicate that the at-risk (but not yet claiming) population has been drawn in (a sign of adverse selection). In sum, the insurance industry relies on *predictive risk modeling, risk scoring, and continual experience analysis* to find pockets of policyholders who are **“latent vulnerable”** – likely to claim soon – so they can adjust pricing or outreach accordingly[\[1\]](https://www.genre.com/us/knowledge/publications/2018/may/iinapc1804-en#:~:text=According%20to%20a%20recent%20report%2C,become%20an%20industry%20best%20practice)[\[2\]](https://www.genre.com/us/knowledge/publications/2018/may/iinapc1804-en#:~:text=Reported%20uses%20of%20analytics%20in,claims%20departments%20include).

### 2\. Credit Risk & Banking (Early Warning for Borrower Distress)

**“Thin File” and Credit-Invisible Borrowers:** Lenders have developed alternative scoring methods to assess consumers with little or no traditional credit history. Traditional FICO scores require credit accounts, so about 45 million Americans have no score or a “thin file.” To identify risk in this group, banks use **alternative data** like rent, utility, and telecom payment records, as well as cash-flow data from bank accounts[\[6\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=Payment%20histories%20from%20non,Telecommunications%20payment%20data%20also)[\[7\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=Transactional%20data%20offers%20another%20rich,Advanced). Research shows incorporating such data can considerably improve risk prediction – e.g. including on-time utility payments improved score accuracy by 15% for thin-file borrowers[\[6\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=Payment%20histories%20from%20non,Telecommunications%20payment%20data%20also). Some lenders use bespoke models that analyze deposit and expense patterns (average balance, overdraft frequency, income consistency) to infer a borrower’s financial cushion and likelihood of distress[\[8\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=liquidity%2C%20key%20predictors%20of%20repayment,that%20traditional%20models%20miss%20entirely). Fintech lenders often combine myriad signals (education, employment stability, phone bill payments) to create an “invisible” credit score. While these borrowers aren’t yet missing loan payments, **lack of history itself is a risk factor**; thus lenders monitor proxies like whether the customer has a savings buffer or multiple sources of debt.

**Behavioral Signals Before Missed Payments:** Modern credit risk management emphasizes *early warning indicators* that a borrower is struggling **before they actually default**. Lenders track changes in borrower behavior across their accounts. For example, a **“payment drift”** – a shift from paying a credit card in full to only making minimum payments – is a red flag of mounting financial stress[\[9\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=Early%20warning%20indicators%20can%20include%3A). Rising credit utilization (balances approaching limits) is another classic precursor to distress. Banks also monitor if a customer starts missing payments on *other* obligations (captured via credit bureau alerts) or if their direct deposit income pattern changes (e.g. a drop in paycheck amount or frequency could signal job loss). Even *customer engagement* can be telling: a borrower who stops logging into their account or responding to emails may be disengaging ahead of trouble[\[10\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=,minimum%20payments%20replacing%20full%20balances). **External data** feed in as well – new credit inquiries (seeking more debt), a change of address or employer, or even macroeconomic layoffs in their industry. When stitched together, these micro-changes create a *predictive risk map*. In fact, machine-learning models now flag accounts **30–90 days before delinquency** by finding patterns like increasing utilization \+ new inquiries \+ declining bank balances[\[11\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=captured%20through%20bureau%20updates%20or,linked%20data%20sources). For example, an analytics firm notes that a combination of rising overdraft frequency and a falling savings rate is an **“early warning”** of default risk well in advance of a missed loan payment[\[8\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=liquidity%2C%20key%20predictors%20of%20repayment,that%20traditional%20models%20miss%20entirely). Lenders also use specialized scores such as **“collection risk” or “roll rate” scores** that update monthly, anticipating which current accounts are likely to become 30-days past due in the next few cycles. These go beyond static FICO, which is more backward-looking[\[12\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=,Credit%20Risk%3A%20Signals%2C%20Not%20Scores). By monitoring these leading indicators, banks can intervene (e.g. offering hardship plans) before a borrower hits a crisis.

**Supplemental Risk Scoring Beyond FICO:** In recent years, credit providers have rolled out new scoring systems to quantify *resilience* or early risk that traditional scores miss. For instance, FICO itself introduced a **“Resilience Index”** to rank consumers’ ability to withstand economic shocks (it looks at how much unused credit and delinquencies they have, separate from the credit score). Lenders also use **custom “behavior scores”** on existing customers – these might incorporate checking account data, utility payments, and even metadata like how an application form was filled out[\[13\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=liquidity%2C%20key%20predictors%20of%20repayment,that%20traditional%20models%20miss%20entirely)[\[14\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=Beyond%20strictly%20financial%20data%2C%20digital,education%20and%20employment%20histories%20provide). The goal is to spot otherwise well-rated borrowers who are trending toward trouble. Studies find that financial health metrics – e.g. the ratio of monthly expenses to income, consistency of bill payments, presence of a savings cushion – can augur distress even when the person’s official credit score is still moderate[\[8\]](https://medium.com/@john_levonick/disenfranchising-fico-alternative-approaches-to-credit-risk-assessment-e8034e488ea8#:~:text=liquidity%2C%20key%20predictors%20of%20repayment,that%20traditional%20models%20miss%20entirely). In other words, someone might still have a 700 FICO score, but if their day-to-day bank transactions show **eroding cash flows and frequent overdrafts, it’s an early-warning score** downgrade internally. Some large banks maintain an internal **“early risk score”** updated in real-time that triggers proactive outreach. As a concrete example, one credit union’s system might flag a member who usually pays $500 on their credit card but has paid only $50 for two months *and* maxed out a retail card – prompting an early intervention call. In sum, the banking sector leverages alternative data and **continuous monitoring systems** (sometimes called *Early Warning Systems*) to track *pre-delinquency vulnerability* – borrowers who haven’t defaulted yet but exhibit patterns of increasing financial fragility[\[15\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=Traditional%20credit%20scoring%20still%20matters%2C,changes%20in%20consumer%20financial%20health)[\[11\]](https://www.optiosolutions.com/seeing-delinquency-before-it-happens-the-next-era-of-early-warning-credit-risk-models/#:~:text=captured%20through%20bureau%20updates%20or,linked%20data%20sources).

### 3\. Public Health Surveillance (Pre-Patient Vulnerability)

**Identifying Health-Vulnerable Populations:** Public health agencies like the CDC and HHS track communities and individuals who are at risk of poor health outcomes **before** they become patients. One major tool is composite indices of community vulnerability. For example, the **CDC’s Social Vulnerability Index (SVI)** uses 15–16 U.S. Census variables (poverty rates, crowded housing, lack of vehicle, etc.) to map which communities will struggle in a disaster or disease outbreak[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability). The SVI produces county and tract-level scores that highlight places where people are likely to defer care or have worse outcomes if stressed. Another is the **CDC/ATSDR Minority Health SVI**, which adds factors for minority and language barriers[\[17\]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rMDWgck1Fhw#:~:text=YouTube%20www,of%20Health%20and%20Human). These indices are essentially *pre-crisis maps*: health officials overlay them with data on hazards or flu outbreaks to prioritize support to high-vulnerability areas[\[18\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20Centers%20for%20Disease%20Control,economic%20loss%2C%20and%20health%20inequities)[\[19\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20CDC%2FATSDR%20SVI%20databases%20and,of%20property%2C%20illness%2C%20and%20death). Separately, HHS and academic groups maintain *health-specific vulnerability indices*. The **Community Health Vulnerability Index** (in some states) or the **Area Deprivation Index (ADI)** ranks neighborhoods by socioeconomic deprivation – correlating strongly with delays in care and hospitalizations. Public health surveillance also identifies at-risk individuals through surveys. The **National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)** is fielded continuously by CDC to monitor insurance status and health access. It asks Americans if they have forgone care. For instance, NHIS data can show what percent of people *delay medical care due to cost* in a given year – a direct measure of a *latent vulnerable* group. Analysts then cross-tabulate this by age, income, region, etc., to locate populations that aren’t patients now but likely will be due to pent-up needs. In 2022, about **5% of U.S. adults reported delaying care due to cost** (and higher percentages reported other access barriers)[\[20\]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339137967_Under_the_ACA_Higher_Deductibles_and_Medical_Debt_Cause_Those_Most_Vulnerable_to_Defer_Needed_Care#:~:text=Under%20the%20ACA%20Higher%20Deductibles,HD%29%20insurance%2C), with much higher rates among the uninsured. Health agencies treat those who defer needed care as an at-risk population to watch. They also use **surveillance of risk behaviors** (smoking rates, obesity rates by area) as proxies for future disease burden – e.g. counties with rising obesity might be flagged for future diabetes interventions. In summary, public health identifies “pre-patient” vulnerability through **composite indices (SVI, ADI)** to flag susceptible communities, and through **surveys tracking underinsurance and unmet needs**, which reveal people likely to enter distress if one health incident occurs.

**Measuring Community Vulnerability:** Several public indices measure community-level health vulnerability. In addition to CDC’s SVI (focused on disaster-health readiness), the **Health Resources & Services Administration (HRSA)** compiles a **“Social Vulnerability Index for COVID-19”** and other purposes, incorporating factors like healthcare access. The **County Health Rankings** (an annual project by Robert Wood Johnson Foundation) rank every county on health factors (economic, clinical, social) which can be viewed as a vulnerability index – counties scoring poorly have populations with worse baseline health and less capacity to cope before becoming sick. These indices are updated regularly (often annually) and give public health officials a moving picture of where latent health risks are highest. For example, a county with a high “Housing Stress” and “Uninsured rate” will score high on vulnerability, indicating a large pool of people one step away from health crisis. Another specialized metric is the **“Deferred Care Rate.”** CDC’s surveys (NHIS) and others like the Commonwealth Fund’s Biennial Health Insurance Survey estimate what fraction of people **skip care due to cost** in a year – effectively sizing the population that is at-risk medically. As of 2023, Commonwealth Fund data show nearly **29% of working-age adults were underinsured (had insurance but still deferred care or had medical bill problems)**[\[21\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=Toplines), and nearly 1 in 3 people with chronic conditions skimped on medicine due to cost. These kinds of statistics are used as ongoing indicators of latent health vulnerability. Public health agencies monitor them year to year (e.g. if the rate of cost-related deferred care starts climbing, it warns that a larger wave of health crises may be brewing among those not currently in treatment).

**“Deferred Care” Population Monitoring:** The healthcare system also tries to track those who need care but aren’t receiving it – as they are one incident away from acute distress. Besides surveys, another method is **claims analysis** for preventive services. For instance, insurers and the CDC can calculate how many people *should* be getting a cancer screening or diabetes check versus how many actually did – those missing preventative care are flagged as a pre-crisis group (likely to show up later with advanced illness). The federal government’s **Healthy People 2030** initiative includes metrics for “unmet medical need” and aims to reduce them. Moreover, agencies like CMS analyze data on prescription fills – a drop in prescription adherence might indicate people deferring medications (often for cost reasons), signaling vulnerability. One concrete example: the **CDC’s Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)** (a state-based survey) asks if people have a personal doctor and if they couldn’t see a doctor due to cost in the past year. BRFSS data are used to create state vulnerability profiles; e.g., a state where 15% report cost-barriers to care is considered to have a larger latent vulnerable population than a state at 8%. These data are updated annually, allowing officials to monitor trends in deferred care. In summary, public health surveillance defines at-risk-but-not-yet-patient groups via **indices (SVI and similar), tracking of insurance gaps, and unmet needs surveys**. They regularly publish reports (quarterly or annual updates) on these indicators, which serve as an early warning system for the healthcare safety net[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability)[\[21\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=Toplines).

### 4\. Disaster Preparedness (Populations Financially Vulnerable to Disasters)

**FEMA’s Modeling of Vulnerable Populations:** Emergency management agencies, especially FEMA, identify communities that are *socially or financially vulnerable* **before** disasters strike. FEMA integrates social vulnerability metrics into its planning models. For instance, FEMA’s **National Risk Index** combines hazard exposure with community vulnerability indicators (poverty, housing stock, etc.) to pinpoint areas where a natural disaster would likely trigger outsized financial losses for residents[\[22\]](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/products-tools/national-risk-index#:~:text=It%20offers%20a%20baseline%20risk,Census%20tract%20or%20county)[\[23\]](https://www.napsgfoundation.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/JRozelle_National-Risk-Index_20181204.pdf#:~:text=2010%29%3A). This index produces a **composite risk score** for each U.S. county, incorporating expected annual disaster losses and a **Social Vulnerability component** (largely derived from the CDC/ATSDR SVI)[\[22\]](https://www.fema.gov/flood-maps/products-tools/national-risk-index#:~:text=It%20offers%20a%20baseline%20risk,Census%20tract%20or%20county). Essentially, FEMA overlays the probability of hazards (floods, hurricanes, etc.) with how fragile the population’s socioeconomic status is. A community with high flood risk and high poverty will rank as extremely vulnerable to financial catastrophe from a flood. These models allow FEMA to identify “latent vulnerable” groups – for example, mobile-home park residents in a tornado zone, or low-income seniors in a hurricane zone – who are not in crisis now but would be hit hardest by an event.

**Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) in Disasters:** The **Social Vulnerability Index** is a cornerstone methodology here. Developed originally for disaster management, the SVI scores communities on 15+ factors that influence resilience (income, minority status, English proficiency, single-parent households, etc.)[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability). FEMA and CDC use the SVI to map which neighborhoods will likely need the most help *before and after* disasters[\[18\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20Centers%20for%20Disease%20Control,economic%20loss%2C%20and%20health%20inequities). For example, FEMA might identify a coastal county that, based on SVI, has many car-less households and high poverty; they know that if a hurricane comes, evacuation and recovery will be especially difficult there. Such a population is *at-risk-but-not-yet-distressed* financially – a hurricane could push many households into sudden financial crisis (needing shelters, unable to rebuild homes, etc.). FEMA’s planning documents explicitly incorporate SVI scores to direct preparedness resources (shelters, emergency funds) **ahead of time** to those vulnerable zones[\[19\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20CDC%2FATSDR%20SVI%20databases%20and,of%20property%2C%20illness%2C%20and%20death).

**Financial Vulnerability Overlays:** Beyond social factors, disaster planners also consider **insurance coverage gaps** and financial buffers in at-risk areas. One way they do this is by examining the **insurance penetration rate** in hazard-prone regions. For instance, FEMA and insurance regulators look at flood zone data: on average, only **30% of homes in high-risk flood areas carry flood insurance**[\[24\]](https://www.iii.org/article/spotlight-on-flood-insurance#:~:text=found%20that%2012%20to%2014,at%20the%20University%20of%20Pennsylvania). That means 70% are financially exposed – a population latently vulnerable to ruin if a flood hits. Such stats serve as an overlay on FEMA’s risk maps: if a coastal county not only has high storm surge risk but also a very low take-up of flood insurance, FEMA knows that an event there will translate to massive out-of-pocket losses for residents. Similarly, in wildfire zones, officials track how many homeowners have only the bare minimum coverage or have been dropped by insurers. **FAIR Plans** (state-run last-resort insurance) are a barometer here: a surge in FAIR Plan policies signals that the regular market has pulled back, often leaving homeowners under-insured. For example, California’s FAIR Plan policy count nearly doubled from covering \~1.6% to about **3% of all home policies (2018–2021)** as private insurers non-renewed fire-zone homes[\[25\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=almost%20two,the%20end%20of%20this%20Fact)[\[26\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20As%20of%20the%20end,residential%20insurance%20policies%20in%20California). This trend told officials that a growing segment of residents could not obtain adequate insurance – effectively one spark away from financial crisis. Likewise in Florida, the state “Citizens” insurer of last resort swelled to **1.4 million policies in 2023** (becoming the largest home insurer in the state) due to private insurers leaving – indicating a huge vulnerable population if a hurricane struck[\[27\]](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/catastrophe/florida-regulators-approve-transfer-of-90000-policies-from-citizens-to-private-insurers-562783.aspx#:~:text=The%20pace%20of%20decline%20has,Florida%20Association%20of%20Insurance%20Agents). Emergency planners track these metrics (residual market counts, uninsured rates) annually or even quarterly. They use them to model worst-case scenarios: e.g., FEMA might model how many households would likely request federal aid if a Category 4 hurricane hit Miami, given X% lack insurance or savings.

**Social Vulnerability Index Construction:** Because the user specifically asked: *What is the SVI and how is it constructed?* – The **SVI** is built from U.S. Census American Community Survey data (5-year estimates). The current version uses **16 variables grouped into 4 themes**: (1) Socioeconomic (poverty, unemployment, income, education), (2) Household Composition (age \<17 or \>65, disability, single-parent households), (3) Minority Status & Language (minority and English proficiency), and (4) Housing/Transportation (multi-unit structures, mobile homes, crowding, no vehicle, group quarters)[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability). Each census tract gets a percentile rank for each theme and an overall SVI score (higher \= more vulnerable)[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability). These scores are updated after each Census/ACS cycle (generally every few years; recent updates in 2014, 2018, 2020 data)[\[28\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20CDC%2FATSDR%20SVI%20has%20changed,seen%20in%20the%20timeline%20below). Planners use SVI maps to identify hotspots of vulnerability so they can **prepare financial mitigation** – for example, prioritizing those areas for flood-proofing grants or pre-storm evacuations.

In summary, disaster preparedness professionals use **integrated methodologies**: *hazard models plus social indices plus insurance data*. They maintain public indices like SVI (for social fragility) and publish tools like FEMA’s National Risk Index (which is updated as new data come in, currently covering 18 natural hazards with socio-economic overlays). These help continuously monitor which populations are *one disaster away* from financial collapse. A key insight from these models is that the severity of a disaster’s impact depends not just on the hazard, but on the pre-existing vulnerability – so those at-risk-but-not-yet-distressed groups are identified well in advance by *tracking social vulnerability scores, insurance gaps,* and other resilience measures on an ongoing basis[\[24\]](https://www.iii.org/article/spotlight-on-flood-insurance#:~:text=found%20that%2012%20to%2014,at%20the%20University%20of%20Pennsylvania)[\[16\]](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/place-health/php/svi/index.html#:~:text=The%20current%20CDC%2FATSDR%20Social%20Vulnerability,measure%20of%20overall%20social%20vulnerability).

### 5\. Poverty & Economic Research (Financial Fragility Metrics)

**Asset Poverty & Liquid Asset Poverty:** Researchers and anti-poverty organizations have defined metrics to capture households who are above the official poverty line but lack the assets to withstand a shock. **“Asset poverty”** is commonly defined as a household not having enough net worth to live at the federal poverty level for three months if their income is lost[\[29\]](https://www.nhpr.org/post/new-poverty-definition-liquid-asset-poverty#:~:text=The%20Corporation%E2%80%99s%20Andrea%20Levere). In dollar terms, this threshold is only a few thousand dollars for a family – so a family may have earnings above poverty, yet if they have minimal savings, they are in asset poverty. **“Liquid asset poverty”** is an even sharper measure, counting only liquid assets (cash, checking/savings, etc., not home equity). If you couldn’t survive 3 months at poverty-level on just your liquid assets, you are liquid-asset poor[\[29\]](https://www.nhpr.org/post/new-poverty-definition-liquid-asset-poverty#:~:text=The%20Corporation%E2%80%99s%20Andrea%20Levere). These indicators directly quantify the *latent vulnerability* of seemingly stable households. In 2019, about **40% of U.S. households were liquid-asset poor**, meaning almost half the country would be plunged into hardship if they missed a few paychecks[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). Even in the strong pre-pandemic economy, this share was high, and it’s often higher for certain demographics (e.g. \~60% of Black and Latino households, per Prosperity Now data)[\[31\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=A%20substantial%20racial%20gap%20persists,income%20poverty%20as%20white%20households). Researchers track these figures over time (using data from the Federal Reserve’s Survey of Consumer Finances and other sources) as an index of financial precarity. For example, the **Prosperity Now Scorecard** is an annual report that compiles asset poverty rates nationally and by state, showing trends and disparities[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). Asset poverty rates are updated whenever new wealth data come out (the Fed’s survey is triennial, but some proxies like the Census SIPP provide more frequent updates). Such measures let researchers see if Americans are becoming more resilient or more vulnerable: a decline in liquid-asset poverty would mean more families have savings buffers, whereas a rise (as happened after the Great Recession) signals growing latent vulnerability.

**Longitudinal Studies of Transition to Distress:** A number of longitudinal studies help track how people move from “struggling” to fully “distressed.” The **Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID)** – a survey tracking families over decades – has been used to observe life events that push households into poverty or financial crisis. For instance, one long-term finding is that a majority of Americans will experience at least one year of significant economic hardship by their 60s, even if they are not poor the whole time[\[32\]](https://csd.wustl.edu/10-25/#:~:text=Estimating%20the%20Life%20Course%20Dynamics,financial%20wealth%2C%20and%20liquid%20wealth). This implies a large swath of the population hovers near vulnerability and then falls into distress when events like job loss or illness occur. *Prospective* cohort studies, such as the **U.S. Financial Diaries project**, have closely followed lower-income families month-to-month and documented how an untimely expense or income dip triggers cascades of distress (e.g. one family’s hours were cut and within two months they missed rent and utilities – illustrating how quickly “struggling” becomes “distressed”). While such studies are often qualitative, on the quantitative side the **Survey of Income and Program Participation (SIPP)**, a longitudinal federal survey, tracks households’ income and wealth quarterly. Analysts use SIPP to calculate how many people fall below certain savings thresholds or fall behind on bills over a multiyear period. These longitudinal datasets allow creation of **transition rates** – e.g., the percentage of “financially coping” households in 2018 that had fallen into outright financial crisis by 2020\. Results show that many households cycle in and out of hardship. One study using PSID found that the *vast majority* of Americans in their 20s and 30s will experience asset poverty at some point, even if temporarily[\[32\]](https://csd.wustl.edu/10-25/#:~:text=Estimating%20the%20Life%20Course%20Dynamics,financial%20wealth%2C%20and%20liquid%20wealth). This highlights that a large “at-risk” population churns just above the poverty or insolvency line, and longitudinal research is crucial to identifying the risk factors (like lack of health insurance, single parenthood, etc.) that predict who will tip into distress. Policymakers monitor such studies (often published in economic journals or think-tank reports) to inform early interventions.

**Composite Financial Vulnerability Indices:** In recent years, institutions have created **composite indices** to summarize consumer financial health by combining multiple indicators (income, savings, debt, etc.). One example is the **Financial Health Network’s Financial Health Score**, which classifies people as Financially Healthy, Coping, or Vulnerable based on an 8-question survey (covering savings habits, debt loads, bill-paying, insurance coverage, etc.). According to their 2023 report, only **30% of households were financially healthy**, while 55% were coping and **17% outright vulnerable** – levels of vulnerability that had returned to pre-pandemic highs[\[33\]](https://finhealthnetwork.org/financial-health-pulse-2023-share-of-financially-vulnerable-americans-grows-to-pre-pandemic-levels/#:~:text=Financial%20Health%20Pulse%202023%3A%20Share,seen%20since%20before%20the%20pandemic). This score is updated annually via surveys and provides a single measure to monitor over time; an increase in the “vulnerable” share signals more households on the brink of crisis[\[34\]](https://finhealthnetwork.org/financial-health-pulse-2023-share-of-financially-vulnerable-americans-grows-to-pre-pandemic-levels/#:~:text=Financial%20Health%20Pulse%202023%3A%20Share,seen%20since%20before%20the%20pandemic). Similarly, the U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) developed a **Financial Well-Being Index** (scored 0–100 from a survey tool). The average American scores around 55; CFPB reports track what percentage fall below a threshold (indicating high financial fragility). Trends in this index can reveal growing latent financial stress even before concrete outcomes (like defaults or bankruptcies) occur. For instance, if the average score in a region drops over a year, it suggests more people are feeling insecure about meeting expenses or have less confidence in their finances – a warning sign of potential distress rises. Another composite measure is the **United Way ALICE metric** (Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed), which isn’t a single index number but defines the population above poverty but still unable to afford basic living costs. United Ways in each state report the percentage of households below the ALICE threshold (often 30–40% of households in a state). That essentially flags the working poor who are not counted as officially poor but are one emergency away from it. These composite and multi-factor indices are typically updated on an **annual basis** and widely cited. They combine data on income, cost of living, assets, and sometimes credit or bill-paying behavior to give a more **holistic vulnerability score**.

**Studies on “Struggling to Distressed” Pathways:** Academic research also directly examines the progression into distress. For example, the **Urban Institute** and others have done panel studies on families who were “struggling” (say, had trouble paying some bills) and how many became fully “distressed” (unable to pay major expenses, in collections) over time. They often find that specific triggers like a job layoff or a health shock are the tipping points. Having certain buffers (education, savings, family support) can prevent the slide. These studies underscore the importance of monitoring not just static snapshots of poverty, but dynamic indicators like **delinquency precursors** (e.g. number of households 30-days late on any bill – which is often reported in credit bureau trend data). **Composite vulnerability indices** attempt to incorporate multiple risk factors – for instance, one might combine unemployment risk, lack of health insurance, and low savings into a single “financial vulnerability score” for a demographic group. Think tanks sometimes publish such composite rankings by city or demographic. One concrete composite is the **CFPB’s Making Ends Meet report**, which looks at combinations of debt-to-income, payment history, and self-reported hardship to segment consumers into risk tiers.

In summary, poverty and economic researchers track “at-risk but not yet in crisis” populations with **measures of asset insufficiency (asset and liquid-asset poverty rates)**, **longitudinal panel data (to see who falls into distress over time)**, and **composite indices of financial health**. These are updated regularly (many annually, some continuously via panel surveys). They provide an ongoing **infrastructure of data** to monitor the financial fragility of US consumers. For example, if liquid asset poverty rates stop improving or worsen, or if a financial health index score drops, these are alerts that more households are one incident away from financial turmoil[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership)[\[35\]](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/seven-tenths-of-households-are-in-poor-financial-health/257353#:~:text=Seven,American%20households%20remain%20financially%20unhealthy).

## Part D: Data Sources for Ongoing Monitoring of Vulnerability

Below are key data sources (with details) for tracking the above categories of latent vulnerability. Each source includes the publishing entity, update frequency, available time series, limitations, and a URL or citation for direct access.

### 1\. **Health Vulnerability Data Sources**

* **National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)** – *Publisher:* CDC’s National Center for Health Statistics. *Frequency:* Annual data release (continuous survey with quarterly early estimates). *Time series depth:* Since 1957, with consistent annual data on insurance coverage, healthcare access, and affordability. *What it measures:* Rates of uninsurance, underinsurance, and **deferred or forgone medical care** (e.g. % of people who delayed care due to cost). *Use:* Serves as a primary gauge of health vulnerability – e.g., NHIS 2022 data show **10.6% of adults couldn’t get needed care when needed** (various reasons)[\[36\]](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr207.pdf#:~:text=sociodemographic%20characteristics,6). *Limitations:* Self-reported data; some questions (like “couldn’t afford care”) have changed wording over time, and state-level data is not directly provided (NHIS is national/regional). *Access:* [CDC NHIS Data & Reports](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/) – annual microdata and reports are available on the CDC website[\[37\]](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr207.pdf#:~:text=Methods%E2%80%94Data%20from%20the%202022%20National,6)[\[36\]](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhsr/nhsr207.pdf#:~:text=sociodemographic%20characteristics,6).

* **Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS)** – *Publisher:* Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). *Frequency:* Annual data (with two-year overlapping panels of households). *Time series:* 1996–present (previously the National Medical Care Expenditure Surveys in 1977 and 1987\)[\[38\]](https://odphp.health.gov/healthypeople/objectives-and-data/data-sources-and-methods/data-sources/medical-expenditure-panel-meps#:~:text=Medical%20Expenditure%20Panel%20,MEPS). *What it measures:* Detailed healthcare utilization and spending, including **out-of-pocket (OOP) spending**, medical debt, insurance status, and underinsurance. MEPS can track trends in **underinsurance** (e.g. what % of families have OOP \>10% of income) and **medical debt levels**. *Use:* Valuable for monitoring **medical financial vulnerability** – for instance, MEPS data can show the share of Americans with high deductible burdens or who couldn’t fill prescriptions due to cost. *Limitations:* Sample size (\~30,000 individuals) can limit state-level analysis; data is released with a 6–12 month lag and some imputation for expenditures. *Access:* MEPS data files and summary tables are on [meps.ahrq.gov](https://meps.ahrq.gov)[\[39\]](https://www.census.gov/econ/overview/mu0900.html#:~:text=Medical%20Expenditure%20Panel%20Survey%20,gov) (public use files for expenditure, insurance, etc., updated annually).

* **Commonwealth Fund Biennial Health Insurance Survey** – *Publisher:* The Commonwealth Fund (non-profit foundation). *Frequency:* Every 2 years (e.g. 2022, 2024 surveys)[\[40\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=November%2021%2C%202024). *Time series:* Conducted since 2001, allowing tracking of underinsurance and access problems over two decades. *What it measures:* Rates of **underinsurance** (defined as having insurance all year but still having high medical costs relative to income), inability to afford care, medical bill problems, etc. For example, the 2024 survey found **nearly 1 in 4 adults were underinsured** and a large segment skipped care due to cost[\[21\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=Toplines). *Use:* Provides granular insight into the “at-risk insured” – those one illness away from financial crisis. Often reports by age, income, and state. *Limitations:* Survey of \~4,000 adults via phone/web – subject to sampling error; self-reported data on bills. Only every two years, so less frequent than federal surveys. *Access:* Survey findings are published in reports on [CommonwealthFund.org](https://www.commonwealthfund.org) (with chartpacks and microdata usually available on request)[\[21\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=Toplines).

* **Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) Health Tracking Poll & Data** – *Publisher:* KFF (non-profit health policy org). *Frequency:* Monthly tracking polls on health issues; also annual reports. *Time series:* Ongoing since mid-2000s for various indicators. *What it measures:* Public’s ability to afford healthcare, medical bill problems, insurance coverage trends. KFF also analyzes federal data (Census, NHIS) to produce up-to-date statistics. For example, KFF’s analysis of 2023 Census data reported **26 million Americans (8%) uninsured**[\[41\]](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/surveys/2024/nov/state-health-insurance-coverage-us-2024-biennial-survey#:~:text=An%20estimated%2026%20million%20Americans%2C,school%20or%20college%2C%20and%20when), and their Health System Tracker (with Peterson Foundation) monitors **delay of care** and **difficulty paying medical bills** continuously[\[42\]](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/health-policy-101-the-uninsured-population-and-health-coverage/#:~:text=In%202023%2C%20uninsured%20adults%20were,to%20publicly%20insured%20adults)[\[43\]](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/indicator/access-affordability/percent-uninsured/#:~:text=Tracker%20www,having%20difficulty%20paying%20medical%20bills). *Use:* Timely pulse of health care affordability – e.g. tracking the % who say they or a family member skipped treatment in the past year due to cost. *Limitations:* Poll data is self-reported and sample sizes \~1,200 per month; question wording can change. KFF’s curated indicators rely on underlying sources like NHIS/MEPS for rigorous time series. *Access:* KFF’s Health System Tracker ([healthsystemtracker.org](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/)) provides interactive charts on access/affordability (e.g. rate of cost-related delayed care, updated with new survey data)[\[42\]](https://www.kff.org/uninsured/health-policy-101-the-uninsured-population-and-health-coverage/#:~:text=In%202023%2C%20uninsured%20adults%20were,to%20publicly%20insured%20adults). KFF issue briefs (on kff.org) regularly compile these stats, with links to data.

* **CFPB Consumer Complaint Database (Medical Debt)** – *Publisher:* U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. *Frequency:* Updated daily (each complaint is added in near real-time). *Time series:* Since 2011 for complaints; users can filter by year/product. *What it measures:* Complaints filed by consumers about financial products. Relevant here, one can filter to **“Medical debt collection”** complaints to gauge the volume of people reporting problems with medical bills. In 2023, about **11% of all debt collection complaints were about medical debt** – indicating persistent issues[\[44\]](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-protection-issues-in-medical-and-rental-debt-collection/#:~:text=Tens%20of%20millions%20of%20people,complaints%20received%20by%20the%20CFPB). The CFPB also publishes annual reports summarizing medical debt trends (e.g. noting $88 billion on credit reports as of 2022). *Use:* While not a representative survey, complaint data are an **early indicator** of financial stress related to healthcare – a spike in medical debt complaints could foreshadow rising medical bankruptcies. *Limitations:* It’s passive (requires people to complain), and not everyone knows to file a CFPB complaint. Data may be influenced by awareness campaigns (not purely volume of problems). No personal identifiers or detailed outcomes in the public data. *Access:* The public dashboard is at [consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/consumer-complaints/) – one can filter by product (medical debt, etc.) and download data. CFPB’s 2024 annual report on debt collection (press release [here](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-protection-issues-in-medical-and-rental-debt-collection/)) highlights key stats[\[44\]](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-protection-issues-in-medical-and-rental-debt-collection/#:~:text=Tens%20of%20millions%20of%20people,complaints%20received%20by%20the%20CFPB).

### 2\. **Insurance Coverage Gaps Data Sources**

* **National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) Reports** – *Publisher:* NAIC (regulatory support organization). *Frequency:* Annual reports and data compilations (with some metrics updated in special studies periodically). *Time series:* Varies by report; e.g. **Auto Insurance Database Report** is annual (with \~1–2 year lag in data); NAIC began tracking some metrics since the 1990s. *What it measures:* NAIC collects data on insurance coverage and markets from state regulators and insurers. Key for vulnerability: **uninsured motorist rates**, insurance affordability indices, residual market policy counts. For example, NAIC cites Insurance Research Council data that **15.4% of U.S. drivers were uninsured in 2023** (about 1 in 7\)[\[45\]](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/uninsured-motorists#:~:text=Overview%3A%20Despite%20laws%20that%20compel,continued%20to%20%20121%20steadily), with state rates from **5.7% (Maine) up to 28.2% (Mississippi) uninsured**[\[45\]](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/uninsured-motorists#:~:text=Overview%3A%20Despite%20laws%20that%20compel,continued%20to%20%20121%20steadily). NAIC also tracks underinsured motorists and publishes white papers on the issue[\[46\]\[47\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,2%20percent%20in%20Mississippi). *Use:* These stats are updated when new studies come out (the latest uninsured motorist figures were updated in 2025). They allow monitoring of **insurance gaps in auto coverage** over time – an important vulnerability indicator since uninsured drivers won’t have financial protection in a crash. *Limitations:* Some data (like detailed state-by-state tables) are in reports that may require purchase or regulator access. NAIC relies on sampling methods (like comparing claims frequencies) for uninsured estimates rather than direct counts. *Access:* NAIC’s website has topic pages (e.g. “Uninsured Motorists” topic) summarizing latest stats[\[45\]](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/uninsured-motorists#:~:text=Overview%3A%20Despite%20laws%20that%20compel,continued%20to%20%20121%20steadily) and pointing to reports. The **2021/2022 Auto Insurance Database Report** (released Jan 2025\) provides the most current data on auto insurance coverage and costs[\[48\]](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/uninsured-motorists#:~:text=markets%3B%20studies%20examining%20the%20use,data%20and%20auto%20insurance%20affordability)[\[49\]](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/uninsured-motorists#:~:text=Through%20the%20NAIC%2C%20state%20insurance,is%20now%20available).

* **Insurance Research Council (IRC) Studies** – *Publisher:* IRC (now part of The Institutes). *Frequency:* Periodic (approximately every 3–5 years) research studies. *Time series:* They have produced uninsured motorist estimates since the 1980s; the latest covers 2017–2023 with annual trend[\[46\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,2%20percent%20in%20Mississippi). *What it measures:* IRC specializes in auto insurance trends. Their **“Uninsured and Underinsured Motorists”** reports provide state and national estimates of uninsured driver rates and even the proportion of drivers carrying insufficient liability limits. The 2025 IRC report found **15.4% uninsured rate in 2023 and 18.0% of drivers underinsured** (liability coverage below needed levels)[\[46\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,2%20percent%20in%20Mississippi)[\[50\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,7%20percent%20in%20Colorado). It also highlights trends (e.g. a jump in uninsured rate in 2020 during the pandemic)[\[51\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,2%20percent%20in%20Mississippi). *Use:* This is a core data source for tracking **auto insurance vulnerability**. Rising uninsured rates indicate more people at financial risk from accidents. Underinsured rates point to people who have insurance but would face personal liability or insufficient payout if a serious accident occurs (a latent financial threat). *Limitations:* Full reports often behind paywall (IRC membership), but summary findings are released publicly. Updates are not annual – they depend on study cycles (the previous edition was a few years earlier). *Access:* Summary findings are often reported in industry press and on the IRC website. For instance, a **free research summary** of the latest report is available via The Institutes website[\[52\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=Countrywide%2C%20one%20out%20of%20every,uninsured%20or%20underinsured%20in%202023)[\[46\]](https://www.insurance-research.org/node/130#:~:text=,2%20percent%20in%20Mississippi), showing headline stats.

* **National Flood Insurance & Disaster Insurance Data** – *Publishers:* FEMA for National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) data; Insurance Information Institute (III) for compiled stats. *Frequency:* Annual figures (NFIP policy counts updated more frequently internally; key stats published in FEMA reports or Insurance Fact Books annually). *Time series:* NFIP data since 1968; readily available trends for the last couple decades. *What it measures:* **Insurance coverage gaps in disaster-prone areas**. For example, flood insurance “take-up rates” – the percent of properties in high-risk flood zones that carry flood insurance. A recent analysis by Wharton Risk Center found only **\~30% of homes in 100-year flood zones have flood coverage** on average[\[24\]](https://www.iii.org/article/spotlight-on-flood-insurance#:~:text=found%20that%2012%20to%2014,at%20the%20University%20of%20Pennsylvania). Some states (inland) have \<10% coverage in floodplains. Similarly, data on earthquake insurance take-up in California (\~10% of homeowners have it) can be tracked. *Use:* These metrics quantify **uninsured property risk** – if the majority of homes in a hurricane zone lack proper coverage, those homeowners form a latent vulnerable population for financial ruin after a disaster. Tracking changes in take-up (e.g. did more people buy flood insurance after a major hurricane?) can show if vulnerability is rising or falling. *Limitations:* Data is fragmented: NFIP publishes total policy counts and premium volumes, but granular take-up rates require combining FEMA flood zone maps with policy data (often done in research studies). Insurance III and NAIC sometimes publish approximate state-level uninsured disaster exposure stats, but not on a high-frequency basis. *Access:* FEMA’s **Insurance Statistics** page and annual **NFIP actuarial reports** give raw numbers (policies in force, coverage amounts by state). The Insurance Information Institute’s *“Facts \+ Statistics: Flood Insurance”* page compiles some stats and cites external research[\[24\]](https://www.iii.org/article/spotlight-on-flood-insurance#:~:text=found%20that%2012%20to%2014,at%20the%20University%20of%20Pennsylvania). The CDC SVI site and FEMA’s Risk Index also list vulnerability measures related to insurance (the Risk Index’s **Community Resilience** component considers insurance coverage as a factor).

* **State Insurance Department Disclosures (FAIR Plans, etc.)** – *Publishers:* Individual state insurance regulators or residual market entities. *Frequency:* Annual reports or legislative reports (varies by state; some provide quarterly updates on residual market). *Time series:* Varies, but e.g. the California FAIR Plan has data going back decades (with current focus on year-to-year since 2015), Florida’s Citizens provides monthly policy counts online. *What it measures:* **Coverage gaps in the private market** – number of policies in state-run last-resort insurance pools like FAIR Plans (for home fire/wind) and state auto insurance plans. For instance, the **California FAIR Plan** reported about **3% of California home insurance policies were FAIR Plan by 2021**, up from \~1.6% in 2018[\[25\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=almost%20two,the%20end%20of%20this%20Fact)[\[26\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20As%20of%20the%20end,residential%20insurance%20policies%20in%20California) (reflecting tens of thousands of high-risk homes that lost private coverage). Florida’s Citizens Property Insurance Corp had **1.407 million policies at peak in Sept 2023** before depopulation efforts began[\[27\]](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/catastrophe/florida-regulators-approve-transfer-of-90000-policies-from-citizens-to-private-insurers-562783.aspx#:~:text=The%20pace%20of%20decline%20has,Florida%20Association%20of%20Insurance%20Agents). These counts indicate how many households can’t find adequate coverage in the voluntary market – a sign of vulnerability to disaster losses or premium shocks. *Use:* Monitoring these residual markets over time shows where insurance gaps are widening. A rapidly growing FAIR Plan (like in wildfire areas) means more owners effectively uninsured for full value (FAIR Plans often cap coverage). Regulators often release **annual summaries** of these trends to inform policymakers. *Limitations:* Data is state-specific and not centralized. One must gather reports from each state’s FAIR Plan or residual market pool. Definitions vary (some count policies, others exposure value). And growth in residual markets can sometimes reflect affordability issues (people choose the cheaper bare-bones coverage). *Access:* State insurance department websites. E.g., the **California Dept. of Insurance Wildfire Data and Statistics** page publishes an annual *Fact Sheet* on homeowners insurance availability, including non-renewals and FAIR Plan policy counts[\[53\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=2,remains%20at%20an%20elevated%20level)[\[54\]](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/200-wrr/upload/CDI-Fact-Sheet-Residential-Insurance-Market-Policy-Count-Data-December-2022.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20The%20number%20of%20new,FAIR%20Plan%20that%20new%20policies). Florida Citizens Insurance posts quarterly statistics in board meeting documents and on its website (with a dashboard showing policy count trends). The **Insurance Information Institute (III)** also occasionally summarizes these in reports on disaster insurance markets.

* **Insurance Coverage Surveys (e.g. III Consumer Surveys)** – *Publisher:* Insurance Information Institute and other research groups. *Frequency:* Occasional (not regular), but worth noting. *What it measures:* Sometimes the III or other organizations conduct consumer polls on topics like **percentage of homeowners who have enough insurance** or who have specific coverages (umbrella liability, renters insurance, etc.). For instance, a survey might find *“X% of homeowners haven’t updated their coverage in 5+ years”*, implying underinsurance given rising construction costs. One often-cited stat (from insurance industry analyses and firms like CoreLogic): **60%+ of homes are underinsured by an average of 20-25%** of the amount needed to rebuild[\[55\]](https://www.mutualbenefitgroup.com/insurance-101/personal-insurance-101/insure-to-value#:~:text=Three%20out%20of%20five%20American,is%20keeping%20pace%20with%20inflation)[\[56\]](https://www.mutual-assurance.com/post/underinsured-homes#:~:text=According%20to%20the%20Insurance%20Information,by%20an%20average%20of%2027). This kind of data (while not updated yearly) highlights a latent vulnerability – even insured homeowners might be one disaster away from a large out-of-pocket loss due to insufficient coverage. *Access:* Press releases or white papers from insurance brokers (e.g., Marshall & Swift/CoreLogic studies, reported by III). Limitations are that these are infrequent and methodology may vary.

### 3\. **Savings and Emergency Buffer Data Sources**

* **Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances (SCF)** – *Publisher:* U.S. Federal Reserve Board. *Frequency:* Triennial (every 3 years; last published 2022 covering data through 2021). *Time series:* Available since 1983 (consistent methodology since 1989). *What it measures:* Detailed household balance sheets – assets, debts, net worth – for a representative sample (with oversamples of wealthy). Crucial metrics include **median liquid savings**, distribution of emergency fund sizes, retirement savings, etc. Analysts use SCF to calculate **asset poverty rates** and **liquid asset poverty rates** nationally and by demographics[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). For example, SCF data underlies the stat that \~40% of households have insufficient liquid assets for 3 months at poverty level[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). It also provides the **debt-to-income ratios**, which relate to vulnerability (households with very high leverage are one shock away from default). *Use:* The SCF is the gold-standard for measuring **financial buffers** and wealth trends over time. One can see if median savings are growing or if the bottom quartile has only say $400 in liquid assets (persistently). It’s used to inform on how many families could handle emergencies of $1k, $5k, etc. *Limitations:* Infrequent (3-year gaps); data release lags about 1 year after collection. Also, it’s cross-sectional (not tracking same family over time, though it gives overall trends). The wealthy oversample means it’s great for net worth distribution but for low-income specifics sometimes the sample is smaller (though still usable). *Access:* Fed Reserve publishes an official SCF report and public data files on [federalreserve.gov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/consumerscommunities/scf.htm). Also, the **SCF chartbook** provides quick stats (e.g. median transaction account balances). Many analyses (Urban Institute, Prosperity Now) use SCF data to report asset poverty after each wave[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership).

* **Federal Reserve “SHED” – Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking** (a.k.a. Economic Well-Being of Households survey) – *Publisher:* Federal Reserve Board. *Frequency:* Annual (typically conducted in fall, released in May of the following year). *Time series:* 2013–present annually. *What it measures:* Households’ self-reported financial well-being, ability to handle unexpected expenses, savings, and employment situation. This is the survey behind the famous **“$400 emergency” question** – e.g., in 2023, **63% of adults said they could cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or equivalent** (meaning 37% could not)[\[57\]](https://www.nrmlaonline.org/2025/05/29/federal-reserve-publishes-household-economic-well-being-report#:~:text=Federal%20Reserve%20Publishes%20Household%20Economic,or%20its%20equivalent%2C%20unchanged). It also asks how long people could sustain expenses if they lost their income (which directly measures how many are one paycheck away from hardship). *Use:* SHED is one of the most up-to-date barometers of **emergency savings adequacy**. Because it’s yearly, one can see changes (for instance, the share who could cover $400 improved from \~50% in 2013 to 68% in 2019, then dipped back to 63% in 2023\)[\[58\]](https://fedcommunities.org/economic-well-being-us-households-2023/#:~:text=Communities%20fedcommunities,doing%20okay%20or%20living%20comfortably). It also breaks results by income, region, etc. *Limitations:* It’s a survey (sample \~11,000 in 2023\) with modest response rates. $400 is a somewhat arbitrary threshold (the Fed chose it to gauge small emergency capability; they also ask about $1,000 in some years). People might mis-estimate what they could do (e.g. rely on credit cards, which is counted as “could handle it” in the survey if they say they’d put it on a card). Still, it’s a respected, straightforward trend source. *Access:* Full reports titled *“Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in {Year}”* are on [FederalReserve.gov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/consumerscommunities/shed.htm). The Fed also provides data downloads and an interactive web tool. For example, the 2023 report and data tables show the emergency expense findings[\[57\]](https://www.nrmlaonline.org/2025/05/29/federal-reserve-publishes-household-economic-well-being-report#:~:text=Federal%20Reserve%20Publishes%20Household%20Economic,or%20its%20equivalent%2C%20unchanged).

* **Bankrate Emergency Savings and Financial Security Surveys** – *Publisher:* Bankrate (private financial publisher) and similar organizations (e.g. CNBC, YouGov polls). *Frequency:* Typically annual (Bankrate releases an **Emergency Savings Report** each January). *Time series:* \~2014–present annually for some questions. *What it measures:* Poll-based metrics on Americans’ savings habits and readiness for emergencies. For example, Bankrate’s January 2026 survey found only **47% of Americans have enough savings to cover a $1,000 emergency** (meaning 53% would have to borrow or not cover it)[\[59\]](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/emergency-savings-report/#:~:text=Bankrate%27s%202026%20Annual%20Emergency%20Savings,a%20new%20Bankrate%20survey). Earlier results: 2023 found 43% could cover $1k (57% could not)[\[60\]](https://www.bankrate.com/f/102997/x/fca64133d1/2023-january-fsp-emergency-savings-press-release.pdf#:~:text=NEW%20YORK%20%E2%80%93%20January%2025th%2C,For%20the%20full%20analysis)[\[61\]](https://www.bankrate.com/f/102997/x/fca64133d1/2023-january-fsp-emergency-savings-press-release.pdf#:~:text=When%20asked%20how%20they%20would,they%20would%20reduce%20spending%2C%2011). They also ask if people increased or decreased savings, worries about paying bills, etc. Another common metric from these surveys: “Only 46% have savings to cover 3 months of expenses”[\[62\]](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/starting-an-emergency-fund/#:~:text=How%20to%20start%20,cover%20three%20months%20of%20expenses). *Use:* These provide **timely, media-friendly indicators** of financial fragility that are easy to communicate. Because they’re done yearly (and sometimes by other firms mid-year), they can capture trends – e.g., any improvement in Americans’ emergency funds. *Limitations:* As commercial polls, sample sizes (\~1,000) are smaller and methodology can vary. The specific phrasing (“how would you pay a $1,000 expense?” with options) can lead to different interpretations. Nonetheless, results have been fairly consistent with the Fed’s findings (just a larger expense threshold). They may also have respondent bias (people might answer aspirationally). *Access:* Press releases on Bankrate’s website (and coverage by news outlets like **Yahoo Finance** which often reprints the results). For instance, the **2023 Bankrate Emergency Savings Press Release**[\[61\]](https://www.bankrate.com/f/102997/x/fca64133d1/2023-january-fsp-emergency-savings-press-release.pdf#:~:text=When%20asked%20how%20they%20would,they%20would%20reduce%20spending%2C%2011) and the 2026 edition on Bankrate.com. These usually include the data and sometimes crosstabs by age/income.

* **U.S. Census Household Pulse Survey (select indicators)** – *Publisher:* U.S. Census Bureau (experimental survey). *Frequency:* Biweekly (since April 2020, ongoing in 2023–2024 in roughly monthly phases). *Time series:* 2020–present high-frequency time series. *What it measures:* The Pulse survey, launched during COVID, asks numerous questions about household financial security. Pertinent to savings/buffers: *“Before the pandemic, could your household cover a $400 emergency?”* and *“Currently, how confident are you could come up with $400?”* (these were asked in earlier phases). It also asks about difficulty paying usual household expenses in the past week – which serves as a near-real-time hardship indicator. *Use:* While primarily known for tracking pandemic impacts, Pulse continues to provide rapid insights into emergency preparedness of households. For example, in late 2021 it showed around **30–35% of adults had difficulty paying usual expenses** in the past week – a sign of financial strain. It’s useful for near real-time monitoring of economic vulnerability, especially since it’s broken down by state and metro. *Limitations:* As an experimental survey with online response, it may not be fully representative (weighting attempts to adjust). Questions can change or be added/removed over phases, limiting long-term comparability for some items. Still, it’s a rich, timely data source. *Access:* The data tool at [census.gov/pulse](https://www.census.gov/data/experimental-data-products/household-pulse-survey.html) allows custom tabulations. For example, one can retrieve the percent of households with **no emergency funds** or those feeling *“not confident”* about paying next month’s rent (which overlaps with employment vulnerability too). Census Pulse microdata are also downloadable for analysis.

* **JPMorgan Chase Institute – Cash Buffer Analysis** – *Publisher:* JPMorgan Chase Institute (research arm of Chase Bank). *Frequency:* Occasional studies (not a regular release, but multiple reports over years). *Time series:* Studies based on bank account data from 2013 onward; they often compare different years or pre/post situations. *What it measures:* Using transaction data of millions of Chase bank customers, they estimate how long households could maintain spending out of liquid assets if income stopped – essentially the **cash buffer days**. A well-known report found median households had about **\~3 weeks of take-home income in liquid savings**; low-income households had less than one week[\[63\]](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/financial-health-wealth-creation/how-vulnerable-are-americans-to-unexpected-expenses#:~:text=The%20vast%20majority%20of%20households,term%20credit). They also examine volatility – frequency of paycheck-to-paycheck balances. *Use:* This provides an empirical, behavior-based measure of emergency savings (as opposed to survey self-reports). It’s great for validating or refining the “one paycheck away” narrative. For example, if median buffer is 20 days, that aligns with about one paycheck cycle. The Institute updates these findings during special analyses (e.g. how buffers changed with stimulus payments in 2020). *Limitations:* Only covers customers of one bank (who may not be fully representative, although Chase is large and diverse). Not updated on a set schedule – more occasional white papers. *Access:* Reports are available on the [JPMorgan Chase Institute website](https://institute.jpmorganchase.com/). E.g., *“Weathering Volatility”* (2015) and follow-ups. Also, **Fed researchers and academics** sometimes cite these in aggregate, e.g., “most households can cover a $400 shock through some combination of cash, credit, etc.”[\[63\]](https://www.jpmorganchase.com/institute/all-topics/financial-health-wealth-creation/how-vulnerable-are-americans-to-unexpected-expenses#:~:text=The%20vast%20majority%20of%20households,term%20credit).

* **Prosperity Now Scorecard** (Assets & Opportunity Scorecard) – *Publisher:* Prosperity Now (formerly CFED). *Frequency:* Annual. *Time series:* Published annually since mid-2000s, with data mostly drawn from federal surveys (SCF, ACS, etc.) for consistency. *What it measures:* A comprehensive set of state-by-state indicators on financial health. Key ones: **Liquid Asset Poverty Rate**, **Asset Poverty Rate**, **Emergency Savings Rate** (percentage of households with at least 3 months expenses in savings), etc. For 2019, they reported **40% liquid asset poverty nationwide**[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). They also include **“income volatility”** (% with fluctuating income month to month \~ one in five households)[\[64\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=One%20in%20five%20jobs%20are,percent), and **bill delinquency rate** (13% fell behind on bills in past year nationally)[\[65\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=expenses%2C%20but%2040,fell%20behind%20on%20their%20bills). *Use:* The Scorecard is useful for ongoing monitoring because it compiles multiple sources into one platform updated yearly. It lets you see, for example, if liquid asset poverty is improving and which states are worst. It also highlights racial disparities in vulnerability (e.g., comparing liquid asset poverty by race)[\[31\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=A%20substantial%20racial%20gap%20persists,income%20poverty%20as%20white%20households). *Limitations:* Not original data – aggregates from SCF, ACS, SIPP, etc., so it’s only as current as those sources (often a 1-year lag). Also, state-level asset poverty is modeled (since SCF is national). But it’s well-documented and widely used by policymakers as a benchmark. *Access:* The Scorecard is interactive at [scorecard.prosperitynow.org](https://scorecard.prosperitynow.org) and the 2019 highlights were summarized by NLIHC[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). Each year’s update typically comes out a few months after new federal data is released.

### 4\. **Employment Vulnerability Data Sources**

* **BLS Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS)** – *Publisher:* U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. *Frequency:* Monthly (with about a 1-month lag). *Time series:* From 2000 to present. *What it measures:* Among other things, JOLTS reports the **layoff and discharge rate** by industry each month. This is crucial for identifying **industries with high layoff risk**. For example, construction and hospitality routinely have higher layoff rates (even in good times), whereas government or education have low rates. During downturns, JOLTS shows which sectors spike in layoffs. *Use:* By monitoring JOLTS, one can gauge where workers are most vulnerable to sudden job loss (e.g. if the manufacturing layoff rate starts rising, those workers are one incident away from missing rent). It also provides **quit rates**, which inversely relate to vulnerability (high quits mean workers feel secure to leave jobs). *Limitations:* It’s aggregate data, not individual. It won’t tell you directly “workers one paycheck from disaster,” but it identifies the *risk exposure* by sector. Also, JOLTS has relatively short history for fine-grain analysis of rare events. *Access:* BLS JOLTS website and data query tool (https://www.bls.gov/jlt/) – one can pull layoff rates for, say, retail trade monthly. Analysts often create “early warning” dashboards using JOLTS to flag if layoff rates break above historical norms.

* **BLS Current Population Survey (CPS) – Supplemental data** – *Publisher:* BLS and Census (CPS microdata). *Frequency:* Monthly (for core labor force data) and **supplements** occasionally. *Time series:* CPS has been around for decades. Relevant supplements include the **Contingent Worker Supplement** (most recent 2017, previously 2005\) and **Household Pulse-like questions in 2020**. *What it measures:* The CPS can be used to identify workers in precarious situations: e.g., **involuntary part-time workers** (want full-time but only have part-time) – reported monthly, **temporary and gig workers** (from the Contingent Worker Supplement, which estimated \~10% of workers in 2017 were in alternative work arrangements), and **multiple jobholders**. Also, the CPS tracks **median usual weekly earnings by occupation** – highlighting workers in low-wage jobs who likely have little cushion. *Use:* From CPS data, one could monitor if the number of people working **“gig” jobs or independent contractors** is rising – potentially indicating more without benefits or stable income (i.e., vulnerable if an incident occurs). Similarly, a rise in involuntary part-time work is an early sign of underemployment stress. *Limitations:* Many of these indicators aren’t front-and-center in headline CPS reports; they require digging into microdata or waiting for special supplements (which are infrequent for contingent work). Also, CPS is survey-based, and gig work may be underreported if people don’t think of side hustles as jobs. *Access:* Monthly CPS data is summarized in BLS’s Employment Situation releases (for things like involuntary part-time). The 2017 Contingent Worker Supplement report is on BLS site, giving proportions of independent contractors, on-call workers, etc. (e.g., 3.8% of workers were independent contractors in 2017). Public microdata via IPUMS or data.census.gov can be used to extract custom vulnerability metrics.

* **Census Household Pulse Survey (Housing Insecurity & Employment)** – *Publisher:* Census Bureau. *Frequency:* Biweekly (ongoing). *Time series:* 2020–present. *What it measures:* Several Pulse questions directly relate to *employment vulnerability and hardship*. Key ones: **“Percentage of adults in households where someone had a loss of employment income in last 4 weeks”** – this shows how many are recently impacted by layoffs/furloughs (during 2020 this was huge; it’s still tracked). Also, **“Confidence in ability to pay next month’s rent or mortgage”** and **“Current rent status”** (caught up or behind). As of early 2023, about **11.6% of renter households were behind on rent** according to Pulse[\[66\]](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/Housing-Market-Indicators-Report-March-2023.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Housing%20insecurity%20due%20to,households%20were%20behind%20on%20their), and \~4% feared imminent eviction[\[67\]](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/Housing-Market-Indicators-Report-March-2023.pdf#:~:text=%28Week%2055%3A%20March%201,85%20percent%2C%20or%20701%2C000). This is a very direct measure of those one step from housing crisis (often due to job/income loss). *Use:* Pulse data, updated quickly, is used by agencies (Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, etc.) to track **housing vulnerability**. For example, CFPB cited \~4.5 million households behind on rent in late 2023[\[68\]](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-report-highlights-consumer-protection-issues-in-medical-and-rental-debt-collection/#:~:text=In%20August%202023%2C%20the%20CFPB,of%20collecting%20debt%20through%20coercion). The employment income loss metric signals ongoing churn – if, say, 20% of households report income loss in the last month, that’s a large pool who might be struggling to pay bills. *Limitations:* Pulse is experimental. The rent questions rely on self-report and the understanding of terms like “caught up on rent”. There can be response biases. But it has proven fairly accurate when compared to other sources (e.g. the rent arrears numbers align with other estimates). *Access:* The Census Household Pulse public tables (for Week 55 in Mar 2023, etc.) on [census.gov](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/household-pulse-survey/data.html). For instance, Pulse Week 55 showed **5.35 million renters behind** (11.6%)[\[66\]](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/Housing-Market-Indicators-Report-March-2023.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Housing%20insecurity%20due%20to,households%20were%20behind%20on%20their). The Census Pulse data tool or the **Household Pulse HUD Analysis** (HUD regularly summarizes housing insecurity from Pulse) are available[\[66\]](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/Housing-Market-Indicators-Report-March-2023.pdf#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Housing%20insecurity%20due%20to,households%20were%20behind%20on%20their).

* **Department of Labor Weekly Unemployment Claims** – *Publisher:* U.S. Dept. of Labor. *Frequency:* Weekly (reported every Thursday). *Time series:* Since WWII (seasonally adjusted series). *What it measures:* The number of new claims for unemployment insurance (and continued claims) each week. *Use:* This is a high-frequency indicator of layoffs. A sudden uptick in claims signals more people potentially one paycheck away from trouble. While it doesn’t give detail on *who* is affected, it’s often the first statistical signal of downturns. It can be broken down by state, which could highlight regional vulnerability spikes (e.g., a hurricane might cause a surge in claims in a region). *Limitations:* It measures initial job loss, not financial condition directly. However, it correlates strongly with financial distress events. Not all who lose jobs file claims (especially gig workers, though Pandemic programs expanded coverage temporarily). *Access:* The weekly press release and datasets on the DOL website. Many economic dashboards incorporate this; it’s a well-known metric (e.g., “claims rose to 250k this week” etc.).

* **LendingClub/PYMNTS Paycheck-to-Paycheck Report** – *Publisher:* Private (LendingClub in partnership with PYMNTS.com). *Frequency:* Monthly reports. *Time series:* Launched in 2021, ongoing monthly through 2022–2023. *What it measures:* Survey-based analysis of the share of consumers living paycheck to paycheck, including breakdowns by income level. This report series has highlighted, for example, **\~60-64% of U.S. consumers living paycheck-to-paycheck** throughout 2022–2023[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their). Notably, it finds high rates even among higher earners (45% of those earning \>$100k were paycheck-to-paycheck as of Nov 2023\)[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their). It also measures what proportion struggle to pay bills even while living P2P (about 20% of consumers were in that category in late 2023\)[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their)[\[70\]](https://www.nascus.org/2023/12/20/nearly-two-thirds-of-struggling-consumers-revolve-credit-card-balances/#:~:text=Nearly%20Two,virtually%20unchanged%20from%20last). *Use:* This is a very direct tracker of **income vulnerability** – if someone is living paycheck to paycheck, by definition one missed check could mean missed rent or bills. The fact this has remained around 60% post-pandemic underscores widespread latent vulnerability. Monthly updates show if things are improving or worsening with inflation, etc. *Limitations:* It’s based on survey self-report (“do you live paycheck to paycheck?”). Different surveys yield different numbers depending on wording. But LendingClub’s has become a cited benchmark. It is also an online survey (sample \~3,000), which may skew to those more engaged with personal finance topics. *Access:* LendingClub’s press releases on their IR site or PR Newswire give topline stats[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their). Full reports often available via a link (e.g. to a PDF on pymnts.com) – e.g., *Reality Check: Paycheck-to-Paycheck series*. The November 2023 report is cited in media and shows the 62% figure[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their).

* **United Way ALICE Reports** – *Publisher:* United Way (various state chapters and United Way Worldwide). *Frequency:* Approximately every 2 years per state (staggered releases). *Time series:* Concept introduced \~2014; many states have data for 2010, 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018, 2020, etc. *What it measures:* ALICE \= Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. It defines a household income threshold above poverty but below a basic cost-of-living in that area. The reports then calculate the percentage of households below that ALICE Threshold in each county and state. Nationally, roughly **33% of households are ALICE (above poverty, below stability) plus \~12% below poverty** – meaning about 45% potentially struggling[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership). *Use:* ALICE is essentially a composite vulnerability index capturing working poor and lower-middle-class strain. United Ways use it to advocate for policy, and it’s updated with new Census income/cost data. It shows over time if more families are falling behind the cost of living. Because it’s localized, it highlights pockets of vulnerability (e.g. 50%+ of households in many counties in the South are below ALICE+poverty thresholds). *Limitations:* It’s not updated yearly for every state in sync, and methodology (budget assumptions) can differ slightly by state. But it uses standardized data (American Community Survey for incomes, and standardized expense budgets). *Access:* ALICE reports are available at [UnitedForALICE.org](https://www.unitedforalice.org/). They have interactive data by state and county. For instance, one can find that in New Jersey 37% of households were ALICE in 2020, etc. As a summary stat, it complements the federal poverty rate by adding those just above poverty who are still one crisis away from trouble.

Each of these sources can be monitored on an ongoing basis to quantify and track the “latent vulnerability” of U.S. consumers. By regularly reviewing these datasets – from **health surveys on deferred care** to **insurance coverage reports**, **savings and credit indicators**, and **employment and housing stress surveys** – a comprehensive monitoring system can detect early signs that more families are teetering on the edge of financial distress. The combination of high-frequency data (Pulse, claims, monthly surveys) and deeper analytical reports (SCF, ALICE, etc.) provides both immediacy and robustness for ongoing surveillance of pre-crisis populations[\[69\]](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/nearly-60-of-credit-cardholders-in-the-us-live-paycheck-to-paycheck-302017514.html#:~:text=The%20Paycheck,ongoing%20inflation%2C%20have%20adjusted%20their)[\[30\]](https://nlihc.org/resource/prosperity-now-scorecard-finds-millions-families-live-economic-uncertainty#:~:text=The%202019%20Prosperity%20Now%20Scorecard,in%20income%2C%20wealth%2C%20and%20homeownership).
