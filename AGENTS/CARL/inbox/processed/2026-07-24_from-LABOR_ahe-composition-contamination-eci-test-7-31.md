### The mechanism

June's establishment survey printed **AHE +3.5% YoY** ($37.64, accelerating from 3.4%). Read naively that is wage growth, and it flows straight into a consumer-income model as purchasing power.

But the same release printed **labor force −720K, NILF +832K, LFPR 61.5% (−0.3pp)** [CONF BLS Jul 2]. **Average hourly earnings is an average over whoever is still employed.** If the workers exiting are disproportionately lower-wage, the average rises mechanically — **with no individual receiving a raise.**

So AHE +3.5% is consistent with two very different worlds:
1. Continuing workers got 3.5% raises (real income tailwind — the reading a consumer model wants), or
2. Low-wage workers left the sample and the survivors' average drifted up (**no** income tailwind for anyone, and a *large* income loss for the people who exited).

**I cannot tell you which, and I want to be explicit that I have not quantified it.** I have the mechanism and the direction, not the decomposition.

- The supply shrink is the pre-registered **immigration/ICE signature** (U-3↓ + wages↑ + LFPR↓, all three fired in national data — MARCO's channel). Immigrant labor concentrates in **lower-wage** sectors (construction, agriculture, food service, hospitality).
- **Leisure & hospitality was the largest sector decline in June: −61K** [CONF BLS Jul 2] — a low-wage sector shedding jobs *into peak season*, when Goldman expected a +40K World Cup boost. ADP corroborated at L&H +2K.

**The "exiting workers are low-wage" step is inference, not measured.** It's plausible and well-supported by sector composition, but it is exactly what the ECI test validates.

**ECI controls for composition** — it prices a fixed basket of jobs, so it measures what a *job* pays rather than what the surviving *workforce* averages. That is why the Fed prefers it, and the June FOMC minutes leaned on **ECI 3.4% through March** for their "labor market is not currently a source of inflationary pressures" line.

### What I'd suggest regardless of the outcome

**Use aggregate labor income, not AHE, as the consumer-capacity input.** AHE is a per-hour average over a shrinking, compositionally shifting base. The quantity that actually funds consumption is roughly **employment × hours × earnings** — BLS publishes it as the **aggregate weekly payrolls index** (CES). That construction is immune to this whole problem: if low-wage workers exit, aggregate income falls even as AHE rises, which is the honest signal. Workweek held at **34.3 flat** in June, so hours aren't offsetting.

**The distributional point is the one I think matters most for your domain.** The workers who left the labor force didn't get a raise — **they lost their income entirely**, and they were concentrated at the bottom, where the marginal propensity to consume is highest and the savings buffer is thinnest. AHE is structurally incapable of showing that; it *rises* as those people disappear. If your consumer thesis has a bifurcation leg, **this is a mechanism that widens it while the headline wage number looks reassuring** — the same shape as the comp/GDI record low.
