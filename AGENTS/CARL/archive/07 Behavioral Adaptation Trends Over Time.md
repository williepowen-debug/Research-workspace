# **The Great Deferral: A Longitudinal Analysis of Household Risk Management and Cost Avoidance Strategies in the United States (2010–2025)**

## **Executive Summary**

The period from 2010 to 2025 represents a distinct epoch in American household economics, characterized by a fundamental shift in risk management strategies. This report, "The Great Deferral," documents a pervasive behavioral adaptation wherein households, squeezed by a widening gap between essential costs and real wage growth, have systematically retreated from proactive risk mitigation. This retreat is observed not merely as a reduction in discretionary spending, but as a dangerous deferral of core protective measures: medical care for serious conditions, comprehensive insurance coverage for property, and essential maintenance of physical assets.

This exhaustive analysis synthesizes data from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), Gallup polling, insurance actuarial reports, and housing market studies to illustrate the scale of this phenomenon. The findings indicate that the "risk tolerance" of the average American household has not increased due to preference, but has been forcibly expanded by liquidity constraints. By 2024, record numbers of Americans were delaying treatment for serious medical conditions, "going bare" without homeowners insurance, and operating vehicles at historically high ages with deferred maintenance.

The cumulative effect of these behaviors is a massive accumulation of "tail risk" on household balance sheets—a latent liability that threatens to manifest as catastrophic health crises, unrecoverable property losses, and long-term wealth destruction. This report details the mechanisms, demographics, and long-term implications of this shift, providing a definitive record of how the American household economy managed—and deferred—risk in the first quarter of the 21st century.

## ---

**Section 1: The Macroeconomic Crucible (2010–2025)**

To understand the shift in household risk behaviors, one must first contextualize the economic environment of the last fifteen years. The decade began with households deleveraging from the 2008 Great Financial Crisis, followed by a period of deceptive stability, and concluded with a historic inflationary shock. These pressures fundamentally altered the calculation between immediate cash flow and future protection.

### **1.1 The Erosion of Disposable Liquidity**

The primary driver of cost-deferral behavior is the erosion of disposable liquidity. While headline inflation metrics (CPI) showed moderation for much of the 2010s, the specific components of the "defensive budget"—health insurance, homeowners insurance, vehicle repair, and essential maintenance—experienced hyper-inflationary pressure distinct from the broader basket of goods.1

Between 2019 and 2024, the cost of protection decoupled from wage growth. For example, while wages saw nominal increases, they often lagged behind the double-digit percentage hikes in insurance premiums and medical services. By 2024, the cost of protection had become prohibitive for a significant minority of the population. Federal Reserve data indicates that financial fragility remains high, with substantial portions of the population unable to cover a $400 emergency expense without borrowing or selling assets.2 This lack of liquidity forces a binary choice: pay for immediate consumption (food, energy, housing) or pay for protection against future probabilities (insurance, preventive medicine, maintenance). In this zero-sum calculus, the future is increasingly sacrificed for the present.

### **1.2 The Financialization of Emergency Reserves: 401(k) Hardship Trends**

A critical trend observed between 2019 and 2025 is the reliance on retirement vehicles as de facto emergency funds. This represents a severe degradation of household financial resilience.

Data from major plan administrators like Vanguard and Fidelity reveals a disturbing upward trajectory in hardship withdrawals—distributions taken from retirement accounts due to "immediate and heavy financial need".3

* **Pre-Pandemic Baseline (2018):** Hardship withdrawals were relatively stable at approximately 2.0% of participants.3  
* **The Inflationary Shock (2022-2024):** As the cost of living surged, withdrawal rates climbed rapidly. By 2024, Vanguard reported that 4.8% of participants took a hardship withdrawal, while Fidelity reported rates touching 5.0%.3

This doubling of withdrawal rates indicates a breakdown in traditional liquidity buffers. When 5% of the working population with retirement benefits—a group that is generally more financially stable than the general population—is forced to cannibalize tax-advantaged future savings to fund current liabilities, it signals that the capacity to absorb risk through standard income streams has been exhausted.3 The primary drivers cited for these withdrawals include preventing eviction, foreclosure, and paying unreimbursed medical expenses—confirming the link between liquidity constraints and the deferral behaviors discussed in subsequent sections.3

### **1.3 The "Defensive" Inflation Basket**

While general inflation (CPI) creates headlines, the specific inflation of "risk management" goods has been the silent killer of household budgets.

* **Auto Insurance:** Rates increased \~15% in 2023 and another \~10% in 2024\.6  
* **Home Insurance:** Premiums in high-risk zones (FL, CA, TX) often doubled or tripled between 2020 and 2024\.7  
* **Vehicle Repair:** The cost of parts and labor for vehicle maintenance rose significantly, with average repair costs jumping due to vehicle complexity and labor shortages.8

This specific "defensive inflation" means that even a household with a wage that kept pace with *average* inflation (groceries/gas) would find themselves becoming poorer in terms of *risk capacity*. To balance the budget, the line items for "future protection" were the first to be cut.

**Section 2: The Medical Deferral Crisis – The Body as a Bank Account**

The most immediate and life-threatening manifestation of cost-deferral behavior is found in the healthcare sector. Data from 2010 through 2025 reveals a disturbing trend: despite high rates of nominal insurance coverage, the *functional* accessibility of care is being throttled by out-of-pocket costs, leading to widespread avoidance of necessary medical treatment.

### **2.1 Longitudinal Trends in Delayed Care (2010–2025)**

The trajectory of Americans delaying medical care due to cost is not linear. It tells the story of policy interventions (ACA) clashing with market realities (deductibles).

**Phase 1: The Pre-ACA High (2010–2013)** In the early part of the decade, prior to the full implementation of the Affordable Care Act's coverage expansion, delayed care rates were historically high. Gallup polling from this era shows that roughly 30-32% of Americans reported delaying some form of medical treatment due to cost.9

**Phase 2: The ACA Stabilization (2014–2019)** With the expansion of Medicaid and the introduction of marketplace subsidies, the percentage of Americans delaying care saw a modest stabilization. The income gap in delayed care narrowed slightly, averaging around 11 percentage points between the top and bottom income quartiles.9 However, this period also saw the rise of High Deductible Health Plans (HDHPs), which began to erode the "protection" offered by insurance.

**Phase 3: The Pandemic and Inflationary Spike (2020–2025)**

The most dramatic shifts occurred in the post-2020 era. Initially, care was deferred due to fear of infection (2020). However, as the biological threat receded, the financial threat took over.

* **2022 Peak:** Gallup recorded a historic high in 2022, with **38%** of Americans reporting they or a family member delayed medical treatment due to cost.10  
* **Serious Conditions:** Most alarmingly, the percentage of adults delaying care for *serious* medical conditions reached **27%** in late 2022, a 12-point increase from previous lows and the highest reading in the trend's history.11  
* **2024 Update:** While there was a slight moderation by November 2024, the rate remained elevated at **22%** for serious conditions, indicating that the structural barrier of cost remains high.12

### **2.2 Demographic Disparities: The Unequal Burden**

The burden of medical deferral is not shared equally. It falls disproportionately on specific demographic groups, exacerbating health inequities.

**Income and Race:** NHIS data from 2022–2023 highlights that the prevalence of delaying medical care due to cost was highest among **non-Hispanic Black adults (13.3%)** and those with a poverty income ratio of less than 4.0.13 Lower-income adults are significantly more likely to report delayed care, and the gap between high and low-income households for delaying serious treatment widened to **23 percentage points** by 2019\.9

**The Gender Gap:** A persistent and widening gender gap exists in medical avoidance. In 2022, women were significantly more likely than men to report delaying medical treatment (32% vs. 20%).11

* **Mechanism:** This 12-point gap is notably higher than the long-term average of 7 points.11 Sociological analysis suggests women often function as the "Chief Medical Officers" of their households, prioritizing the care of children and partners over their own needs when resources are constrained.  
* **NHIS Confirmation:** NHIS data confirms this, showing the prevalence of delaying care due to cost was 12.7% for women versus 8.6% for men in 2022-2023.13

**Age Cohorts:** Younger adults (18-49) consistently report higher rates of delayed care (35% in 2022\) compared to those aged 65+ (13%).11 The insulation provided by Medicare for the 65+ cohort contrasts sharply with the exposure of working-age adults to commercial insurance deductibles and copays.

### **2.3 The "Underinsurance" Trap**

A critical finding of this research is that "having insurance" is no longer synonymous with "having access." The rise of the "underinsured" is a defining characteristic of the 2010–2025 period.

* **Commonwealth Fund Data:** In 2022, **43%** of working-age adults were inadequately insured.14  
* **Behavioral Impact:** Among these underinsured adults, **57%** reported avoiding needed health care because of cost.15  
* **Medicaid Paradox:** Interestingly, adults with Medicaid were significantly *less* likely to delay medical care compared to those with private insurance (aOR \= 0.2).13 This counter-intuitive finding underscores that while private insurance suggests higher status, its high out-of-pocket costs (deductibles often exceeding $2,000-$5,000) create a steeper barrier to entry for care than the low-to-zero cost sharing of Medicaid.

### **2.4 The Phenomenon of "Stage Migration"**

The economic decision to delay care has profound clinical consequences, best described as "stage migration"—the progression of a disease from a manageable, early stage to a complex, late stage during the period of deferral.

**Cancer Diagnoses:**

The disruption of screenings during the pandemic (2020-2021) and the subsequent cost-related deferrals (2022-2025) created a cohort of "missing diagnoses."

* **Clinical Impact:** Studies modeling delay impacts found that a 6-month delay in diagnosis could result in significant stage shifts. For example, delaying diagnosis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by 6 months increases the probability of presenting at a later stage significantly.16  
* **Economic Impact:** Treating cancer at a later stage is exponentially more expensive. For breast cancer, the cost of treating Stage IV disease is **2.1 to 3.1 times higher** than treating Stage I disease.17 By trying to save the cost of a screening or initial visit, the system and the patient incur a massive future liability.

**Chronic Disease Management:** The same logic applies to chronic conditions like diabetes. Actuarial modeling indicates that delaying the intensification of therapy for Type 2 diabetes by just one year is associated with increased total costs of billions of dollars across the population and significant loss of life-years.18 The deferral of routine maintenance (medication adherence, check-ups) leads to the acute failure of the body (stroke, amputation, renal failure).

### **2.5 The "Luxury" of Dental and Mental Health**

When households engage in medical triage, dental and mental health services are often the first to be severed, despite their critical role in overall health.

**Dental Deferral:**

By 2024, dental care avoidance had become endemic.

* **Rate of Avoidance:** A staggering **41%** of adults reported delaying or skipping dental care due to cost.15  
* **Vulnerable Populations:** For parents with children under 18, **72%** reported postponing dental care to cover other household costs.19  
* **ER Utilization:** This avoidance leads to an inefficient shifting of costs to the Emergency Room. ER visits for non-traumatic dental conditions (NTDCs) have seen rising charges even as visit counts fluctuate, representing a failure of the primary care system.20 These visits often result in "treat and release" scenarios (pain meds/antibiotics) rather than curative treatment, merely deferring the inevitable extraction or expensive restoration.

**Mental Health Deferral:**

The mental health crisis has been collided with a financial wall.

* **Cost Barriers:** In 2024, **20%** of respondents delayed mental health care specifically due to cost.15  
* **Youth Impact:** The situation is dire for adolescents. Among those with major depressive episodes and substance use disorders in 2024, **27.9%** received *no treatment at all*.22  
* **Duration of Untreated Illness (DUI):** The median delay between symptom onset and treatment for some mental disorders has historically been measured in years (often over a decade).23 The current cost-of-living crisis exacerbates this, prolonging suffering and increasing the likelihood of comorbid physical health issues and productivity loss.24

**Section 3: The Retreat from Insurance – Risk Transfer Reversal**

Parallel to the deferral of medical care is a systemic retreat from property and casualty insurance. As premiums for auto and home insurance surged between 2021 and 2024—driven by social inflation, climate risk modeling, and reinsurance costs—consumers responded by reducing coverage limits, raising deductibles, or dropping insurance entirely. This "going bare" phenomenon represents a massive transfer of risk from the global insurance markets back to individual household balance sheets.

### **3.1 The Auto Insurance Crisis: Liability-Only and Uninsured Motorists**

The auto insurance market from 2020 to 2025 was characterized by the sharpest premium increases in decades. In response, American drivers radically altered their coverage profiles.

**The Rise of the Uninsured Motorist:**

As premiums rose, a growing segment of the population dropped mandatory coverage, accepting the legal and financial risk of driving uninsured.

* **National Trend:** The Insurance Research Council (IRC) estimated that the percentage of uninsured motorists increased from a low of **11.6% in 2019** to **15.4% by 2023**.25  
* **State-Level Crisis:** In high-cost states, the non-compliance rate is far higher. In 2022, **25.2%** of drivers in Washington D.C. were uninsured. Mississippi followed closely at **28.2%**.25  
* **Implication:** This creates a negative feedback loop. As more drivers drop insurance, the cost of Uninsured Motorist (UM) coverage for compliant drivers increases, forcing premiums higher and causing even more drivers to drop coverage.

**The Shift to Liability-Only and High Deductibles:**

Beyond illegal non-compliance, legal "under-compliance" became a dominant trend. Drivers increasingly dropped comprehensive and collision (Comp/Coll) coverage, retaining only the mandatory liability limits.

* **Mechanism:** With the average vehicle age reaching 12.8 years in 2025, many owners calculated that the premiums for physical damage coverage were not worth the depreciated value of the asset.29  
* **Deductible Gaming:** Those retaining full coverage aggressively raised deductibles to suppress premiums. In Q3 2024, **22.1%** of collision claims carried a **$1,000 deductible**, up significantly from early 2023, while the share of $500 deductibles declined.30  
* **Shopping Behavior:** Policy shopping hit all-time highs, with **45%** of insureds shopping their policy in 2024, indicating a highly price-sensitive consumer base willing to switch carriers for marginal savings.6

### **3.2 The Homeowners Insurance Retreat: "Going Bare"**

Perhaps the most alarming trend in property risk is the decision by homeowners to forgo insurance entirely. Historically rare and typically forbidden by mortgage covenants, "going bare" has surged among those who own their homes outright or are willing to risk default.

**The Scale of Non-Coverage:** By 2024, approximately **7.4%** of U.S. homeowners lacked insurance, equating to **$1.6 trillion** in unprotected property value.31

* **Income Correlation:** The rate is heavily stratified by income. For homeowners with family income less than $25,000, **29%** were without insurance in 2024\.2  
* **Demographic Disparities:** Homeowners of color are disproportionately affected. **22%** of Native American homeowners and **14%** of Hispanic homeowners lacked coverage, compared to the national average of 7.4%.31  
* **Geographic Risk:** In vulnerable markets, the numbers are far higher. In Houston and Miami—cities highly exposed to climate risk—the uninsured rate ranged from **10% to 15%**.32 In Mississippi and New Mexico, state-wide uninsured rates for homeowners hit **13%**.32

**The Driver: Affordability vs. Risk:** This trend is driven by an affordability crisis where premiums in disaster-prone areas have doubled or tripled. In New Orleans-Metairie, homeowners spend a staggering **17.48%** of their income on homeowners insurance.33 When faced with a $6,000 annual premium on a fixed income, many retirees are choosing to "self-insure," a misnomer for simply accepting the risk of total financial ruin in the event of a disaster.

**Coverage Gaps: The Flood/Quake Void:**

Even among the insured, coverage gaps are widening. Standard policies exclude flood and earthquake damage, requiring separate riders.

* **Flood:** In 2022, fewer than **20%** of Florida homeowners carried flood insurance despite high exposure.34  
* **Deductibles:** Similar to auto, homeowners are raising deductibles to 2% or 5% of the home's value, particularly for wind/hail claims. On a $400,000 home, a 2% deductible means the homeowner is responsible for the first **$8,000** of damage. This high threshold discourages filing claims for minor roof damage, leading to deferred maintenance.35

**Section 4: Asset Neglect – The Deferred Maintenance of the American Dream**

The deferral of costs extends to the physical assets that underpin household wealth and mobility. The data from 2020–2025 describes a crumbling stock of private assets where preventive maintenance is sacrificed for cash flow.

### **4.1 The Aging Automotive Fleet**

The average age of light vehicles in the U.S. reached a record **12.8 years** in 2025\.29 This aging is not merely a function of better engineering; it is a function of affordability. With new car prices and interest rates soaring, households are holding onto vehicles longer than ever before.

**Service Deferral:**

As vehicles age, they require more maintenance, yet service behaviors are deteriorating.

* **Declining Dealership Visits:** There has been a measurable shift away from dealership service centers toward independent shops or DIY solutions. In 2023, only 30% of service visits occurred at dealerships, down from 35% in 2021\.8  
* **Trust and Cost:** The erosion of trust in dealerships (down to 54% from 62%) and the rising cost of labor (average ticket up 45% in two years) are driving consumers to skip non-critical repairs.8  
* **Inoperability Risk:** Consumer Reports data from 2024 highlights a link between reported problems and vehicle inoperability.37 By skipping fluid changes, timing belt replacements, or brake service, households increase the risk of catastrophic mechanical failure.

**Total Loss Trends:** The combination of aging vehicles and deferred maintenance impacts insurance outcomes. In 2024, **22.6%** of all auto claims were declared total losses.38 This is partly because the cost to repair (inflated by parts/labor) often exceeds the depreciated value of the older vehicle. Deferred maintenance exacerbates this; a car with a neglected engine is worth less, making it easier to "total" after a minor accident.

### **4.2 The Housing Stock: Crumbling from Within**

The U.S. housing stock is aging, with the median owner-occupied home age exceeding 40 years. Maintaining these structures requires consistent capital injection, which households are increasingly withholding.

**Renovation vs. Repair Bifurcation:** While top-line remodeling spending hit record highs in 2022 ($600 billion), this data masks a bifurcation in the market.39

* **Luxury vs. Necessity:** High-income households continued to invest in discretionary upgrades (kitchens, baths). Conversely, low-to-middle income households shifted spending almost exclusively to *emergency* repairs (HVAC failure, major leaks) while deferring routine preservation.40  
* **Spending Drop:** Spending on emergency projects dropped in 2024 compared to 2023, which *could* suggest proactive maintenance, but survey data suggests it is more likely due to simple affordability constraints and delaying the work.41

**The Survey Data: Regret and Stress:** Surveys from 2024 indicate that nearly **one-third (32%)** of homeowners regret delaying routine maintenance.42 The top regrets include neglecting "hidden" systems like refrigerator coils and dryer vents—small deferrals that reduce efficiency and increase fire risk. Furthermore, **43%** of homeowners reported increased stress related to repairs, citing affordability as the primary barrier.41

**The "Uninsurable" Home:** The intersection of deferred maintenance and insurance is becoming a flashpoint. Insurers are increasingly using aerial imagery (drones/satellites) to identify deferred maintenance (e.g., moss on roofs, overhanging trees, debris in yards) and issuing non-renewal notices based on these observations.43

* **The Trap:** This creates a "catch-22": the homeowner cannot afford the new roof ($15,000+), so they lose insurance; without insurance, they cannot get a home equity loan to pay for the roof. This forces them into the "going bare" statistic mentioned in Section 3.2.

## ---

**Section 5: Systemic Implications and Conclusion**

The behaviors described above—skipping doctors, dropping insurance, ignoring squeaky brakes—are not isolated consumer preferences. They are symptoms of a financial strategy that relies on "self-insurance" without the capital reserves to support it.

### **5.1 The "Tail Risk" Accumulation**

By systematically removing layers of protection—health screenings, insurance coverage, asset maintenance—households have reduced their monthly burn rate but massively increased their exposure to "tail risk" (low probability, high impact events).

* **Health:** A missed screening becomes Stage IV cancer.  
* **Auto:** A missed insurance payment becomes a bankruptcy-inducing liability lawsuit after an accident.  
* **Home:** A deferred roof repair becomes a total loss from mold or water damage that is uncovered by insurance.

### **5.2 Wealth Inequality Acceleration**

This "Great Deferral" accelerates wealth inequality. Wealthy households can afford the "defensive budget"—the preventive care, the comprehensive insurance, the new roof. They transfer risk away from themselves. Lower and middle-income households are forced to *retain* the risk. When that risk manifests (illness, accident, storm), it destroys what little wealth they have accumulated.

**Conclusion:**

The years 2010 to 2025 taught American households that risk management is a luxury good. Faced with the relentless arithmetic of inflation and stagnant real wages, millions of families have been forced to become their own actuaries, gambling that the roof will hold, the car will start, and the pain in their chest is nothing serious. The data—record hardship withdrawals, rising uninsured rates, and delayed medical care—suggests that for a growing segment of the population, this gamble is becoming increasingly difficult to win. The shift from "risk avoidance" to "risk tolerance" is not a psychological evolution but a financial survival strategy—one that leaves the nation's households more fragile and exposed to shock than at any point in recent history.
