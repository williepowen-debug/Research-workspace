A historical analogue can match on **event class** (weekend attack on Saudi oil infrastructure, market closed) and still be **wrong on sign**, because the **asset class** differs.

**The case (2026-07-25, WALTER).** Houthi missiles struck Aramco's **Jazan refinery**. Every piece of coverage reached for **Abqaiq, September 2019** — also a Saturday, also market-closed, also Aramco. The base-rate work looked clean: geopolitical oil spikes revert fast (Abqaiq's record +19.5% intraday round-tripped in ~2 weeks; 9/11 +5%→−25% in 14 days; Ukraine +30%→pre-invasion in ~8 weeks).

**Then the asset classes turned out not to match.** Abqaiq was a crude **PROCESSING/stabilisation** facility — take it out and **crude physically cannot reach export**, a direct crude-supply loss. Jazan is a **REFINERY** — take it out and **the crude that fed it becomes available for export instead**, which is arguably **crude-bearish and product-bullish**. Same headline shape, potentially **opposite sign**, and the real read runs through **cracks and diesel** rather than flat price. No coverage anywhere made the distinction.

**Discipline:** when reaching for an analogue, ask *"is the thing that was damaged the same KIND of thing?"* before asking *"what happened to prices last time?"* For energy specifically: production ≠ processing ≠ refining ≠ storage ≠ transport — each has a different sign and a different affected product. The same applies outside energy (a servicer failing ≠ an originator failing; a clearinghouse ≠ a broker).

**Corollary — name which axis you're borrowing.** In the same session I had warned the fleet that Abqaiq is *the* trap on this event (its **5.7 mb/d** would get attached to a 400kbpd refinery) and then leaned on Abqaiq as a behavioural precedent. **Magnitude and behaviour are different uses of one case; say which you mean**, or it reads as inconsistency — and if the asset classes don't match, the case is bad on *both* axes.
