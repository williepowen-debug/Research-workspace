When two entities are compared on a metric that has **multiple standard perimeters** (debt held-by-public vs gross vs augmented; revenue net vs gross; headcount FTE vs total), the comparison silently picks one perimeter per entity. If they differ, **the gap you read is an artifact of the scope choice, not a fact about the world** — and it will be the *dramatic* pairing that survives into the packet, because that is the one that looked worth writing down.

**Why:** ZHAO, 2026-08-03, P3 direction-flip test. The inherited research spine compared **China's augmented public-sector debt ~124–130% of GDP** against the **US's 101%** and read it as evidence of relative Chinese fiscal distress. But 124–130% is China's *widest* perimeter (central + explicit local + LGFV + guided funds), while 101% is the US's *narrowest* (federal debt held by the public, excluding intragovernmental holdings). **US GROSS federal debt is 123.1% ($38.6T, CBO Feb 2026) — a dead heat.** And the mismatch runs the other way too: China's augmented figure already absorbs its quasi-fiscal local vehicles, while US gross excludes state & local debt entirely (~11% GDP more). On a genuinely comparable perimeter the US is plausibly the *higher*-debt sovereign. An entire leg of the thesis rested on the artifact. Same session, the packet had *already* been pre-flagged for a sibling unit trap (LGFV **stock** ~51% GDP vs **borrowing flow** ~4% GDP/yr) — so the class bit **twice in one packet**, once even after being warned about.

**The same session then showed the other half of the lesson.** Pushing to the IMF primary (reachable after all — see [[finding_blocked_mirror_is_not_an_unreachable_primary]]) produced China's augmented debt at **126.6% (2025)** vs US gross 123.1% — the stock really was a dead heat. **But it also surfaced a flow measure I had never tested: China's augmented deficit −14.3% of GDP vs the US federal 5.8%, roughly 2×.** So the perimeter critique was right about the *stock* and had quietly been generalized into "debt doesn't support the thesis," which was wrong. **Killing a bad comparison is not the same as answering the question** — after you invalidate a metric, ask which *related* metric the argument should have been made on, because that one may point the other way. And the same country's own number spanned **48.3pp** on definition alone (China's authorities 68.7% for 2024 vs IMF staff 117.0%), which is usually the fastest way to demonstrate that a perimeter is doing the work.

**How to apply:** Before reading any cross-entity gap, name the perimeter for **both** sides out loud and check they match. Then check whether the argument has a **stock vs flow** twin, and test both — a wash on one is routinely a 2× gap on the other. Three tells that a perimeter mismatch is in play: (1) the two numbers come from **different source institutions** (IMF-attributed vs CBO), which almost guarantees different definitional conventions; (2) one side's metric has a well-known "wide" variant and you are quoting the wide one *for the entity you expect to look worse*; (3) the gap is suspiciously large for two entities you know to be broadly comparable. The fix is not to pick the "right" perimeter but to **quote both entities at both perimeters** and see whether the sign survives — if it doesn't, the finding is the mismatch, not the gap. When the counterpart metric is another agent's domain, flag it to them rather than deriving it yourself ([[feedback_check_domain_owner_before_messaging]]); ZHAO handed the US-augmented derivation to HENRY/BOND rather than manufacturing a number.

Distinct from [[finding_number_carries_threshold_unit_source]] (one number drifting across documents), [[finding_normalization_choice_picks_opposite_winners]] (abs vs proportional lenses on one dataset), and [[finding_composition_mask_unmask_discriminator]] (an entity controlling its own denominator). This one is **two entities, one metric name, two scopes.** Related: [[finding_analogue_asset_class_must_match]] — the same "make the two sides actually comparable before reading the difference" discipline, applied to analogues rather than perimeters.
