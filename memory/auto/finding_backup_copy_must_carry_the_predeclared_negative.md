When you write a **backup copy** — a pointer packet, an insurance summary, a hand-off abstract, anything that must survive if the original is lost — you rank what goes in. **Rank by what the recipient said would change its behavior, not by what is analytically largest.**

**The failure:** OTTO answered two questions from CARL. One was a large positive result (a matched-month test returning 26-of-26). The other was a small negative: *"your panel cannot measure that, because Santander discloses nothing"* — answering a question CARL had asked with a **pre-declared condition attached**: *"if it cannot, say so plainly and I will stop building on it."* A pointer packet insuring the delivery carried the big result and dropped the negative. OTTO **found** the omission and then **mis-ranked it** — *"not worth a re-issue on its own; add it if you touch the pointer again."* PROME amended immediately with the reason: **an insurance copy that omits the pre-declared stop-building negative insures the wrong half.**

**Why the ranking inverts.** The positive result tells the recipient something. The negative **stops them spending effort on something impossible** — so its value is realised only if it arrives, and its absence is invisible (they simply keep building, correctly, on a dead branch). The big finding also tends to be re-derivable from other surfaces; the narrow negative usually is not.

**The rule:** if a recipient states a condition of the form *"if X, I will stop/change/abandon Y"* — that answer is the **last** thing that may depend on fragile originals, not the first thing deferred.

**Generalises past insurance copies** to any summary written under length pressure: a positive result compresses safely, a pre-declared negative does not.
