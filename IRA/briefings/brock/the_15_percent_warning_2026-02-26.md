# The 15 Percent Warning
*Update | 12min | 2026-02-26*

---

UBS just raised the alarm on private credit. And they're not being subtle about it.

Less than a month ago, their strategists laid out a worst-case scenario: private credit defaults could hit 13% if things went wrong. That was already alarming — triple the current rate.

This week, they revised that forecast upward to **15%**.

That's not a tweak. That's a 2-percentage-point jump in less than 30 days. And the reason they give is simple: **artificial intelligence is disrupting software companies faster than they expected.**

Let me walk you through what's happening, why it matters, and how it connects to the research we've been tracking.

---

## The Numbers

First, let's establish scale.

**Private credit** — loans made by non-bank lenders like asset managers and private equity funds — is now a **$1.8 trillion market**. It exploded over the last decade as banks pulled back from lending and investors chased yield.

Current default rates in private credit are running between **3% and 5%**. That's elevated but manageable. The industry says everything's fine.

UBS strategist Matthew Mish and his team are saying: not so fast. In a worst-case scenario — specifically, if AI disrupts corporate borrowers aggressively — defaults could hit **15%**.

Fifteen percent of $1.8 trillion is **$270 billion in losses**.

To put that in perspective: the entire subprime mortgage crisis in 2008 involved about $1.3 trillion in losses across the global financial system. Private credit alone could generate a fifth of that.

---

## What Changed in a Month

UBS published their original worst-case forecast — 13% defaults — less than a month ago. So what happened between then and now to make them raise it to 15%?

One word: **AI**.

Here's the quote from their Tuesday report:

> "What is new: a clearer catalyst — rapid, severe AI disruption."

They're not talking about some distant future risk. They're talking about **right now**. AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, and Gemini are cannibalizing revenue at software companies.

Think about it: if a company used to charge you $50 a month for a productivity tool, and now ChatGPT can do the same thing for free or for $20 a month... what happens to that company's revenue?

It drops. Fast.

And here's the problem: **private credit is massively overweight software companies**.

---

## The Software Concentration Problem

This is the core vulnerability.

UBS estimates that private credit firms have **40% of all sponsor-backed loans tied up in the software industry**.

Forty percent. In one sector.

Why? Because for the last five years, software was the golden goose. High margins. Recurring revenue. Sticky customers. Private equity loved it. They'd buy a software company, lever it up 5-to-1, and borrow from private credit funds to finance the deal.

That worked great when software companies were growing 20-30% a year.

But now? AI is eating their lunch.

**SaaS businesses** — Software as a Service — are getting disrupted. Tasks that used to require expensive enterprise software can now be done with general-purpose AI tools. And those AI tools cost a fraction of what the old software cost.

So revenue drops. But the debt doesn't. A company that borrowed $50 million at 10% interest still owes $5 million a year in interest — even if its revenue just dropped 25%.

When revenue falls below debt service costs, companies default.

And if 40% of your loan book is in the sector getting disrupted, **you have a problem**.

---

## The Evidence: It's Already Happening

This isn't theoretical. We're seeing the cracks in real time.

**Blue Owl Capital** — one of the biggest private credit managers in the world — just **permanently shut the gates** on one of its funds. That means: investors who want their money back can't get it. The fund had to sell assets to meet redemptions, and now it's closed.

Blue Owl's stock dropped **$2.4 billion** in market value in a single day. And it dragged down the entire sector: Ares, Blackstone, Apollo — all down.

**New Mountain Finance** — a publicly traded BDC (business development company) — announced Tuesday that it's **selling nearly $500 million in assets**. And not at par. At **94 cents on the dollar**.

That's a **6% haircut**. Real losses, being realized right now.

The company framed it as "diversification" and "financial flexibility." But the timing tells you what's really happening: they need cash, and they're willing to take losses to get it.

And then there's the **PIK problem**.

---

## PIK: The Masking Mechanism

PIK stands for **payment-in-kind**. It's when a borrower can't pay cash interest, so instead they just... add it to the loan balance.

Think of it like paying your credit card bill with another credit card. You're not actually paying. You're deferring.

UBS confirms in their report: **"Signs of strain such as interest paid-in-kind (PIK) are nearing post-pandemic highs."**

This is exactly what we've been tracking in BROCK's research. The BDC industry is running **12.8% PIK income** as a percentage of total income. Some individual BDCs are way higher — PSEC is at **35%**.

What that means is: a third of PSEC's "income" isn't cash. It's just IOUs from borrowers who can't pay.

And when PIK gets too high, it eventually converts to a default. Because you can't defer forever.

So the **real default rate** is higher than the reported rate. UBS is now acknowledging that publicly.

## The Chorus of Warnings

UBS isn't alone. In the last week, a whole lineup of heavyweights has started warning about private credit.

**Jamie Dimon**, CEO of JPMorgan, has been the most vocal. He's been talking about "credit market cockroaches" — problems hiding under the surface that haven't been exposed yet. This week he doubled down, calling out the **"dumb things"** some lenders are doing and drawing explicit comparisons to the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis.

**Danny Moses** — one of the guys from *The Big Short*, who made a fortune betting against subprime mortgages in 2007 — said this week that the **"aggressive push by private markets into retail products reminds him of the years preceding the subprime mortgage crisis."**

That's not a casual comparison. He's saying: this is 2007 all over again, just with private credit instead of housing.

**Boaz Weinstein**, the activist investor who runs Saba Capital, warned of **"the wheels coming off"** in private credit. And he's not just talking — he's positioning. His fund is trying to buy stakes in three Blue Owl funds at steep discounts to their stated value. That's vulture positioning. He thinks those assets are worth way less than the funds claim.

---

## The 2008 Parallel

Let me spell out the parallel, because it's important.

In 2006-2007, Wall Street took subprime mortgages — loans to people with bad credit — and packaged them into securities called CDOs. They sold these to institutional investors and even retail investors, calling them "safe" because they were rated AAA.

When housing prices stopped going up, the mortgages defaulted. The CDOs blew up. Lehman Brothers collapsed. The financial system froze.

**What's happening now:**

Private credit funds are taking loans to highly leveraged software companies — many of them backed by private equity — and packaging them into structures that get sold to retail investors. BDCs trade on the stock exchange. Interval funds are quasi-liquid vehicles that retail can access.

These are being marketed as "safe" because they're senior secured debt. First in line if the company goes bankrupt.

But here's the problem: if the company is a software business that just lost 30% of its revenue to AI, **what is the collateral worth?**

Not much.

So "senior secured" doesn't save you if the whole company is worthless.

And when defaults spike, retail investors — people who bought these BDCs thinking they were getting steady 8-10% yields — lose money. Funds gate redemptions. Banks that lent to the funds take losses. Credit spreads widen. The whole thing unravels.

**That's the parallel Danny Moses is drawing.**

---

## How It Transmits to Banks

You might be thinking: okay, but I don't own private credit. Why does this matter for the regional bank positions we're tracking?

Here's the transmission:

**First: BDCs borrow from banks.**

BDCs use revolving credit facilities and warehouse lines from banks to fund their lending. When BDCs take losses, they either draw down those lines (which ties up bank capital) or they default on them (which causes direct bank losses).

**Second: Fund finance.**

Regional banks — especially ones like Citizens Financial (CFG) and Western Alliance (WAL) — have built big **fund finance** businesses. They lend to private equity funds and private credit funds using the fund's portfolio as collateral.

If those funds blow up or gate redemptions, the banks holding the lines eat the losses.

**Third: Credit spread contagion.**

If private credit defaults spike to 15%, that doesn't stay contained. It bleeds into **leveraged loans** (UBS now forecasts up to 10% defaults) and **high-yield bonds** (up to 6% defaults).

When high-yield spreads widen — which is what your HYG put is betting on — that's a signal of broad credit stress. And credit stress hits regional banks harder than big banks, because regional banks are more exposed to middle-market lending.

**So the chain is:**
Private credit defaults → BDC losses → Bank credit facilities stressed → Credit spreads widen → Regional banks take losses.

It's not immediate. There's a **1-2 quarter lag**. But it's building.

## The Counter-Argument

Now let me give you the other side, because there are smart people who think the panic is overblown.

**Dan Loeb**, who runs Third Point, just announced he's **launching a BDC** on April 2nd. That's right — he's buying INTO the stress.

His argument: yes, some software loans will get impaired. But private credit funds aren't like banks. They don't have a mismatch between assets and liabilities. There's no run-on-the-bank scenario because investors are locked in for years.

He's essentially saying: this is going to be painful, but it's not systemic.

**Bruce Flatt**, CEO of Brookfield, echoed that view. He said: **"This is not a systemic situation."** Private credit is relatively small compared to the global credit market. Even if it takes losses, the system can absorb it.

And there's a grain of truth to that. Private credit is $1.8 trillion. The global credit market is over $100 trillion. So as a percentage, it's small.

But here's the counterpoint: it's not about the size of the market. It's about **who's exposed** and **where the stress transmits**.

If retail investors lose confidence in BDCs, and funds start gating redemptions, and banks holding fund finance lines take losses, and credit spreads blow out... that's a **confidence shock**, not just a loss absorption problem.

Confidence shocks move fast.
