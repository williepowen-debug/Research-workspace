# The Phantom Jobs
*Update | 12min | 2026-02-12*

---

Over one million jobs that the government said existed in 2025... didn't exist.

This isn't a rounding error. This is the largest downward benchmark revision since the Great Financial Crisis. And it fundamentally changes what we thought we knew about the U.S. labor market.

Let's talk about what just happened, why it matters, and what it means for the research we're tracking.

---

## The Numbers

On February 11th, 2026, the Bureau of Labor Statistics released the January jobs report. The headline looked fine: 130,000 jobs added, beating the 55,000 expected. Markets spiked. Algorithms bought. 

But buried in the report was the benchmark revision.

Here's what the government SAID happened in 2025:
- Total job growth: 584,000 jobs

Here's what ACTUALLY happened:
- Total job growth: 181,000 jobs

That's a revision of **negative 403,000 jobs**. But the real shock is deeper. When you look at the level of employment — the total number of people working — the government overstated it by between 898,000 and 1,045,000 jobs, depending on the month.

Think about that. Over the course of 2025, somewhere between 900,000 and 1 million jobs that were reported... were phantom. They didn't exist.

The 2025 monthly average? Fifteen thousand jobs per month. That's not growth. That's stagnation.

---

## Why This Happens

You might be asking: how does the government get it this wrong?

The answer is complicated, but here's the short version: the monthly jobs report is based on a survey. They call up businesses and ask "how many people do you employ?" Then they model that out to the whole economy.

But every year, they get actual data — unemployment insurance records, tax filings, real counts. And once a year, they revise the estimates to match reality. That's the benchmark revision.

Usually, it's small. A few thousand here or there. Normal statistical noise.

This year? The benchmark revision was **negative 862,000 jobs**. According to Bloomberg, you have to go back to **2009** — the depths of the Great Financial Crisis — to find a comparable miss.

This isn't statistical noise. This is structural.

---

## What It Means

Here's the thing: if you averaged 15,000 jobs per month in 2025, and the population is growing by about 150,000 people per month entering the workforce... you're not keeping up. You're falling behind.

And the composition matters. Where did the "phantom jobs" come from?

A big chunk was **federal government employment**. DOGE — the Department of Government Efficiency — has cut 327,000 federal jobs since October 2024. Down 10.9% from the peak. In January alone, the government shed 50,000 jobs.

But it's not just federal. **Financial activities** lost 25,000 jobs in January. Insurance carriers — which we've been told are "resilient" — cut 11,000 jobs in a single month.

The only bright spot? **Healthcare and education**, which added 140,000 jobs. But that's masking weakness everywhere else.

---

## The "Hotel California" Pattern

We've been tracking what we call the "Hotel California" labor market: easy to keep your job, hard to find a new one.

This revision confirms it.

Look at **long-term unemployment** — people who've been out of work for 27 weeks or more. It's up 386,000 year-over-year. That's a **28% increase**. 

One in four unemployed people has been jobless for over six months. They're not finding new jobs. They're stuck.

Meanwhile, **JOLTS** — job openings — hit 6.5 million in December. That's the lowest since December 2017. There are now **1 million more unemployed people than job openings**. For the first time since February 2021, the ratio dropped below 1.0.

So if you lose your job, you're competing with seven and a half million other people for six and a half million openings. And if you don't find something quickly, you're likely to be stuck for months.

---

## The Small Business Safety Valve Just Broke

Here's the part that should scare you.

For the last year, the narrative has been: "Yes, big companies are cutting, but small businesses are hiring. The job market is bifurcated, not broken."

That was the safety valve. Large corporations shed jobs, but small businesses — which account for 46% of U.S. employment and 63% of new job creation — would pick up the slack.

New data from the New York Fed shows that safety valve just broke.

**Small businesses with 1-9 employees** — two-thirds of all firms — are now showing:
- **Negative revenue growth** for the first time since the pandemic
- **Negative or weak employment growth** in 2024, and it continued in 2025
- Higher downsizing rates than at any point since 2020

These aren't tech startups trying to scale. These are local businesses — restaurants, contractors, retail shops. The backbone of the economy. And they're cutting.

When small businesses stop hiring, there's no cushion left.

---

## The Corporate Bankruptcy Wave

Here's the final piece that connects this.

Last week, **nine large companies** — each with over $50 million in liabilities — filed for bankruptcy. The three-week rolling average is now **six large bankruptcies per week**.

When was the last time we saw that? The COVID pandemic in 2020. Before that? The peak of the Great Financial Crisis in 2009. Before that? The 2001 recession.

Corporate bankruptcies are a **leading indicator** for labor market stress. Companies don't file for bankruptcy on a whim. They file after months of trying to survive — cutting costs, laying off workers, burning through reserves.

When bankruptcy filings spike, it tells you two things:
1. Companies have been under stress for a while (you're seeing lagged pain)
2. More layoffs are coming (bankruptcies lead to liquidations or restructurings, both of which shed jobs)

We track this because BDCs — Business Development Companies — are the lenders to these middle-market firms. When bankruptcies spike, BDC loan losses follow with a 1-2 quarter lag. And BDCs borrow from banks using credit facilities.

So the chain is: corporate stress → BDC losses → bank credit tightening → more corporate stress. A feedback loop.

---

## Why the Market Got It Wrong

So why did the stock market spike when the jobs report came out?

Because algorithms read the headline: "+130K vs +55K expected" and bought. Machines trade fast. They don't read footnotes.

But humans read the revision. And by 9:30 AM, the market started giving back the gains. Small caps — the Russell 2000 — spiked to $269, then faded to $266-267 by mid-morning.

The market is slowly re-pricing the reality: the "resilient labor market" was a statistical mirage.

---

## What This Changes

This matters for our thesis in three ways.

**First:** The employment cushion we thought existed... doesn't. We're not starting from a position of strength. We're starting from stagnation.

**Second:** The transmission mechanism from employment to consumer stress is **slower but deeper** than we thought. If you average 15K jobs per month for a year, you don't trigger a crisis. But you do bleed. Slowly. And by the time the data shows it, the damage is done.

**Third:** The danger window remains **Q2-Q3 2026**. Here's why: 

The WARN Act requires companies to give 60 days' notice before mass layoffs. WARN filings in Q4 2025 were the highest since 2009. Those filings become **actual layoffs** in Q1-Q2 2026. The pain is locked in.

Add to that: Challenger job cuts in January 2026 were 108,000 — the highest January since 2009. And hiring plans? 5,306. The **lowest January ever recorded**.

So companies are cutting aggressively and not hiring to replace. That's the recipe for claims to spike.

---

## The Transmission Path

Here's how this plays out:

**Step 1:** WARN filings materialize as actual layoffs (Q1-Q2 2026)

**Step 2:** Initial jobless claims breach 300,000 (our threshold)

**Step 3:** Long-term unemployment continues rising (people can't find new jobs)

**Step 4:** Consumer stress accelerates (unemployment insurance runs out)

**Step 5:** Credit card delinquencies and auto loan defaults spike (CARL's domain)

**Step 6:** Regional banks with consumer and CRE exposure take losses (REGINALD's domain)

We're currently between Step 1 and Step 2. Initial claims hit 231,000 last week — a 22,000 jump that breached our 230,000 threshold. If that sustains or accelerates toward 250,000, Step 2 is in motion.

---

## The Counter-Argument

Let me steelman the other side.

The unemployment rate is still 4.3%. That's not recessionary. Claims at 231K — even with the spike — are below the 300K level that historically signals serious trouble.

And the January report beat expectations. Yes, the revision was bad, but that's **backward-looking**. Maybe 2026 is different.

Here's the problem with that argument: the benchmark revision tells us the **baseline was wrong**. We thought the labor market entered 2026 with momentum. It didn't. It entered with stagnation.

And the leading indicators — temp employment (down 12% year-over-year), JOLTS openings (lowest since 2017), Challenger hiring plans (lowest ever), WARN filings (highest since 2009) — all point the same direction.

The labor market isn't collapsing. But it's not resilient either. It's fragile. And fragile systems break fast when stress hits.

**Small business surveys:** NFIB optimism is still showing "hard to fill positions," but the New York Fed data suggests that's lagging. If NFIB flips negative on hiring, the last pillar falls.

---

## The Bottom Line

Over one million jobs that we thought existed in 2025... didn't. The U.S. labor market averaged 15,000 jobs per month last year — effectively stagnation. Long-term unemployment is up 28%. Small businesses are cutting. Corporate bankruptcies are at COVID-era levels.

The "resilient labor market" was a story we told ourselves based on data that turned out to be wrong.

The real question now is: how long can a labor market that's been stagnating for a year hold together before something breaks?

We're about to find out.
