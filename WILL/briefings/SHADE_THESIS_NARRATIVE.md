# The Shadow Insurance Thesis — A Narrative Briefing

*Written for listening. Take your time with this one.*

---

Let me tell you a story about the biggest financial engineering project in American history. It's happening right now, it involves almost a trillion dollars, and most people have never heard of it.

## Part One: The Setup

Imagine you're sixty-two years old. You've worked your whole life, saved what you could, and now you're sitting across from a financial advisor who tells you about a product called a fixed annuity. You give an insurance company a lump sum — say, a hundred thousand dollars — and in return, they promise to pay you a guaranteed income for the rest of your life. It's safe. It's boring. It's insurance.

For a hundred years, that's exactly what it was. Insurance companies took your hundred thousand, bought Treasury bonds and high-quality corporate debt, earned a steady return, paid you your guaranteed rate, and kept the spread. The business was simple. The assets were liquid. The regulation was strict. It worked.

Then came 2008. Interest rates went to zero. And they stayed there. For over a decade.

This created an existential problem for life insurers. If you've promised retirees a three or four percent return, but Treasury bonds are paying half a percent, where does the money come from? The old model — buy safe bonds, earn the spread — was broken. Some companies merged. Some cut their rates. Some struggled.

And some got bought by private equity.

## Part Two: The Takeover

Starting around 2009, a new kind of deal started appearing in the financial world. Private equity firms — Apollo, KKR, Blackstone, Brookfield, Ares — began acquiring life insurance companies. Not because they suddenly cared about retirees. Because they saw something nobody else was paying attention to.

An insurance company is, at its core, a pool of money. Policyholders hand over their savings, and the insurer invests that money for decades. The liabilities are long-dated — people don't need their annuity payments for twenty, thirty years. And unlike a bank, where depositors can withdraw at any time, annuity holders face surrender charges — five, ten percent penalties — if they want their money back early. The money is, in industry terms, "sticky."

To a private equity firm, that's not insurance. That's permanent capital. It's a pool of money that doesn't leave, doesn't demand quarterly returns, and doesn't panic during a downturn. It's the holy grail of alternative asset management.

Apollo was the first to see it clearly. In 2009, they created Athene, a company purpose-built to collect annuity premiums and hand the money to Apollo to invest. By 2022, Apollo didn't just manage Athene — they merged entirely. Today, Athene has three hundred and sixty-three billion dollars in assets. It's one of the largest annuity providers in America. And one hundred percent of that money is managed by Apollo.

The others followed. KKR bought Global Atlantic — two hundred and seven billion. Brookfield bought American Equity — over a hundred billion. Blackstone partnered with Resolution Life — eighty-five billion. Ares built Aspida from scratch — twenty-seven billion and growing fast.

In total, private equity firms now control approximately seven hundred billion dollars in insurance assets. That's almost eight percent of the entire nine-trillion-dollar US insurance industry. They account for thirty-five percent of all new annuity sales. Up from seven percent in 2011.

This is not a niche strategy anymore. This is the industry.

## Part Three: The Alchemy

So what do these private equity firms actually do with grandma's retirement money?

They don't buy Treasury bonds. They don't buy high-grade corporate debt — at least, not primarily. They invest in what the industry calls "private credit." These are loans to middle-market companies, collateralized loan obligations, asset-backed securities, real estate debt, infrastructure loans — all originated by the PE firm's own lending platforms.

Apollo, for example, doesn't just manage Athene's money. They lend it through their own subsidiaries — MidCap Financial, Atlas SP, and others. They originate the loan, they structure the security, they rate it, and they put it on Athene's balance sheet. The insurer provides the funding. The asset manager provides the product. And the same parent company owns both sides of the transaction.

This creates what Apollo calls a "flywheel." More annuity sales mean more assets for Apollo to manage, which means more management fees, which means higher earnings, which attracts more annuity sales. It's a beautiful machine — as long as the assets perform.

And here's where the alchemy begins.

These private credit assets are, by their nature, illiquid. They don't trade on exchanges. There's no daily market price. So who decides what they're worth? The manager. Apollo tells you what Apollo's loans are worth. They use internal models, internal assumptions, and internal judgment. This is called "Level Three" accounting — valuations based on unobservable inputs.

In good times, this means the portfolio shows steady, predictable returns. No volatility. No scary markdowns. Investors love it. Regulators accept it. Rating agencies bless it.

But the absence of volatility is not the absence of risk. It's the concealment of risk.

## Part Four: The Shell Game

Now, even with favorable valuations, there's still the problem of capital requirements. Insurance regulators require companies to hold reserves — money set aside to guarantee they can pay claims even if things go wrong. The more risk you take, the more capital you need.

Private credit is riskier than Treasury bonds. So logically, an insurer loading up on private credit should need MORE capital, not less. This is where the architecture gets creative.

The solution is something called captive reinsurance. Here's how it works.

Athene, the primary insurer based in Iowa, writes your annuity. It has a legal obligation to hold reserves against that annuity. But instead of holding those reserves on its own balance sheet, Athene "cedes" the risk — transfers it — to an affiliated company. This affiliated company is a captive reinsurer, typically located in Bermuda, Vermont, or one of a handful of other friendly jurisdictions.

Once the risk is ceded, Athene takes a "reserve credit" — it removes the liability from its own books. This makes Athene's capital ratios look better. On paper, the risk has been transferred.

But where did it actually go?

The captive reinsurer in Bermuda is also owned by Apollo. And the "assets" backing the reserves in that captive aren't cash or bonds. They're something called "XOL assets" — Excess of Loss agreements. In plain English, these are contracts where one Apollo affiliate promises to cover losses for another Apollo affiliate. They're IOUs within the family.

A forensic accountant named Tom Gober examined one of these captives and found seven billion dollars in liabilities backed by two hundred million in real assets. The other six point eight billion? XOL entries. Accounting fiction.

And it gets more circular than that. The parent company often guarantees the XOL agreement. So the entity supposedly offloading the risk is actually backstopping the entity that's supposed to be absorbing the risk. If you trace the money in a circle, nobody is actually holding anything. It's a shell game.

One specific example: Athene has a captive in Vermont called Athene Re USA IV. Vermont's insurance commissioner granted it a "permitted practice" — a special exception — allowing it to count the face value of letters of credit as admitted assets. Without this single permitted practice, the captive would have failed its minimum capital requirements. One bureaucratic decision, in one state, is the difference between solvency and insolvency for an entity backing billions in policyholder obligations.

The scale of this is staggering. As of late 2025, Athene takes seventy point eight billion dollars in reserve credit from its affiliates. That's over forty percent of its total reserves. The Financial Stability Oversight Council reports that reserves ceded to offshore jurisdictions by the entire US life insurance industry have more than doubled since 2019, reaching one point one trillion dollars. Thirty-eight percent of the industry's total reserves now sit in Bermuda.

## Part Five: The Rating Agency Problem

There's another layer to this that most people don't know about. It involves a small credit rating agency called Egan-Jones.

When an insurance company buys a private credit asset, the amount of capital it must hold against that asset depends on the asset's credit rating. A triple-A rated bond requires almost no capital. A junk-rated loan requires a lot. The difference between "investment grade" and "speculative grade" isn't a small step — it's a cliff. Capital requirements can jump three hundred to five hundred percent at that boundary.

So the rating matters enormously. And for private credit — loans that don't trade publicly, that have no market price — the rating is the only thing standing between "this is a safe asset" and "this is a regulatory crisis."

Egan-Jones is one of the agencies that rates these private credit instruments. They're small — about twenty analysts. In 2024, those twenty analysts rated over three thousand private credit deals. That's a hundred and fifty complex transactions per analyst per year. For context, proper due diligence on a single private credit deal typically takes weeks.

The NAIC — the national association of insurance regulators — examined Egan-Jones ratings and found they were, on average, three notches higher than the NAIC's own internal assessments. In seven percent of cases, the gap was six or more notches. That's the difference between investment-grade and deep junk.

Senator Elizabeth Warren has launched a formal inquiry. The SEC is investigating "improper commercial influence" on Egan-Jones's rating procedures. The Department of Justice's Criminal Fraud Section — which has expanded to two hundred lawyers and recently ended a fifteen-year hiatus on corporate indictments — is reportedly building cases using testimony from the former CFO of 777 Partners, who has already pled guilty to fraud in a related scheme.

And in January 2026, the Bermuda Monetary Authority revoked Egan-Jones's recognition entirely. Any asset rated only by Egan-Jones is now treated in Bermuda as if it's rated below triple-C-minus — the lowest possible category.

Here's the nightmare scenario: if the DOJ indicts Egan-Jones as a corporation, it would lose its NRSRO status — its license to rate. Every rating it has ever issued would be revoked. Overnight. Three hundred and fifty billion dollars or more in assets held by all twenty-five of the top life insurance groups would need to be re-rated or treated as unrated. The capital requirements would explode. It would be the equivalent of a simultaneous credit downgrade across the entire insurance industry.

## Part Six: The Run Risk

So we have an industry built on illiquid assets, valued by the people who made them, backed by affiliate IOUs, rated by an agency under criminal investigation, and housed in jurisdictions that grant special exceptions to make the math work.

What breaks it?

A run.

Not the kind you see in movies, with people lining up outside a bank. Insurance runs work differently.

Athene doesn't just sell annuities to individual retirees. It also sells something called Funding Agreement-Backed Notes — FABNs — to institutional investors. Think Vanguard, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds. These are essentially deposits. The institution gives Athene money; Athene promises to return it with interest at a fixed maturity date.

Athene's FABN program has thirty-five billion dollars in capacity. And here's the critical difference from retail annuities: FABNs have no surrender charges. They mature on a schedule. If Athene can't refinance them — if the market for new notes closes because investors lose confidence — the company has to pay them off in cash.

Sixteen point five billion dollars in FABNs mature between 2026 and 2027. The specific dates: August 2026, March 2027, and August 2027.

Athene says it has a sixty-nine billion dollar liquidity buffer. That sounds like a fortress. But when you look closer, that number includes forty-nine point nine billion in "highly liquid assets" — some of which are pledged to the Federal Home Loan Bank as collateral and can't be sold. It includes FHLB borrowing capacity — theoretical access to credit, not actual cash. And seven point four billion is already drawn on FHLB advances.

In a crisis, "highly liquid" is a statement of faith, not a statement of fact.

The run works like this. Something triggers a loss of confidence — a downgrade, a regulatory action, a fraud discovery. The institutional investors, who are fiduciaries with an obligation to protect their clients, exercise their downgrade surrender clauses and demand their money back. They get paid first, at book value. One hundred cents on the dollar.

To generate cash, Athene sells its most liquid assets — its good bonds, its Treasuries. The portfolio that remains gets progressively worse — more illiquid, more concentrated in private credit. The remaining policyholders, the retirees, are left holding a bag of assets that nobody wants to buy.

If Athene needs to sell private credit to meet redemptions, those sales happen at fire-sale prices. And under accounting rules, those prices become observable market data. Every other insurer and fund holding the same or similar credits has to mark their books to those prices. The markdowns trigger more redemptions at other funds. More fire sales. More markdowns. A doom loop.

This isn't hypothetical. This exact sequence happened in 1991 with Executive Life — a junk-bond-fueled insurer that experienced a four-billion-dollar run in a single year. Annuitants were eventually paid seventy cents on the dollar. It happened again with PHL Variable — a PE-owned insurer that regulators seized in 2024 and discovered a two-point-two billion dollar hole. That liquidation is happening right now. Policyholders are facing over a hundred and twenty million in losses above what state guaranty funds will cover.

And today, we have the Blackstone headline. Their flagship private credit fund just lost one point seven billion dollars in outflows in a single month. The Financial Times describes it as "an exodus from the asset class." Blackstone can handle it. But the signal matters. When institutional investors start pulling money out of private credit, the "permanent capital" assumption that the entire PE-insurance model is built on starts to wobble.

## Part Seven: Why Apollo Specifically

All of the major PE-insurance firms are exposed to these dynamics. But Apollo is uniquely vulnerable, because Apollo IS Athene in a way that no other PE firm is their insurer.

Athene provides fifty-seven percent of Apollo's core operating earnings through spread-related income. Another forty percent of Apollo's management fees come from managing Athene's assets. In total, Athene-driven earnings account for over sixty percent of Apollo's equity value.

If Athene enters rehabilitation — if the Iowa insurance commissioner seizes control, which Iowa law gives them the power to do — several things happen simultaneously.

First, the commissioner can reject or renegotiate Apollo's management agreement. The fee stream that generates billions per year? It's at the commissioner's discretion. They could replace Apollo with a low-cost passive manager tomorrow.

Second, Athene qualifies as a "Significant Subsidiary" of Apollo under SEC definitions — it's more than ten percent of consolidated assets and income. A rehabilitation order for Athene would constitute an Event of Default under Apollo's own senior note indentures. Apollo has five point three billion in senior debt that could be accelerated — meaning creditors could demand immediate repayment.

Third, Apollo's common equity — the stock — would be repriced to reflect an asset management company that has lost its primary source of capital, its primary source of fees, and its primary source of earnings, while simultaneously facing a debt crisis.

At a hundred dollars a share, you'd be pricing Apollo as just its fee-related earnings from non-Athene clients, at a discount for the reputational and legal fallout. That's the $100 put thesis.

## Part Eight: The Four Paths

There are four independent ways this can unfold. Any one of them is sufficient. All four are converging in the 2026 window.

Path one: the FABN maturity wall. Sixteen and a half billion coming due in 2026-2027. If credit spreads blow out and Athene can't roll the notes, it's a liquidity crisis.

Path two: AG 55 forced disclosure. New regulation effective December 31, 2025. For the first time, insurers must explain exactly how offshore reinsurance reduces their reserves. If the attribution analysis reveals what Gober found — seven billion in liabilities backed by two hundred million in real assets — the market sees through the shell game.

Path three: Egan Jones indictment. DOJ indicts the company, NRSRO revoked, every rating disappears. Three hundred and fifty billion in assets need re-rating overnight. Capital requirements explode across the entire industry.

Path four: external shock. An oil price spike crushes Apollo's portfolio companies — chemicals, manufacturing, hospitality. Private credit marks deteriorate. Confidence cracks. Paths one through three accelerate.

## Part Nine: Where We Are Right Now

We are sitting at the intersection of all four paths. The Bermuda Monetary Authority has already revoked Egan-Jones recognition. The DOJ investigation is active. AG 55 filings are being prepared right now. The FABN maturity wall begins in eighteen months. And a war in the Middle East is disrupting sixteen million barrels per day of oil production while making the Strait of Hormuz uninsurable.

Meanwhile, Blackstone's investors are heading for the exits. One point seven billion in one month. The question isn't whether private credit has a problem. The question is whether the insurance companies holding a trillion dollars of this stuff can maintain the illusion of stability long enough for the maturity wall to pass.

Tom Gober, the forensic accountant who found the seven-billion-dollar hole, says the industry is "set up for fire sale dynamics."

Steve Eisman — the man who shorted subprime in 2008 — says private equity firms have turned insurance companies into vehicles that "offload liabilities to secret captives in Bermuda and the Caymans."

The Bank for International Settlements estimates a hundred-and-fifty-billion-dollar shortfall in a severe downturn — and that estimate was made before the Egan Jones crisis.

This is the shadow insurance thesis. The shadow is seven hundred billion dollars deep, stretched across a hundred and thirty-seven entities, in five jurisdictions, backed by assets that nobody can see, rated by an agency under criminal investigation, and held together by permitted practices that can be revoked with the stroke of a commissioner's pen.

The light is starting to come in.
