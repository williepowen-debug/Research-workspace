## Historical precedents in U.S. control of major canals and straits

The historical record is asymmetric: the U.S. has **rarely sought sovereign-style “ownership” of a strait**, but it did effectively hold **long-term sovereign-like rights over a canal zone** (Panama), and it has repeatedly run **naval escort / sea-control operations** near chokepoints (most notably during the 1980s Gulf tanker war). citeturn38search3turn0search7turn10search5

### Panama Canal from U.S. construction-era control to handover

The clearest precedent for sustained U.S. physical control of a major maritime chokepoint is the Panama Canal era: after the U.S. backed Panama’s separation from Colombia and obtained treaty rights, the U.S. built, administered, and defended the Canal Zone for decades, culminating in the 1977 treaties signed by entity["politician","Jimmy Carter","us president 1977-1981"] and Omar Torrijos that ended the Canal Zone (effective October 1, 1979) and set a definitive handover date of December 31, 1999. citeturn38search3turn38search16  
This is not just a legal footnote: the Canal Zone arrangement (and later the implementation of staged transfer) is the closest U.S. analogue to the idea of “taking over” or “running” an international chokepoint as an operating authority rather than merely policing it. citeturn38search3turn38search16

Two later Panama-related episodes are relevant as “military control” precedents rather than treaty governance:  
• In December 1989, under entity["politician","George H. W. Bush","us president 1989-1993"], the U.S. executed Operation Just Cause (preceded by troop increases under Operation Nimrod Dancer) in part to protect U.S. personnel and interests in Panama during the Noriega crisis—an episode where U.S. forces were positioned to ensure canal-area security in practice even though the canal’s treaty framework was already transitioning. citeturn38search14turn38search1  
• The formal final handover on December 31, 1999 ended U.S. control per the treaty schedule. citeturn38search3turn38search6turn38search16

### Suez Crisis and the U.S. role as an anti-seizure precedent

The 1956 Suez Crisis is often misremembered as a U.S. seizure episode; it is better understood as a **U.S. counter-precedent**: entity["politician","Dwight D. ### Persian Gulf tanker war escorts as a “sea-control without occupation” template

In 1987–1988, the U.S. entered the “Tanker War” phase of the Iran–Iraq War by escorting reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and conducting broader protective operations—an episode closer in mechanics to “securing Hormuz” than Panama/Suez, because it involved **naval escort, deterrence, and mine/missile threats** rather than territorial administration. citeturn0search7turn2search2turn10search5

This episode is directly relevant to contemporary Hormuz scenarios because it shows a realistic operational boundary: the U.S. can execute punitive strikes and escort operations, but **does not “own” the strait**; instead it seeks to establish temporary local sea control under persistent asymmetric threat. citeturn38search0turn10search5turn11search0

### Other relevant precedents that inform feasibility and risk

The 1991 Gulf War experience is a key adjunct precedent for **mine warfare and clearance timelines**. Contemporary U.S. naval analysis emphasizes that inexpensive sea mines can significantly constrain naval operations and that mine countermeasures can be prolonged—even after major combat ends. citeturn10search6turn10search10turn10search2

## What “taking over the Strait of Hormuz” would mean operationally

A key fact shaping any operational definition is that the Strait is geographically narrow at its navigable lanes and legally complex: it lies between entity["country","Iran","country"] and entity["country","Oman","country"], connects the Persian Gulf to the entity["place","Gulf of Oman","northwest indian ocean gulf"], and its traffic separation scheme uses **two 2-mile-wide shipping lanes with a 2-mile buffer** (a structure repeatedly cited in technical references). citeturn11search18turn10search4turn10search8

In practical military terms, “taking over” the Strait can mean at least three distinct (and increasingly escalatory) objectives:

First is **protected transit**: establish a convoy/escort regime where commercial ships can move with naval protection through the lanes, accepting that some residual risk remains. This is conceptually closest to the 1987–1988 escort model. citeturn0search7turn10search5turn38search0  

Second is **local sea control and coastal suppression**: reduce risk to a level where commercial insurers and shipowners will resume routine scheduling. That typically requires not only escorts but sustained suppression (and defense) against shore-based threats—missile batteries, drones, coastal radars, and fast-attack craft. citeturn10search5turn11search0turn11news38  

Third is **physical occupation of key terrain** (the most escalatory interpretation): seizing or neutralizing strategic islands/coastal positions that enable fires into the lanes. This would strongly resemble a broader war plan rather than a maritime security operation because it implies attacking or occupying sovereign territory. citeturn10search3turn11search19turn10search5

### Assets required

To execute the middle objective (“sea control + suppression”) at scale, the U.S. would typically require a layered force package:

Naval surface combatants for air/missile defense and escort (Aegis-capable destroyers/cruisers), submarines for undersea dominance and strike, and continuous ISR and maritime patrol (P-8-class equivalents, drones, satellites). citeturn10search5turn11search19turn10news38  

A decisive constraint is mine warfare. Analyses of Iran’s naval approach emphasize large mine inventories and the ability to deploy mines from small craft; the entity["organization","Defense Intelligence Agency","us defense intelligence"] has assessed Iran’s naval inventory and its asymmetric artillery/mine/small-boat posture, and summaries citing DIA reporting describe a mine stockpile “more than 5,000” and craft configured to lay mines. citeturn11search0turn11search4  

Mine countermeasures (MCM) therefore become foundational assets: dedicated MCM vessels, MCM helicopter/UUV systems, divers/EOD teams, and protected staging bases. This is complicated by the U.S. Navy’s recent transition away from Avenger-class forward-deployed MCM ships in Bahrain toward LCS-based and modular MCM concepts, a shift explicitly described in U.S. Navy communications. citeturn11search2turn11search6turn11search17  

### Timelines

If substantial forces are already in theater, escort operations can begin quickly; the limiting factor becomes whether (and how fast) risk can be reduced enough for commercial operators to return. Current reporting on Hormuz disruptions emphasizes that protecting the Strait over the long term is resource-intensive, and short-horizon surges do not automatically restore routine flows. citeturn11news38  

Mine clearance is the largest uncertainty in timelines. Historical mine-warfare experience in the Gulf indicates mines can restrict operations for months and require multinational mine-clearing contributions; the narrative record of Gulf War MCM operations shows prolonged clearance activity and continued mine discoveries well after major combat and liberation operations. citeturn10search2turn10search10turn10search6  

## Risks that persist even if the U.S. “controls” the Strait

Even with powerful naval forces, the Strait’s geometry and proximity to Iranian territory make it unusually suitable for asymmetric disruption. External analyses and U.S. government reporting converge on a threat mix of fast attack craft, drones, mines, and small submarines as the practical tools for disruption. citeturn11news38turn11search0turn10search16  

### Anti-ship missiles and drone saturation

Iran’s broader strike posture matters because the Strait’s lanes are narrow and predictable. DIA reporting highlights Iran’s large missile forces and their role as a strategic deterrent, and contemporary conflict reporting emphasizes the plausibility of sustained drone pressure even if missile inventories are stressed. citeturn11search8turn10news40  
This means that “control” is not a one-time event; it implies continuous air/missile defense and attrition management, including defending not just ships but also ports, oil terminals, and nearby bases. citeturn10news38turn11search19turn11search9  

### Mines as the pacing threat

The combination of a large mine stockpile and suitable platforms for mine laying is what turns the Strait into a “months, not days” operational problem. A small number of mines can create disproportionate fear among commercial operators because the consequence of a single detonation is catastrophic, and clearance requires painstaking detection/neutralization. citeturn11search4turn10search6turn10search10

DIA’s order-of-battle accounting describes Iran’s undersea fleet including Kilo-class submarines and numerous midget/coastal submarines, which are relevant because they can operate in constrained waters and complicate escort operations. citeturn11search0

## Legal framework for seizing or controlling an international waterway

### International law constraints

Under the U.N. Charter, the baseline rule is the prohibition on the “threat or use of force” against the territorial integrity or political independence of a state (Article 2(4)), with the primary exception of self-defense under Article 51 or specific Security Council authorization. citeturn11search34turn10search3  
For straits used for international navigation, the U.N. Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) sets out the “transit passage” regime: transit passage “shall not be impeded,” and coastal states are not to hamper transit passage (UNCLOS Part III; commonly cited via Articles 38 and 44). citeturn10search3turn10search7turn10search11  
Even analyses written for practitioners emphasize that, regardless of whether the U.S. has ratified UNCLOS, many of its straits provisions are treated as reflecting customary international law; Iran is a party to UNCLOS and thus bound by those transit passage obligations. citeturn10search14turn10search11turn11search34  

Critically, none of these rules grant a third party an automatic right to “seize” a strait; they instead recognize navigation rights. A U.S. action that involved occupying Iranian territory (even if framed as “securing shipping”) would therefore face a high risk of being characterized internationally as an unlawful use of force absent Security Council authorization or a clearly articulated self-defense necessity/proportionality claim tied to ongoing attacks. citeturn10search3turn10search5turn11search34  

### U.S. domestic authority and congressional role

Domestically, the U.S. President has extensive Commander-in-Chief powers, but sustained, escalatory operations typically trigger the War Powers Resolution framework, which requires reporting to Congress and constrains the duration of hostilities absent congressional authorization (with the commonly cited 60-day clock and an additional withdrawal period). citeturn1search9turn10news37  
Historically, U.S. practice shows that even when presidents initiate limited strikes or urgent deployments, major escalations that resemble “seizing” foreign territory or entering extended hostilities tend to create intense War Powers and authorization debates, especially when the operation is not purely defensive or is expected to be prolonged. citeturn10news37turn1search9  

## Effects if the U.S. physically secures Hormuz

### Whether oil flows resume immediately

In a limited escort scenario, some flows can resume quickly, but “immediately” in commercial terms is constrained by (a) shipowner risk tolerance, (b) crew willingness, (c) port functionality, and (d) whether insurers will quote tractable war-risk rates. ### Mine clearance and shipping lane security

Mine clearance is unlikely to be instantaneous. In a Strait scenario, the likely operational sequence is: reopen an initial “controlled corridor” for escorted transits while simultaneously conducting broader mine-hunting and threat-suppression to expand confidence. ### Iran’s ability to disrupt even under U.S. control

Most serious assessments conclude Iran may be unable to “completely” close the Strait in the face of determined U.S. action, but can likely sustain **disruption**—via sporadic missile/drone strikes, small-boat harassment, mining, or attacks on adjacent infrastructure—long enough to keep commercial confidence fragile. citeturn11search9turn10search5turn10news40

### Insurance market impacts

“Hormuz is uninsurable” is not an accurate static description; rather, war-risk coverage typically shifts by (i) exclusions, (ii) sharply higher premiums, and (iii) in some cases, temporary capacity withdrawal for specific routes/vessels. Current reporting around Hormuz disruptions emphasizes surging insurance costs and, in some accounts, insurers reducing coverage availability, which—regardless of the technical existence of policies—functionally suppresses traffic. citeturn11news38turn10news42
