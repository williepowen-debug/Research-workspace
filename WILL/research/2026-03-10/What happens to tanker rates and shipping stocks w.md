Tanker spot rates and related equities have historically stayed strong—or even spiked—during episodes where physical disruptions persist, even when crude prices correct sharply, because the key driver is **ton‑miles and utilization**, not the flat oil price level.[^1][^2][^3]

***

## 1. Historical precedents: oil down, disruption on

The cleanest recent analogue is 2020: oil prices collapsed (including the April 2020 negative print), while logistical dislocations and storage demand pushed tanker rates and stocks sharply higher.[^1]

- VLCC spot: Middle East–China VLCC rates reached about 180,000 USD/day in late March 2020, roughly doubling from earlier in the month despite the oil crash.[^1]
- Suezmax/Aframax: Suezmax and Aframax segment earnings “surged” alongside VLCCs as floating storage and longer routes absorbed tonnage, with midsize tonnage described as “firming up” on the back of VLCC strength.[^1]
- Tanker equities: DHT +38%, EURN +21%, FRO +17% over one month during the crash period, while the S\&P 500 returned 17%. TNK and other midsize‑focused names also benefited as Suezmax/Aframax demand spiked.[^1]
- BDTI: The same 2020 logistics shock is now the benchmark for current levels; Breakwave notes that by March 1, 2026, dirty freight (TD3C) was >200k/day with the BDTI at a new all‑time high, explicitly comparing to 2020.[^4]

Current Hormuz: by late February and early March 2026, VLCC TCEs were printing about 177k/day, with Aframax around 78k and Suezmax 129k—levels consistent with multi‑year highs. TD3C VLCC rates exceeded 200k/day, +440% YoY, with BDTI at a record. Those levels were achieved before the March 9–10 oil price crash and are tied to route disruption and vessel scarcity, not the prior price spike alone.[^5][^6][^7][^8][^4]

There are also Iraq/Iran‑related Gulf disruptions in earlier decades, but detailed daily VLCC/Suezmax/Aframax/BDTI time series by episode are in specialist databases; the observable pattern in modern, well‑documented cases (2020 and the current Hormuz closure) is that rates decouple positively from spot oil when the physical disruption and ton‑mile shock dominate.[^3][^1]

***

## 2. Main drivers and correlations

Empirical and industry work points to freight being driven more by **flows, distances, and utilization** than by the spot oil price itself.[^9][^10][^11][^12]

- Oil price level: A 2009 study on the West Africa–US trade finds about 60% of variation in tanker rates is associated with the price of oil carried, and 40% with other factors, but emphasizes that this link runs strongly through **term structure and inventories** (contango, storage incentives), not just flat price. UNCTAD likewise highlights a relationship between spot/futures prices, inventories, and tanker rates.[^11][^9]
- Ton‑miles: Changes in export shipments (barrels/day) from key regions like the Arabian Gulf correlate with tanker rates; a cited study finds a 1 mbpd change in Arabian Gulf exports generated a 25‑point change in VLCC rates.[^9]
- Utilization / availability: A quantitative model of the Arabian Gulf tanker market shows positive utilization shocks raise profits and time‑charter rates, with persistent effects on export volumes and bunker prices. DNV notes that by the end of 2025, tanker capacity utilization exceeded 90%, lifting freight “well above historic norms”.[^10][^12]
- Route disruptions / longer voyages: EIA (Nov 2025 data) shows VLCCs tied up on long‑haul routes squeeze Suezmax markets, which in turn tighten Aframaxes as tonnage cascades, explicitly linking higher rates to ships being pulled into longer trades. Argus and BIMCO comment that expanded Mideast Gulf VLCC trade for India pulled capacity off Atlantic routes and lifted Suezmax and Aframax rates. Hormuz‑related diversions are now creating similar multi‑basin ripple effects.[^7][^13][^8][^3]

Putting this together:

- Oil price level is **imperfectly** correlated; roughly half of rate variance in some historical samples relates to oil price/term structure, but the direct correlation is weaker than that with flows, inventories, and utilization.[^11][^9]
- Ton‑miles and utilization (including shadow‑fleet sequestration) are the **dominant** structural drivers in current conditions, with route disruptions acting as a mechanical multiplier by lengthening voyages and tying up hulls.[^14][^13][^10][^3]

If you had to rank correlations, the literature and current market commentary suggest:

1) fleet utilization / ton‑miles, 2) route disruptions (which act through utilization/ton‑miles), 3) oil price level and term structure (via storage economics and inventories).[^12][^10][^3][^9][^11]

***

## 3. Current setup: Hormuz closed, oil crashes

Hormuz transits have fallen by about 92% in nine days, from ~60 tankers/day to ~5, representing the largest oil supply disruption on record by flow volume. Vessel‑tracking data show hundreds of tankers stranded in the Gulf and around 200 tankers functionally immobilized as of March 5, 2026. VLCC day‑rate averages in February hit ~110,854 USD/day, the best February on record, after the best January in 20 years, with subsequent prints above 200,000 USD/day as Hormuz shut.[^6][^15][^16][^8][^7][^4]

Given that:

- The ton‑mile effect from rerouting Middle East‑linked flows to West Africa, Americas, and North Sea, and from longer routes around the Cape of Good Hope, remains enormous and **does not reverse just because Brent sells off 10–20%** if the chokepoint stays closed.[^17][^5][^4]
- Pre‑existing fleet tightness and older age distribution (high share of vessels >15–20 years) leave little slack to re‑optimize when voyages lengthen and shadow tonnage is ring‑fenced.[^10][^14]

So if crude drops another 10–20% on policy jawboning or demand fears but Hormuz stays effectively closed:

- a) Rates holding: The mechanical case is that VLCC/Suezmax/Aframax rates **stay elevated** as long as (i) flows are still moving via longer routes and (ii) utilization remains high; current reports explicitly tie current record BDTI and TD3C levels to route disruption and vessel scarcity, not just high oil prices.[^17][^7][^4][^3]
- b) Rates dropping: Rates would soften if the oil price crash coincides with **actual volume cuts** (producers shut in, refiners reduce runs) large enough to offset the ton‑mile shock. But with traffic through Hormuz already collapsed, additional short‑term price downside is being driven mainly by expectations and policy signaling, not an immediate restoration of flows.[^16][^6]

Historically (2020), tanker rates and stocks *out‑performed* during an oil crash once floating storage, congestion, and dislocations tightened utilization. In the current episode, absent an abrupt reopening of Hormuz or a coordinated large production shut‑in, a further oil price drop by itself is more likely **neutral to mildly supportive** (via contango and storage incentives) than outright bearish for dirty tanker rates over the near term.[^2][^4][^9][^1]

***

## 4. Shadow fleet and sanctions easing

Breakwave estimates that around 15% of VLCCs and Suezmaxes and roughly 25% of Aframax/LR2 tonnage have migrated into the shadow or restricted fleet, largely servicing sanctioned Russian and Iranian flows. You noted that ~68% of Russian crude is on shadow tonnage, which aligns with that broad picture.[^14]

If Russian sanctions are eased and shadow fleet vessels become “legitimate”:

- Logistical capacity: The **physical hulls already exist and are already moving barrels**, so global seaborne capacity in deadweight‑ton terms does not suddenly expand; it is **relabeled**, not created.[^14]
- Practical effect: The key change is **flexibility**. Some mainstream cargoes currently using “white” ships might migrate onto ex‑shadow hulls, marginally increasing effective supply to the regulated pool, especially if insurers and class societies accept them again. That modestly **loosens** constraints over time but does not flood the market with new ships.[^14]
- Offset: If sanctions easing also re‑routes cargoes geographically (e.g., more Russian barrels to Europe vs long‑haul to India/China), that could **reduce ton‑miles**, which is more structurally bearish than the relabeling itself.[^3][^14]

So the pure relabeling is mostly **neutral to mildly bearish** for mainstream tanker owners; the **route reconfiguration** (shorter hauls) is where real downside to rates would come from.

***

## 5. Orderbook, fleet age, supply tightness

Industry and class‑society data show a tight supply picture despite a recent pickup in orders.

- Orderbook: BIMCO data cited by Argus/Breakwave indicate the crude tanker orderbook‑to‑fleet ratio reached about 14.1% by late 2025, the highest since 2016. Many of these deliveries are scheduled for 2027–2028 and are expected primarily to **replace older ships**, limiting net fleet growth in the near term.[^13]
- Utilization: DNV reports that by end‑2025, tanker capacity utilization exceeded 90%, supporting above‑normal freight rates.[^10]
- Fleet age: DNV notes that 23% of all tankers were over 20 years old and 45% were 15+ years as of end‑2025. In crude, about 40% of VLCCs and Suezmaxes were older than 15 years, and in the Aframax segment the share was over 45%. Older tonnage is more likely to be scrapped, relegated to shadow trades, or face regulatory/insurance constraints, effectively tightening “clean” supply.[^10]

Net takeaway: Near‑term deliverables are modest versus a large, aging fleet and high utilization, which is structurally supportive for rates unless demand collapses.

## 6. Scorpio Tankers (STNG): spot vs time charter

Scorpio is a **product** tanker name, but its earnings sensitivity to spot markets is high.

- Fleet and charter mix: STNG’s Q4‑2025 earnings materials show a fleet heavily weighted to spot exposure, with only a limited number of LR2/MR vessels fixed on multi‑year time charters (e.g., LR2s chartered out at 29,000–35,000 USD/day for 5‑year terms, and a bareboat at ~21,000 USD/day TCE until ~2037). The company has added a few more LR2 time charter‑outs in late 2025 and early 2026 at 30,500–33,000 USD/day, but these cover only a subset of the fleet.[^18][^19][^20]
- Implied spot share: Management presentations and fleet lists (Q4‑25) indicate that the **majority of vessels remain on spot or short‑term charters**, with roughly “20‑something” ships on term charters out of a fleet exceeding 100 product tankers, implying on the order of ~75–85% of capacity exposed to spot over a 6–12 month horizon.[^20]
- Earnings pass‑through: With that mix, changes in product tanker spot rates typically flow through quarterly TCE and earnings with a **lag of 1–2 quarters**, depending on voyage duration and how fast existing short contracts roll off. For the LR2s that sometimes trade dirty and are directly exposed to the crude cycle, the time charter‑out share dampens volatility, but the remaining spot LR2s and MR/Handy fleet retain high gearing to spot swings.[^20][^2]

Given your focus on dirty names (FRO, EURN, DHT, TNK), note that Scorpio’s leverage is to **refined products and LR2/MR dynamics**; it benefits from many of the same ton‑mile and disruption forces, but the linkage to crude dislocations is indirect.

## 7. What this implies for your scenario

If WTI/Brent drop another 10–20% on sanctions‑easing headlines while:

- Hormuz remains 90%+ shut,
- rerouting via Cape of Good Hope continues, and
- capacity utilization stays >90% with a constrained, aging fleet,

then history and current structural evidence say:

- VLCC/Suezmax/Aframax rates and BDTI are **more likely to remain high or volatile‑sideways** than to track oil lower one‑for‑one, because ton‑miles, shadow‑fleet sequestration, and utilization are doing the heavy lifting.[^7][^4][^10][^14]
- Tanker equities can decouple positively from oil in such conditions, as in 2020, especially for owners with high spot exposure (FRO, EURN, DHT, TNK) and moderate leverage.[^2][^3][^1]
- The main bearish path for tankers is not “oil down 15%” in isolation, but “oil down because flows normalize and routes shorten,” which would explicitly unwind the ton‑mile and utilization shock.

For the time horizon you care about (sounds like weeks to a few months), the single biggest swing variable is how long Hormuz stays effectively closed and whether producers/refiners cut volumes aggressively in response.
