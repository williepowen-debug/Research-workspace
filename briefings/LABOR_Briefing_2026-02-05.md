# LABOR Briefing: The Employment Break
*Executive briefing for audio listening — February 5, 2026*
*Estimated listening time: 25 minutes*

---

## Opening

Let me walk you through something important that's happening right now in the American labor market. This isn't a forecast or a prediction — this is what the data is actually showing us today, and what it means for everything else we're tracking.

The employment picture in America is one of the most confusing things to read right now, because it depends entirely on which numbers you're looking at. If you glance at the headlines, everything looks fine. Unemployment at four point four percent. Weekly claims at two hundred nine thousand. The economy added jobs every month last year. Nothing to see here, move along.

But if you dig beneath the surface — and I mean really dig — you find a completely different story. A story of five hundred eighty thousand announced layoffs. A story of temp employment collapsing at rates we haven't seen in years. A story of job openings falling to their lowest level since twenty seventeen. A story that ends, eventually, at the doorstep of every regional bank in America.

So let me tell you that story.

---

## Part One: The Surface

Let's start with what the optimists see.

The unemployment rate is four point four percent. That's elevated from where it was a year ago — it's up about three tenths of a point — but it's still historically low. In a normal economy, we'd call this full employment.

Initial jobless claims are running around two hundred nine thousand per week. That's actually quite healthy. During recessions, we typically see claims spike above three hundred thousand, sometimes four hundred thousand or more. At two hundred nine thousand, the claims data is flashing green.

And yes, the economy is still adding jobs. December came in at fifty thousand, which is weak, but it's positive. We haven't had a negative print yet. The labor market is bending, but it's not breaking.

That's the optimist's view. And here's the thing — it's not wrong. Those numbers are real. The economy is not in freefall.

But those numbers are also incomplete. And in some cases, they're misleading.

---

## Part Two: The Hidden Stress

Now let me show you what's happening beneath the surface.

First, let's talk about the U-6 rate. Most people focus on the headline unemployment rate, which is called U-3. But there's a broader measure called U-6 that includes people who are working part-time but want full-time work, plus people who've given up looking for jobs temporarily. U-6 is at eight point four percent, up almost a full percentage point from a year ago. That's a much bigger move than the headline number suggests.

Part-time employment for economic reasons — meaning people who want full-time work but can only find part-time — is at five point three million, up nearly a million over the past year. These aren't people choosing to work part-time. These are people whose hours got cut or who couldn't find the full-time job they wanted.

Long-term unemployment — people out of work for twenty-seven weeks or more — is at one point nine million, up four hundred thousand over the year. And here's the key insight: that's now twenty-six percent of all unemployed people. More than a quarter of the unemployed have been out of work for over six months. In a strong labor market, you'd expect people to find jobs quickly. They're not.

So while the headline unemployment rate looks manageable, the people who are unemployed are staying unemployed longer, and more people are getting pushed into part-time work when they want full-time.

---

## Part Three: The Leading Indicators

Now let me walk you through the leading indicators — the signals that tell us where the labor market is going, not where it's been.

The single most important leading indicator is temporary employment. Temp workers are the first to be cut when companies sense trouble, because they're easy to let go. And temp employment is down twelve percent year-over-year. That's about four hundred thousand jobs lost in the temp sector. Even more concerning, the decline was accelerating as of the November data. It's not stabilizing — it's getting worse.

Historically, temp employment leads the broader labor market by three to six months. If temp is collapsing now, the rest of the labor market typically follows. This is one of the most reliable leading indicators we have, and it's flashing red.

Second, look at job postings. The Indeed Job Postings Index is down five point two percent year-over-year. Postings are only six percent above February twenty twenty levels — despite almost a decade of economic and population growth since then. Companies are not posting jobs at the rate they were.

Third — and this just came out today — JOLTS data for December showed job openings fell to six point five million. That's the lowest level since December twenty seventeen. A year ago, there were seven point four million openings. We've lost almost a million job openings in twelve months.

And here's the number that really matters: the ratio of job openings to unemployed workers is now zero point eight seven. There are seven point five million unemployed Americans and only six point five million job openings. For the first time since February twenty twenty-one, there are more unemployed people than jobs available. The game of musical chairs that is the labor market? The music has stopped, and there aren't enough chairs.

## Part Four: The Layoff Pipeline

Now let's talk about what's already locked in.

Challenger Gray and Christmas tracks announced layoffs. In twenty twenty-five, companies announced one point two million job cuts — up fifty-eight percent from twenty twenty-four. That's the highest since twenty twenty and the seventh highest on record going back decades. 

The fourth quarter of twenty twenty-five alone saw two hundred sixty thousand announced cuts — the highest fourth quarter since two thousand eight, the year of the financial crisis. We're not talking about a normal environment here.

And here's the part people miss: announced layoffs take time to show up in the data. Under the WARN Act, companies with more than a hundred employees have to give sixty days notice before mass layoffs. So when a company files a WARN notice in December, those workers don't actually lose their jobs until February. The layoffs announced in the fourth quarter of twenty twenty-five are just now hitting payrolls in the first and second quarters of twenty twenty-six.

The pipeline is full. The pain is locked in through the first half of this year. Nothing can stop it — those WARN notices have already been filed.

---

## Part Five: The Sector Story

Let me break down where the cuts are coming from, because it's not just one sector.

Federal government: three hundred seven thousand cuts, representing twenty-four percent of all announced layoffs in twenty twenty-five. This is the DOGE effect — Musk's cost-cutting initiative targeting federal agencies. USAID has been effectively eliminated. IRS, EPA, CDC, FEMA, CISA — all have been hit. Two point two million federal civilian workers plus roughly five million contractors are at risk. This is the largest federal restructuring in American history.

Technology: two hundred forty-five thousand cuts. Amazon announced sixteen thousand in January alone. Intel has cut twenty-five thousand. Microsoft is at twenty-two thousand and counting. The cumulative tech layoffs since ChatGPT launched in November twenty twenty-two have now exceeded five hundred thousand. AI isn't creating jobs fast enough to offset the ones it's eliminating.

Logistics: UPS alone is cutting seventy-eight thousand jobs over twenty twenty-five and twenty twenty-six. This isn't cyclical — it's structural. UPS is building automated mega-hubs that cost twenty-eight percent less to operate than conventional facilities. Sixty-eight percent of their volume will run through automation by end of twenty twenty-six. The freight sector has been in recession for forty-five months. These jobs are not coming back when volumes recover.

Auto: Nissan twenty thousand, Ford eleven thousand, GM one and a half thousand, Bosch thirteen thousand. This isn't company-specific — it's coordinated. The entire sector is contracting simultaneously.

Services and consumer: Accenture eleven thousand, PwC five thousand six hundred, Nestle sixteen thousand, Target nearly two thousand. Even pharma is cutting — Novo Nordisk nine thousand.

Add it all up: roughly five hundred eighty thousand announced layoffs from late twenty twenty-four through early twenty twenty-six. This is not a blip. This is a structural repricing of labor across the American economy.

## Part Six: The Barbell Economy

Here's where it gets complicated, and why the headline numbers are so misleading.

We don't have a synchronized recession. We have what I call a barbell economy — stress concentrated at both ends with something different in the middle.

On one end of the barbell, you have large corporations cutting aggressively. The Fortune 500, big tech, logistics giants — they're all in cost-cutting mode. That's where the five hundred eighty thousand layoffs are coming from.

On the other end, you have manufacturing. Output is growing, but jobs are shrinking. ISM Manufacturing Employment has been below fifty — meaning contraction — for twenty-eight consecutive months. This is a jobless expansion. Companies are producing more with fewer workers through automation and productivity improvements.

But here's the weird part: in the middle, small businesses are actually trying to hire. The NFIB Small Business Survey shows thirty-three percent of owners report positions they can't fill — above the two thousand seven peak. Ninety-one percent of hiring owners say they find few or no qualified applicants.

So you have large companies cutting, manufacturing automating away jobs, but small businesses can't find workers. How do you reconcile that?

Skills mismatch and geography. The federal worker laid off in Washington DC doesn't easily become the plumber a small business needs in rural Ohio. The tech engineer let go in San Francisco isn't going to fill the healthcare aide position in Florida.

This is why claims stay low while layoffs pile up. Small businesses aren't laying off — they can't find workers to hire in the first place. But that doesn't mean the large corporate layoffs don't matter. They do. Those workers have to go somewhere.

## Part Seven: The Sentiment Signal

There's one more piece of data I want to walk you through, because it's capturing something the official statistics miss.

Google searches for "severance package" are at an all-time high. Not elevated — all-time high. Higher than the pandemic. Higher than the global financial crisis of two thousand eight. Searches for "layoffs" are at ninety-two, approaching the pandemic peak of one hundred and above the financial crisis level of eighty-five.

This is real-time sentiment data. People don't search for "severance package" unless they're expecting to need one or are in the middle of negotiating one. This tells us that the anxiety — the fear — is at historic levels, even if it hasn't all shown up in the official unemployment statistics yet.

There's a concept called the "vibe-cession gap." One in three American workers reports feeling anxious about losing their job. They're employed, but they're behaving as if they're about to be unemployed. They're cutting spending. They're not making big purchases. They're building precautionary savings.

This matters because consumer spending is seventy percent of GDP. If employed workers start behaving like unemployed workers, you get the economic damage of a recession without the official recession statistics. The vibes are bad, and vibes affect behavior.

## Part Eight: Why This Matters for Everything Else

Let me connect this back to the bigger picture.

Employment is not just one variable among many. Employment is the master variable — the trigger that activates latent stress across the entire financial system.

Here's how it works:

Start with consumers. Thirty-seven percent of Americans can't cover a four hundred dollar emergency expense. Sixty percent live paycheck to paycheck. When these people lose their jobs, they don't have a buffer. Within zero to three months for hourly workers, two to five months for salaried workers, they start missing payments. Credit card delinquencies rise first — that's the lowest priority in the payment hierarchy. Then auto loans. Then eventually mortgages.

That's the consumer transmission — employment shock converts latent vulnerability into actual distress.

Then move to banks. Regional banks in particular are exposed on multiple fronts. They hold the commercial real estate loans. They hold the consumer credit portfolios. They hold the lines of credit to Business Development Companies. When employment breaks, all of these portfolios deteriorate simultaneously.

Our research has found that if initial claims breach three hundred thousand sustained, or if unemployment hits five percent, every regional bank we've flagged as at-risk escalates simultaneously. It's not a question of which bank has problems — it's a question of when the trigger fires.

And finally, the market structure. When unemployment rises above five percent, something changes in market flows. 401(k) contributions slow down because unemployed people stop contributing. Hardship withdrawals spike — they're already at all-time highs at nearly five percent of participants. The structural bid from passive flows that has supported markets for years begins to reverse.

Employment is the domino that knocks over everything else.

## Part Nine: The Timeline

So when does this play out?

The leading indicators are telling us the danger window is the second and third quarters of twenty twenty-six. Here's why:

Temp employment typically leads payrolls by three to six months. Temp collapsed through the end of twenty twenty-five and into early twenty twenty-six. Three to six months from now is Q2 to Q3.

The WARN pipeline is full. Q4 twenty twenty-five filings become Q1 and Q2 actuals. Those workers are being laid off now and over the next few months. But many of them have severance packages — typically three to six months of runway. When that severance runs out, they file for unemployment. So claims could spike in Q2 to Q3 as severance exhausts.

ADP just printed twenty-two thousand versus expectations of forty-five thousand. JOLTS just showed openings at their lowest since twenty seventeen. NFP comes out next week. If these prints continue to deteriorate, we may be closer to the inflection than the timeline suggests.

The consensus still believes the labor market is resilient. Today's JOLTS print might start to change that. Friday's... actually, the February NFP is delayed to February eleventh due to the government shutdown. That print will be important.

Let me close with what we're monitoring.

Tomorrow — February sixth — we get weekly initial claims. We're watching for any breach of two hundred thirty thousand as an early warning.

Tomorrow we also get Kforce earnings. Kforce is a temp staffing company. They're a bellwether for temp employment trends. We have a prediction — seventy percent confidence — that they guide down on twenty twenty-six expectations. If they do, it validates the temp collapse thesis.

The employment break isn't a single event — it's a sequence. Job postings decline, then temp employment falls, then hours get cut, then claims rise, then NFP misses, then unemployment rises. We appear to be in stage two or three of that sequence right now. Postings are down, temp is collapsing, hours are starting to decline. Claims and NFP are next.
