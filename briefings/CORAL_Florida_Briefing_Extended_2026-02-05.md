## Part One: Setting the Stage

Alright, let me take you through something we've been working on today that I think is genuinely important. This is going to be a longer conversation because the material deserves it, and honestly, I think hearing it laid out in full will help both of us understand where we stand.

We've been building this research operation for a while now, tracking what we call the "convergence thesis" — this idea that multiple independent stress channels are all pointing toward regional banks as the place where risk accumulates. We've got employment stress through LABOR, consumer credit through CARL, commercial real estate through CREED, private credit through BROCK, Japan through SAM, and so on.

Today we went deep on Florida. And what we found doesn't just add another channel to the list — it actually changes how we think about one of the most important pieces of the puzzle. So let me walk you through it, starting from the beginning.

### Why Florida Matters in the First Place

If you think about regional bank exposure geographically, Florida is one of the most interesting states to focus on. It's the third most populous state in the country. It has massive real estate exposure — both residential and commercial. It has unique insurance dynamics because of hurricane risk. And it has a demographic profile that matters: a lot of retirees, a lot of people on fixed incomes, a lot of wealth concentration in coastal areas.

For a regional bank that wants to grow, Florida has been irresistible. Population growth was strong for years. Real estate values were climbing. Corporate relocations were bringing new businesses. Miami became a financial hub in its own right.

But Florida also has risks that other states don't have. The hurricane exposure is obvious. Less obvious — until recently — was the structural maintenance issue. And that's where our story really begins.

### The Surfside Collapse: The Catalyst for Everything

On June 24th, 2021, at about 1:22 in the morning, the Champlain Towers South condominium in Surfside, Florida partially collapsed. Ninety-eight people died. It was the deadliest structural building failure in American history since the 1981 Hyatt Regency walkway collapse in Kansas City.

The building was forty years old. It had known structural issues — engineers had been warning about concrete deterioration and corrosion for years. But the condo association had been deferring maintenance, in part because the repairs were expensive and in part because Florida law allowed them to.

See, before Surfside, Florida condo associations had this incredible power: they could vote to waive their reserve contributions. Every year, the board could say "we don't need to fund reserves for the roof, for the foundation, for the elevator — we'll just keep the monthly fees low and deal with it later." And unit owners loved this because it kept their costs down.

The problem is that "later" kept getting pushed further and further away. Buildings that should have been setting aside money for thirty years of maintenance were operating on shoestring budgets. And when you do that for decades, you end up with Champlain Towers. You end up with concrete that's crumbling, rebar that's corroded, structures that are fundamentally unsafe.

After Surfside, Florida had to act. And they did — aggressively.

### The Legislative Response: SB 4-D and Its Children

In 2022, Florida passed Senate Bill 4-D. This was the direct response to Surfside, and it was sweeping.

The law created something called Milestone Structural Inspections. Any building three stories or taller has to get a professional inspection when it hits thirty years of age — or twenty-five years if it's within three miles of the coastline, because salt air accelerates corrosion. The inspection has two phases: Phase One is visual, and if they find problems, Phase Two involves more invasive testing.

The law also created the Structural Integrity Reserve Study, or SIRS. This is a financial document that says: here are all the major structural components of your building, here's how much it will cost to replace them, here's how many years of useful life they have left, and here's how much you need to be setting aside each year to be fully funded when those replacement dates arrive.

But here's the really important part — and this is what changed everything. Starting January 1st, 2025, associations can no longer vote to waive or underfund their reserves for structural items. That decades-long practice of kicking the can down the road? It's now illegal.

Think about what that means. You've got thousands of buildings across Florida that have been underfunding maintenance for twenty, thirty, forty years. Suddenly they're required — by law — to start collecting what they should have been collecting all along. The catch-up is brutal.

Let me give you a concrete example to make this real. Imagine a building with a hundred units. The roof needs to be replaced in five years and the replacement cost is two million dollars. If the association had been properly funding reserves from day one — let's say over a twenty-five year roof lifespan — they would have been collecting eighty thousand dollars a year, or eight hundred dollars per unit per year. That's manageable.

But if they waived reserves the whole time, they now have zero in the bank and five years until the roof needs replacing. Under the new law, they need to collect four hundred thousand dollars per year — four thousand dollars per unit per year — just for the roof. And that's one of nine mandatory components. Multiply across all of them, and you're talking about catch-up assessments that can reach thirty thousand, fifty thousand, even over a hundred thousand dollars per unit.

For a retiree who bought their condo twenty years ago and has been living on Social Security, a fifty thousand dollar special assessment is not "inconvenient." It's impossible.

### The "Glitch Bill" and Subsequent Refinements

After SB 4-D passed, there was immediate pushback. Some of the deadlines were unrealistic — there literally weren't enough engineers in Florida to inspect all the buildings that needed inspections. So in 2023, they passed what people called the "glitch bill," Senate Bill 154, which extended some deadlines and clarified some requirements.

Then in 2025, House Bill 913 made further adjustments. They added a provision that allows associations to pause reserve contributions for up to two years after completing major repairs — which gives some breathing room. They created requirements for associations to maintain online accounts with the state, increasing transparency.

But the core mandate — no more waiving reserves — stayed in place. That's the structural change that matters. That's the thing that's already in motion and can't be reversed.

### The December 31, 2025 Deadline

So where are we now? The deadline for completing your Structural Integrity Reserve Study was December 31, 2025. We're past it. And the deadline for adopting a budget that fully funds reserves applied to any budget adopted on or after January 1, 2025. We're past that too.

Associations all over Florida are now in the process of discovering exactly how bad their situation is. Some of them knew already — the responsible boards had been tracking this for years. But many associations are just now getting their SIRS reports back and realizing that they have funding gaps in the millions of dollars.

This isn't speculation about what might happen. This is happening right now, as we speak.

## Part Two: The Original Doom Loop vs. The New Reality

### What We Thought Was Going to Happen

When I first started looking at Florida as part of the REGINALD thesis, I was focused on Citizens Property Insurance. Citizens is the state-created insurer of last resort — if you can't get coverage in the private market, Citizens will write you a policy.

The problem was that Citizens had grown enormous. At its peak in October 2023, they had one point four two million policies and somewhere around six hundred seventy-eight billion dollars in total insured value. That's not a small insurance company — that's one of the largest property insurers in the country.

And here's what made it scary: if a major hurricane hit and Citizens couldn't pay all the claims out of their surplus, they had a mechanism to raise money. First, they could levy a fifteen percent surcharge on their own policyholders. But if that wasn't enough, they could assess up to ten percent on ALL property insurance policies in Florida — including policies written by private insurers.

That assessment is essentially a senior lien on cash flows. If you're a Florida property owner with a mortgage, that assessment competes with your mortgage payment for your available income. And if you can't pay both, the assessment doesn't go away.

So the doom loop I was envisioning went like this: Major hurricane hits. Citizens gets depleted. Assessments get triggered. Every Florida property owner suddenly has a massive new expense. Some of them can't pay their mortgages anymore. Delinquencies spike. Banks that are heavily exposed to Florida real estate — banks like Valley National, Seacoast, BankUnited — start taking losses.

It was a compelling thesis. But today's research showed me that the story has changed.

### The Depopulation Success Story

What actually happened over the past two years is that Citizens shrank. Dramatically. They went from one point four two million policies down to somewhere between three hundred eighty-five and three hundred ninety-five thousand. That's a seventy-three percent reduction.

How did they do this? Through what they call "depopulation" — actively pushing policies off their books and into the private market. They have takeout programs where private insurers can bid on blocks of Citizens policies. And in 2022, the legislature changed the rules so that if a private insurer offers you a policy at a rate within twenty percent of your Citizens renewal rate, you're no longer eligible to stay with Citizens. You have to take the private policy.

This worked. Private insurers came back to Florida. New companies entered the market. Existing companies expanded. Over two hundred fourteen billion dollars in exposure moved from the state-backed insurer to private carriers.

On paper, this looks like fantastic news. The state's contingent liability dropped by seventy-three percent. The doom loop I was worried about became much less likely. A hurricane that might have bankrupted Citizens in 2023 is now spread across dozens of private insurers who each hold a smaller piece of the risk.

But here's the thing — risk doesn't disappear. It just moves.

### Where the Risk Went

All that exposure that used to be at Citizens is now sitting with private insurers. Companies like Slide Insurance, which has grown explosively by absorbing failed carriers and Citizens takeout policies. Companies like Tower Hill, American Integrity, Universal Property and Casualty, Heritage.

And the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation is watching these companies closely. They have something called the Insurer Stability Unit that maintains "enhanced monitoring" on companies they're concerned about. As of January 2026, fourteen companies are on that enhanced monitoring list. That's roughly fifteen percent of the domestic property insurance market under close regulatory scrutiny.

Now, I should say — there's good news here too. There were no property insurer insolvencies in 2025. That's remarkable, because in 2022 and 2023, we had a wave of failures: United Property and Casualty, FedNat, Weston, Avatar, St. Johns. The market was hemorrhaging.

The insurance reforms — particularly Senate Bill 2-A, which eliminated one-way attorney fees and assignment of benefits abuse — seem to be working. Litigation against insurers is down twenty-six percent year over year. Loss ratios are improving. Reinsurance costs actually ticked down slightly. Citizens is even recommending a two point six percent rate cut for 2026, which would be their first decrease since 2015.

So the insurance channel is stabilizing. Not healthy, but stabilizing. And that means it's no longer the primary trigger for the doom loop. It's become what I'm calling an "amplifier" — something that could make an existing crisis worse, rather than something that initiates the crisis on its own.

### The Primary Trigger: It's Not Weather Anymore

Here's the key insight from today's research: the thing that's going to stress Florida real estate and the banks exposed to it isn't meteorological. It's regulatory. And it's already happening.

The condo reserve mandate is the trigger. The SIRS deadline has passed. Associations are discovering their funding gaps. Special assessments are being levied. Owners are starting to default. And there's nothing speculative about this — it's a compliance timeline that has already begun.

Let me say that again because it's important: we're not predicting a hurricane. We're not predicting an insurance company failure. We're observing a structural repricing of Florida's aging condo stock that is mandated by law and is already in progress.

## Part Three: The Mechanics of the Condo Crisis

### The Blacklist

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — the government-sponsored enterprises that back most residential mortgages in America — maintain what's informally called a "blacklist." Officially it's the "unavailable list" or the "ineligible projects list."

If your condo building doesn't meet their standards, Fannie and Freddie won't purchase or guarantee mortgages on units in that building. And if they won't touch it, most conventional lenders won't either — because they can't sell the loan to the secondary market.

What makes a building ineligible? Inadequate reserves. Failed structural inspections. Insufficient insurance. Excessive litigation. High delinquency rates. Basically, any sign that the building is financially or structurally distressed.

As of our research, one thousand four hundred thirty-eight Florida condo associations are on this blacklist. And about half of them — six hundred ninety-six — are concentrated in the Miami-Dade and Palm Beach corridor.

Think about what this means for someone trying to sell their unit in one of these buildings. Their buyer can't get a conventional mortgage. The only people who can buy are cash buyers — and cash buyers know they have leverage, so they demand a steep discount. We're talking twenty to forty percent below what the market value would otherwise be.

And this creates a vicious cycle. Prices fall. Owners who bought at higher prices are now underwater. Some of them can't afford to sell even at the discounted price because they'd have to bring money to closing. They're trapped.

Meanwhile, the building still needs the repairs. The special assessment still has to be paid. And some of these trapped owners simply can't pay it.

### The Delinquency Spiral

When an owner can't pay their special assessment, they become delinquent on their HOA fees. And when enough owners become delinquent, the association's cash flow starts to collapse.

In some South Florida buildings, we're seeing delinquency rates of thirty percent or more — thirty percent of units sixty-plus days behind on their dues. That's not a few bad apples. That's a systemic problem.

When the association's cash flow collapses, they can't pay for basic operations. They can't pay for insurance. They can't service their own debt, if they've taken out loans to fund repairs.

And this is where it gets really interesting from a banking perspective.

### The Bridge Loan Market

Many associations, when faced with these massive funding gaps, have turned to debt. They take out what are called "association loans" or "bridge loans" to fund the repairs.

The traditional banking channel — Valley National, Popular Bank, Seacoast — offers term loans at six and a half to nine and a half percent over ten to fifteen years. These are secured by the association's right to collect assessments — if the association defaults, the bank can step into their shoes and collect dues directly from unit owners.

There are also private bridge lenders — firms like Nexos and Insula — that offer faster funding at higher rates, nine to thirteen percent, with shorter terms of six to twenty-four months. These are for associations that need capital immediately and plan to refinance into permanent financing once the repairs are complete.

Here's the problem: this creates what I'm calling "hidden leverage."

When an association takes a five million dollar loan to fix the building, that loan gets converted into a monthly assessment increase for each unit owner. Maybe it adds a hundred fifty dollars a month to everyone's HOA fee for the next ten years.

But here's the thing — that assessment increase doesn't show up on the unit owner's credit report. When they go to refinance their personal mortgage, the underwriter doesn't see it. When they apply for a car loan, it's invisible. The household's actual debt burden has spiked, but traditional credit metrics don't capture it.

So you have banks making lending decisions based on debt-to-income ratios that are systematically understating the true burden on Florida condo owners. The stress is there, but it's hidden.

### Receivership and the Biscayne 21 Problem

When an association completely fails — when they can't seat a board, can't pay their bills, can't function — the state can appoint a receiver. This is a court-appointed professional who takes over management of the building with broad powers, including the ability to levy assessments without owner votes.

Receivership is expensive and slow. But it was supposed to at least provide an exit path — a way for a distressed building to be sold to a developer who would tear it down and build something new.

That exit path just got a lot narrower.

In October 2025, the Florida Supreme Court declined to review a case called Biscayne 21 — formally, Avila versus TRD Biscayne LLC. The details are complicated, but the bottom line is this: if your condo's original declaration required one hundred percent owner consent to terminate the condo and sell the building, that requirement is a binding contract. A majority of owners cannot vote to amend the declaration to lower the threshold to eighty percent just because the current Florida statutes allow eighty percent.

This sounds technical, but the implications are huge. A developer who has acquired eighty or ninety percent of the units in a building — intending to tear it down and build a luxury tower — can now be blocked by a small group of holdouts. And those holdouts have no incentive to sell because they know the developer needs them.

Before Biscayne 21, there was a way out for distressed buildings: sell to a developer, cash everyone out, move on. After Biscayne 21, that exit is often blocked. Buildings that can't function as condos but can't be sold to developers are stuck in limbo.

The result is that workout timelines have extended dramatically. We're seeing estimates of six hundred days or more to resolve a distressed building. That's almost two years of a bank holding an impaired asset with no clear path to resolution.

### The First Bankruptcy: Palm Greens

In January 2026, we got our first major data point. Palm Greens at Villa Del Ray — a fifty-five-plus community in Delray Beach, Palm Beach County — filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy with over forty-three million dollars in debt.

This is significant because it's the first major condo association bankruptcy of this cycle. It demonstrates that associations can and will fail under the weight of these new requirements.

The largest creditor listed was Lennar Homes, with a twenty-five million dollar claim — apparently related to some kind of developer dispute. But the broader picture is clear: even large, established communities can be pushed into insolvency by the combination of aging infrastructure, deferred maintenance, and the new legal requirements.

Palm Greens won't be the last. If we see three to five more association bankruptcies in the coming months, that's thesis validation. The cascade is underway.

### The Heron Pond Benchmark

There's another case that's important to understand: Heron Pond.

Heron Pond is a three-hundred-four-unit condo complex in Pembroke Pines, Broward County. It's nineteen two-story garden buildings on about twenty-five acres. The buildings were deemed structurally unsafe and vacated.

A receiver was appointed — Daniel Stermer — and he determined that the cost to restore the buildings exceeded their value as condos. So he's pursuing what's called a "judicial termination" — essentially dissolving the association and selling the entire property as a development site.

The stalking horse bid — the initial offer that sets the floor for the auction — came from a company called Integra Real Estate. They bid twenty point five million dollars for the entire property. That works out to about sixty-seven thousand dollars per unit.

Now, individual units in that submarket were previously trading for one hundred eighty to two hundred ten thousand dollars. So the bulk acquisition price represents a discount of more than sixty percent from market values.

This is important because it tells us what distressed condos are actually worth when they're sold in bulk. The answer is: way less than anyone thought. Sixty-seven thousand dollars per unit for buildings that individual owners thought were worth two hundred thousand.

If you're a bank holding mortgages on units in a building that goes through this process, your collateral just got repriced at sixty percent below where you thought it was.

## Part Four: Valley National Bank

### Why VLY Is the Target

Now let me explain why Valley National Bank — ticker VLY — emerges from all this research as our best single-name short candidate.

Valley is a regional bank headquartered in New Jersey, but they've expanded aggressively into Florida over the past decade through acquisitions. They bought USAmeriBank, which gave them a foothold in Tampa. They bought CNLBank, which gave them Orlando. They've built out commercial real estate lending across the state.

As of their most recent disclosures, Valley has seven point four billion dollars in Florida and Alabama commercial real estate exposure. That represents twenty-eight percent of their total CRE book.

But here's where it gets specific. When you drill into the geographic breakdown, forty-five percent of that Florida exposure — three point three three billion dollars — is concentrated in the Miami metropolitan statistical area. Miami-Dade, Broward, Palm Beach. That's ground zero for the condo crisis. That's where six hundred ninety-six of the one thousand four hundred thirty-eight blacklisted buildings are located.

### The Double Exposure Problem

Valley doesn't just have general Florida CRE exposure. They have a dedicated community association lending division. They explicitly market to HOAs and condo associations, offering loans and lines of credit for exactly the kind of structural repairs we've been discussing.

This creates what I'm calling "double exposure."

Imagine a condo building where Valley has made a three million dollar loan to the association for roof repairs. Valley is also holding mortgages on individual units in that same building — let's say they have five million dollars in mortgage exposure across twenty units.

If that building goes into distress — if the association can't service its loan because too many owners are delinquent — Valley gets hit twice. They've got the association loan going bad, and they've got the individual mortgages facing collateral deterioration because the building is now on the blacklist and the units are worth forty percent less.

Same building, two different loan categories, both impaired. That's the double exposure problem.

### The Numbers We Can Already See

Valley's non-accrual rate — loans where they've stopped accruing interest because they don't expect to collect — has been ticking up. It went from zero point seven two percent to zero point eight seven percent over the second half of 2025. That's not crisis territory. But the trend is moving the wrong direction.

Their Florida multifamily portfolio has a debt service coverage ratio of only one point four one times. That's compressed. For context, lenders typically want to see a DSCR of one point two or higher — meaning the property generates at least twenty percent more income than is needed to service the debt. At one point four one, there's not much cushion.

Why is it compressed? Insurance costs. When your operating expenses spike because insurance premiums went up thirty to fifty percent, the cash flow available for debt service shrinks. The math is simple: DSCR equals net operating income divided by debt service. When NOI goes down, DSCR goes down.

### Valley Is Actively De-Risking

I should be fair to Valley: they're not asleep at the wheel. Management clearly understands the risk and is actively managing it down.

In December 2024, they sold about a billion dollars in performing CRE loans to Brookfield Asset Management at only a one percent discount to par value. That's a good sign — it means the assets they're selling are high quality. If the portfolio were distressed, Brookfield would have demanded a much steeper discount.

They've brought their CRE concentration ratio down from around four hundred percent to three hundred thirty-three percent. That's meaningful de-risking. They've grown core deposits, which reduces their reliance on wholesale funding. They're making all the right moves.

But here's why Valley is still the target:

First, they can't exit fast enough. They've got seven point four billion in Florida exposure. Even if they sell a billion a year, it takes the better part of a decade to get out. And the stress isn't going to wait.

Second, unlike Eagle Bancorp — which is already priced for distress, trading at rock-bottom valuations — Valley trades at a relatively normal valuation. There's room for the stock to fall.

Third, unlike Seacoast Banking — which is a pure Florida play — Valley doesn't have the same M&A speculation providing a floor under the stock. Seacoast could get acquired. Valley is probably too big and too complex for most acquirers.

Fourth, Valley is transparent about their exposure. They disclose the geographic breakdown. They disclose the metrics. We can track what's happening. With some other banks, we'd be guessing. With Valley, we can measure.

### The Q1 Earnings Watch

Valley's Q1 2026 earnings will come out around April 23rd. That's going to be the first time we get visibility into how they're handling the post-SIRS environment.

Here's what we'll be watching for:

Non-accrual trends. If they tick up again, from zero point eight seven toward one percent or higher, that's stress manifesting.

Commentary on HOA and association loan performance. Management usually discusses problem areas on the earnings call. If they start talking about Florida association lending specifically, pay attention.

Reserve builds. If they're adding to their loan loss reserves in a meaningful way, that tells us they're expecting losses.

Any additional loan sales. Another Brookfield-style transaction would tell us they're accelerating the de-risking.

## Part Five: How This Fits the Bigger Picture

### The Nine Channels

Before today, we had identified eight independent stress channels that all terminate at regional banks:

One: Commercial real estate losses, tracked through CREED. Banks hold seventy percent of CRE loans, and the maturity wall is coming.

Two: Non-depository finance institution exposure, tracked through OTTO. Auto lenders, equipment finance, fraud cases — banks have one point seven trillion in exposure through warehouse lines.

Three: Federal layoffs impacting DC corridor banks, tracked through LABOR. Eagle Bancorp and Burke and Herbert are directly exposed to government cuts.

Four: Consumer credit deterioration, tracked through CARL. Thirty-seven percent of Americans can't cover a four hundred dollar emergency expense.

Five: BDC credit line draws, tracked through BROCK. Business Development Companies have a hundred forty-two billion in unfunded commitments from banks.

Six: Migration and regional economic stress, tracked through MARCO. Border cities, population shifts, labor market disruptions.

Seven: FHLB funding concentration, tracked through LIQUID. The Federal Home Loan Bank system is where all regional bank stress eventually shows up.

Eight: Japan contagion, tracked through SAM. If Japan's bond market breaks, repatriation flows affect CLOs, which affect BDCs, which affect banks.

Florida — the CORAL thesis — adds a ninth channel. And what's powerful about this channel is that it directly validates Valley National as a multi-channel target.

Valley has Florida CRE exposure. Valley has consumer exposure. Valley has BDC exposure through their fund finance business. They have at least three, maybe four independent paths to stress.

### The Convergence Thesis

The whole point of the convergence thesis is that you don't need all the channels to break. You just need two or three to correlate during the same period.

Think about what happens if employment softens — which LABOR is already tracking — at the same time the condo reserve cascade is hitting Florida. You've got people losing their jobs who are also facing fifty thousand dollar special assessments. That's not additive stress; it's multiplicative.

Or think about what happens if a hurricane hits during a period when association finances are already stretched. The insurance channel, which we said is now an amplifier rather than a trigger, would pour gasoline on an existing fire.

The value of the Florida thesis is that it's specific, it's quantifiable, it's already in motion, and it has a clear transmission mechanism to specific bank balance sheets. We're not speculating about what might happen. We're tracking what is happening.

## Part Six: What Could Go Wrong

### The Bull Case for Florida

I should be honest about what could make this thesis wrong.

First, the Florida legislature could provide relief. They have a session in 2026. If they loosen the SIRS requirements, extend deadlines, or create funding programs for distressed associations, that would reduce the pressure.

Second, insurance could continue improving. If rates keep declining, more capacity enters the market, and the fourteen companies under enhanced monitoring get healthy, the amplification channel becomes less dangerous.

Third, the courts could find a way around Biscayne 21. There's been discussion of creating an "economic waste" exception — allowing terminations even without unanimous consent if the cost of maintaining the building exceeds its value. That would reopen the developer exit path.

Fourth, interest rates could drop significantly. If the Fed cuts aggressively, refinancing becomes easier, bridge loan maturities become less dangerous, and the whole system gets more runway.

Fifth, Valley specifically could exit faster than expected. If they find more buyers like Brookfield willing to take performing loans at small discounts, they could reduce their Florida exposure faster than we're modeling.

### The Bear Case Accelerants

On the other hand, here's what could make the thesis more bearish:

A major insurer failure would reactivate the insurance channel as a primary trigger rather than an amplifier. If Slide or Universal went under, that would create coverage gaps and forced-place insurance situations for thousands of units.

A hurricane hitting the Miami urban corridor would be catastrophic. Even if the insurance system holds, the physical damage and displacement would accelerate every other stress.

More association bankruptcies would validate the cascade. Palm Greens is one data point. Five would be a trend. Ten would be a crisis.

If Valley's Q1 earnings show significant deterioration — non-accruals jumping to one percent or higher, commentary about HOA lending problems, major reserve builds — that would accelerate our single-name thesis.

And if employment breaks — if the February jobs report or subsequent ones show significant weakness — that turns the multiplier on for every stress channel simultaneously.
