# The Subprime Auto Implosion: When the Cockroaches Start Appearing
*Standard | 12min | 2026-02-08*

---

There's an old saying on Wall Street: you never find just one cockroach. When fraud or stress shows up in one corner of a market, it's usually a sign that the whole kitchen is filthy.

Right now, the subprime auto lending industry is finding cockroaches everywhere it looks. And the implications run deeper than you might think — all the way to regional bank balance sheets.

Let me tell you a story about how America's car loan market went from boom to potential bust, and why 6.74% might be the most important number you haven't heard about.

## The Number

Auto loan delinquencies — borrowers more than 60 days late on payments — just hit 6.65% in Q4 2025.

That's the highest level since the Federal Reserve started tracking this data in 1993. Higher than the Great Financial Crisis. Higher than the pandemic. Thirty-two year high.

And if you narrow the lens to the riskiest borrowers — the ones working with non-bank "monoline" lenders who specialize in subprime — the delinquency rate is 16.6%.

One in six borrowers, more than two months behind.

Now, delinquency rates are one thing. The real question is: what happens when those loans default and the lender tries to recover by repossessing and selling the car?

Here's where it gets ugly. 52.9% of auto borrowers are "underwater" — they owe more on the loan than the car is worth. When you repo a car that's worth less than the loan balance, you take a loss. A big one.

And that brings us to the fraud.

## The Fraud

In 2024, a subprime auto lender called Tricolor Holdings collapsed under the weight of an $800 million fraud scheme.

The playbook was classic: double-pledging collateral (using the same car loan as collateral for multiple credit lines), manipulated loan tapes, creating fake warehouse lending silos to hide the shell game from auditors.

Tricolor went bankrupt. Its warehouse lenders — the banks that had fronted cash against those car loans as collateral — started doing forensic audits.

JPMorgan and Fifth Third Bank took combined losses over $200 million. Fifth Third alone took a $170-200 million charge in Q3 2025 just from Tricolor exposure.

Then, in January 2026, another lender called PrimaLend filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Different company, similar problems.

And now the entire sector is under audit. Because if it happened at Tricolor and PrimaLend, where else did it happen?

That's the cockroach theory in action.

## The Lenders on the Watchlist

Let's talk about who's under stress.

**Flagship Credit Acceptance** is the big one. This is a $5+ billion loan portfolio. Between April 2025 and January 2026, they laid off staff in waves. In November 2025, they sold a chunk of their portfolio to a company called InterVest in what looks like a fire sale.

When a lender with $5 billion in subprime auto loans starts mass layoffs and selling assets, that's not a strategic pivot. That's distress.

**Exeter Finance** is another name to watch. S&P recently hiked the expected loss forecast on their 2026-1 securitization to 20.75%. Think about that — the ratings agency expects one in five dollars to default.

Exeter's still operating, still issuing bonds backed by these loans. But the market is pricing in severe stress.

**Consumer Portfolio Services** is facing class-action litigation over its repossession practices. They also have a $900 million "forward flow" dependency — meaning they rely on continuously selling new loans to other investors to fund operations. If that pipeline clogs, they're in trouble.

Why does this matter beyond the obvious human cost of people losing their cars?

Because these lenders don't fund themselves. They borrow from banks.

## The Warehouse Lending Connection

Here's how the chain works:

Subprime auto lender originates a loan to a borrower with a 580 credit score who's buying a used Honda Civic at 18% interest.

The lender doesn't keep that loan on its own balance sheet. It takes the loan to a bank — often a regional bank — and uses it as collateral for a warehouse line of credit.

The bank advances cash, say 85 cents on the dollar. The lender uses that cash to make more loans. Rinse and repeat.

It's a leveraged machine. And it works great… until the loans start defaulting.

When the collateral (the car loan) goes bad, the bank takes a hit. JPMorgan and Fifth Third learned this the hard way with Tricolor.

But it's not just the money-center banks. Regional banks are deep into warehouse lending for auto loans. And if Flagship or Exeter collapses the way Tricolor did, the next round of write-downs hits smaller institutions that are already dealing with commercial real estate stress, consumer credit deterioration, and funding pressures.

This is a second front opening up on regional bank balance sheets.

## Why Are Delinquencies Spiking Now?

The surface answer is: people can't pay.

The deeper answer is: the job market is softening, and subprime borrowers are always the first to feel it.

Our labor research shows about 580,000 layoffs announced between 2024 and 2026. Temp employment — a leading indicator — is down 12% year-over-year. Initial jobless claims just spiked to 231,000, breaching a key threshold.

For someone living paycheck to paycheck with a 580 credit score, losing a job or even having hours cut means the car payment is the first thing to go. Especially if you're already underwater on the loan and can't refinance.

Now layer in the extended loan terms. 27.5% of auto loans are for more than 72 months. That's six years. When you stretch a loan that long on a depreciating asset, you're almost guaranteed to be underwater for most of the loan's life.

If you lose your job three years into a seven-year car loan, you can't sell the car to get out from under the payment. You owe more than it's worth. Your only option is to keep paying… or default.

The delinquency rate tells you how many people are choosing default.

## The Transmission Timeline

Here's how this flows through the system:

**Month 0:** Borrower loses job (or has hours cut, or faces an unexpected expense).

**Month 1-2:** Borrower misses first car payment. This is the delinquency we're seeing in the data now.

**Month 3-4:** Lender repossesses the car.

**Month 4-6:** Lender sells the car at auction, realizes it's worth 60-70% of the loan balance, takes a loss.

**Month 6-9:** Lender reports the loss to its warehouse bank. If losses are severe enough, the lender can't borrow more. Liquidity dries up.

**Month 9-12:** Lender distress becomes public (layoffs, asset sales, bankruptcy).

**Month 12-18:** Warehouse banks take write-downs on their exposure.

We're currently somewhere around Month 2-4 in this timeline. The delinquencies are visible. The repos are happening. The losses are getting realized at the lender level.

The bank-level write-downs are still coming.

## The Regional Bank Angle

Why does this matter for regional banks specifically?

Because they're the ones most exposed to multiple stress channels simultaneously.

Think about a bank like Valley National or Western Alliance. They've got:
- Commercial real estate loans (under pressure from office vacancies and higher rates)
- Consumer credit exposure (credit card and personal loan delinquencies rising)
- Warehouse lending to subprime auto lenders (this story)
- Funding pressures (deposit flight to money market funds)

No single channel might be enough to break a bank. But when three or four channels start correlating — when the same economic stress (labor market weakening) hits consumer credit AND subprime auto AND commercial real estate at the same time — that's when things get interesting.

The subprime auto implosion is one more path to stress for banks that are already walking a tightrope.

## What Could Make This Better?

The optimistic case: employment holds up better than the leading indicators suggest. People keep their jobs, keep making payments, delinquencies stabilize.

Or: the Fed cuts rates aggressively, refinancing options open up, some borrowers can get out from underwater positions.

Or: fraud is contained to Tricolor and PrimaLend, the rest of the sector is clean, no more cockroaches.

But the trend right now is going the other way. Every data point — JOLTS openings down to 2017 levels, initial claims spiking, Challenger layoffs at 2009 levels — suggests the labor market is weakening, not strengthening.

And the fraud audits are expanding, not narrowing.

## The Bottom Line

6.65% auto delinquency doesn't sound like a crisis number. It's not a headline that moves markets.

But it's a 32-year high. It's happening while 52.9% of borrowers are underwater. It's triggering forensic audits across an entire industry after $800 million in fraud just got uncovered. And it's creating a second front of stress for regional banks that are already dealing with commercial real estate, consumer credit, and funding pressures.

The subprime auto market is a $1.7 trillion ecosystem. When cracks appear in something that size, connected to regional bank balance sheets through warehouse lending, you pay attention.

The first cockroach was Tricolor. The second was PrimaLend. The question now is: how many more are we going to find?

And when the warehouse lenders start taking write-downs, which regional banks take the hit?

We're watching Flagship, Exeter, and Consumer Portfolio Services. We're watching the warehouse lending exposures at smaller regional banks. And we're watching the employment data, because that's the trigger that converts delinquency into default.

This story isn't over. It's just getting started.
