# The Buffer is Gone: What Happens When the Fed's Shock Absorber Runs Dry
*Standard | 13min | 2026-02-08*

---

There's a number that most people have never heard of, tracking a facility most people don't understand, that just hit a threshold that should make everyone nervous.

The Reverse Repo Facility — the RRP — is down to $10.4 billion.

To understand why that matters, you need to know three things: what it was, what it did, and what happens now that it's gone.

This is the story of how the U.S. financial system just lost its shock absorber, and why the plumbing is more fragile than the calm surface suggests.

## What the RRP Was

Let's go back to 2020-2022. The Fed is running unprecedented monetary stimulus: buying trillions in Treasuries and mortgage bonds, rates at zero, flooding the system with cash.

All that cash has to go somewhere. Banks, money market funds, institutional investors — they're all sitting on mountains of liquidity they need to park overnight.

The problem: there aren't enough safe, short-term assets to absorb it all. Treasury bills are in short supply. Private repo rates are getting pushed negative.

So the Fed creates a pressure relief valve: the Reverse Repo Facility.

Here's how it works: If you're a money market fund with excess cash, you can "deposit" it at the Fed overnight and earn a fixed rate. The Fed pays you interest, you get a safe overnight return, everybody's happy.

At the peak in December 2022, $2.5 trillion was parked in the RRP. Every single day. That's not a typo — two and a half trillion dollars of excess liquidity, just sitting at the Fed, waiting for somewhere better to go.

## What It Did (The Shock Absorber Function)

But the RRP wasn't just a parking lot. It was a shock absorber.

When funding stress hit the market — a big Treasury auction, quarter-end window dressing, a sudden demand for collateral — cash could flow OUT of the RRP and into the market to meet that demand.

Think of it like a reservoir behind a dam. When there's a drought downstream, you release water from the reservoir. The system stays stable.

From 2022 to 2024, every time there was funding pressure, the RRP would drain a little. Cash would flow out to where it was needed. Stress would dissipate.

And here's the key: this happened automatically. The Fed didn't have to intervene. The market self-stabilized.

## How It Drained

Starting in mid-2023, the RRP started draining steadily.

Why? Because money market funds found better opportunities. Treasury bill issuance picked up. T-bill yields started offering more than the RRP rate. Private repo markets offered competitive rates with more flexibility.

So the cash rotated out. Slowly at first, then faster.

By January 2025, the RRP hit what's called the "effective floor" — around $2 billion. Technically above zero, but functionally empty.

The Fed noticed. In early 2025, they launched Reserve Management Purchases — buying $40 billion a month in T-bills to inject liquidity back into the system and prevent reserve balances from draining too low.

It's like the Fed realized the reservoir was empty and started pumping water back in manually.

## Where We Are Now: $10.4 Billion

As of February 2, 2026, the RRP stands at $10.4 billion. Eight counterparties. That's down from $2.5 trillion and 100+ counterparties at the peak.

Technically, it's above the $5 billion "critical" threshold. But let's be real: $10 billion in a multi-trillion dollar funding market is a rounding error.

The buffer is gone.

## What This Means (The Fragility)

Now when funding stress hits, there's no automatic release valve.

Let's walk through a scenario:

Big Treasury auction settles. Dealers need to fund their positions. Collateral demand spikes in the repo market. Normally, cash would flow out of the RRP to meet that demand.

But the RRP is at $10 billion. There's nothing to flow out.

So what happens instead?

Option 1: The Fed intervenes directly through the Standing Repo Facility (SRF) — a backstop where banks can borrow cash against Treasury collateral at a fixed rate.

Option 2: Repo rates spike. The cost of funding goes up. Dealers pull back. Market liquidity tightens.

Option 3: Both.

We got a preview of this on December 31, 2025. Quarter-end funding stress hit. The SRF saw $74.6 billion in usage — the highest since it launched.

And here's the kicker: SOFR, the benchmark overnight rate, traded 12 basis points ABOVE the SRF rate.

Think about that. The SRF is supposed to be a ceiling — a rate where banks can always borrow if needed. But SOFR spiked through it anyway.

Why? Because the SRF has limited access. Only certain institutions can use it directly. Non-bank dealers, money market funds, hedge funds — they can't walk up to the Fed's window.

So when stress hits, SOFR can blow through the "ceiling." The plumbing is more fragile than the Fed's backstops suggest.

## The Dealer Constraint

Here's another layer: dealer capacity is clogged.

Primary dealers — the big banks that intermediate Treasury market flows — are holding about $200 billion net long in Treasuries right now.

That doesn't sound insane until you understand the constraint they're under: the Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR).

Banks have balance sheet limits. Even if it's profitable to buy a Treasury bond and repo it out for a positive spread, if the bank is near its SLR cap, it won't do the trade.

What this means: even when there are profitable arbitrage opportunities, dealers can't step in. The market's "shock absorption capacity" at the dealer level is also tapped out.

So you've got:
- RRP at zero (no automatic buffer)
- SRF with access limitations (can't reach everyone who needs it)
- Dealers at capacity constraints (can't expand to intermediate flows)

This is a system running with no margin for error.

## The Basis Trade Wild Card

Now let's add one more layer of fragility: the $1.85 trillion basis trade.

Hedge funds borrow in the repo market to fund positions that exploit tiny pricing differences between Treasury cash bonds and futures contracts. It's a highly leveraged, tightly managed arbitrage.

But it depends on continuous access to repo funding.

If money market funds face redemptions and stop rolling their repo lending, hedge funds can't fund the basis trade. They're forced to unwind — selling Treasuries into the market.

In March 2020, this dynamic triggered a Treasury market meltdown. The Fed had to step in with emergency asset purchases and swap lines.

The difference between then and now: in March 2020, the RRP had buffer capacity. Today, it's at $10 billion.

If a basis trade unwind starts, there's no automatic cushion. It's Fed intervention or cascade.

## The Japan Connection

Here's where this connects to the Japan story we covered earlier.

If Japanese life insurers need to cover losses on Japanese government bonds, they might sell their U.S. Treasury holdings — estimates range from $60 to $100 billion of potential repatriation.

That's a lot of Treasury selling. In a normal environment with RRP buffer, the market absorbs it. Cash flows out of the RRP, into the market, buying the Treasuries that Japanese sellers are dumping.

But the RRP is at $10 billion. There's no buffer.

So Japanese repatriation flows hit a market with:
- No RRP cushion
- Dealers at capacity constraints
- A $1.85 trillion basis trade funded by flighty repo money

You see how the transmission works. A shock in Japan doesn't stay in Japan. It transmits through Treasury markets, through funding markets, through the repo plumbing that holds everything together.

And the shock absorber that would have dampened that transmission? It's gone.

## What the Fed Is Doing

The Fed sees this. That's why they launched Reserve Management Purchases in early 2025 — buying $40 billion a month in T-bills to keep reserve balances from draining too low.

Reserves — the cash that banks hold at the Fed — are down to $2.95 trillion, down $377 billion year-over-year.

There's a level called "ample reserves" — the point where the system has just enough liquidity to function smoothly. Estimates vary, but it's probably somewhere around $3 trillion.

We're approaching that line.

So the Fed is manually managing liquidity: buying T-bills, maintaining the SRF, watching funding markets like a hawk.

But here's the risk: all of this depends on the Fed's willingness and ability to intervene in real time when stress hits.

## The Warsh Factor

Which brings us to Kevin Warsh.

Warsh was just nominated to replace Jerome Powell as Fed Chair when Powell's term expires in May 2026.

Warsh has a history. He's a balance sheet hawk. He's criticized the "bloated balance sheet" that he says "subsidizes Wall Street." He's on record preferring to "let markets clear" rather than intervene aggressively.

Now, the Fed's capacity hasn't changed. The SRF still exists. Swap lines are still there. The tools are the same.

But willingness? That could shift.

If Warsh gets confirmed and a funding crisis hits, does he activate the SRF as quickly as Powell would have? Does he expand the balance sheet via emergency QE, or does he let the market "find its level" first?

We don't know. But the uncertainty itself is a factor.

The market is used to a Fed that responds aggressively to plumbing stress. If that assumption gets tested under new leadership, the reaction could be sharp.

## The Danger Windows

So when does this fragility matter?

**March 31:** Quarter-end. We already saw SOFR spike through the SRF ceiling on December 31. March 31 is the next test.

**April:** Tax season. The Treasury General Account (TGA) — the government's checking account at the Fed — gets refilled. When the TGA builds, it drains reserves one-for-one. In a world with RRP buffer, that's fine. Without it, reserves drop closer to the "ample" floor.

**Any Japan repatriation event:** If Japanese stress forces $60-100 billion of Treasury selling, as discussed in the Japan briefing.

**Any money market fund redemption wave:** This would cut off repo funding for the basis trade. March 2020 playbook, but without the RRP cushion.

## The Bottom Line

The U.S. funding market is like a car that's had its shock absorbers removed.

On smooth roads, you don't notice. The ride feels fine. SOFR is stable, Treasury auctions are clearing, everything looks calm.

But the first time you hit a pothole, the whole car shakes.

The RRP was a $2.5 trillion cushion that absorbed shocks automatically, without Fed intervention, without drama.

Now it's $10 billion. Effectively zero.

What's left is a system that depends entirely on:
- The Fed's real-time intervention capacity (SRF, emergency facilities)
- Dealer willingness to intermediate (constrained by SLR)
- Hedge fund basis trade stability (funded by flighty repo money)
- No large external shocks (Japan, geopolitics, etc.)

Remove any one of those assumptions and stress transmits faster than it would have in 2020-2023.

The buffer is gone. The plumbing works perfectly… until it doesn't.

And when it doesn't, we'll find out very quickly whether the Fed's manual interventions can substitute for the automatic stabilization we used to have.

The RRP hit $10.4 billion on February 2nd. It's not coming back. This is the new normal.

The question is: what happens during the first real stress test?
