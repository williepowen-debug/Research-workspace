# CORAL Briefing: The Florida Thesis
## February 5, 2026

*Audio briefing format — approximately 18-20 minutes*

---

Let me walk you through what we discovered today about Florida, because it fundamentally changes how we understand one of the most important pieces of our regional bank thesis. This is dense material, but I'll try to make it conversational and digestible.

### The Setup: Why Florida Matters

When we first started looking at Florida as part of the REGINALD research, we were focused on what I was calling the "doom loop" — this idea that Citizens Property Insurance, the state's insurer of last resort, had grown so large that a major hurricane could trigger a cascade of assessments that would hit every Florida borrower simultaneously.

The numbers were scary. At its peak, Citizens had one point four two million policies and somewhere around six hundred seventy-eight billion dollars in total insured value. The fear was simple: a big enough storm depletes Citizens' surplus, they levy a fifteen percent surcharge on their own policyholders, and if that's not enough, they can assess ten percent on *all* Florida property insurance policies — including private ones. That assessment becomes essentially a senior lien on cash flows, competing directly with mortgage payments.

But here's what today's research revealed: that story is outdated. The doom loop mechanism has evolved, and in some ways, the new version is even more concerning for regional banks.

### What Actually Happened: The Depopulation Success

Citizens has been aggressively shrinking. They've gone from one point four two million policies down to roughly three hundred eighty-five to three hundred ninety-five thousand. That's a seventy-three percent reduction. They've pushed over two hundred fourteen billion dollars in exposure back to the private market.

On the surface, this looks like good news. The state's contingent liability has shrunk dramatically. But here's the catch: all that risk didn't disappear. It moved to private insurers — companies like Slide, Tower Hill, American Integrity, and Universal Property and Casualty.

And the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation currently has fourteen companies under what they call "enhanced monitoring." That's roughly fifteen percent of the domestic market under close regulatory scrutiny. So the risk hasn't gone away — it's just become more distributed and potentially more fragile.

The good news is that there were no insurer insolvencies in twenty twenty-five, which is remarkable after the wave of failures in twenty twenty-two and twenty twenty-three. Litigation is down twenty-six percent year over year thanks to the reforms in Senate Bill 2-A. Reinsurance costs actually ticked down slightly. Citizens is even recommending a two point six percent rate *cut* for twenty twenty-six — the first decrease since twenty fifteen.

So the insurance channel, while not completely healthy, is stabilizing. It's no longer the primary trigger for the doom loop. Instead, it's become an *amplifier* — something that could accelerate an already-stressed market, rather than being the thing that initiates the crisis.

### The Real Trigger: The Condo Reserve Cascade

This is where the research gets really interesting. The primary trigger for Florida real estate stress isn't meteorological anymore. It's *regulatory*. And it's already in motion.

After the Surfside condo collapse in June twenty twenty-one — the Champlain Towers disaster that killed ninety-eight people — Florida passed a series of laws that fundamentally changed how condo associations operate. Senate Bill 4-D in twenty twenty-two, followed by refinements in twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-five.

Here's what these laws require: Any building three stories or taller has to complete what's called a Milestone Structural Inspection when it reaches thirty years of age — or twenty-five years if it's within three miles of the coastline. They also have to complete a Structural Integrity Reserve Study, or SIRS.

But the real kicker came with the January first, twenty twenty-five deadline. As of that date, condo associations can no longer vote to waive or underfund their reserves for structural items. For decades, Florida condo boards kept monthly fees low by kicking the maintenance can down the road. That option is now legally prohibited.

Let me give you a concrete example of what this means. Say you have a hundred-unit building. The roof needs to be replaced in five years, and the replacement cost is two million dollars. But the association has zero in reserves because they'd been waiving contributions for years.

Under the old rules, they could just keep waiving. Under the new rules, they need to collect four hundred thousand dollars annually — four thousand dollars per unit per year — just for the roof. When you multiply that across the nine mandatory structural components — roofing, load-bearing walls, floors, foundations, fire protection, plumbing, electrical, and waterproofing — the catch-up requirements are staggering.

We're seeing special assessments in the range of thirty thousand to over one hundred thousand dollars per unit. For many owners — especially retirees on fixed incomes who bought into these buildings decades ago — that's simply unpayable.

### The Blacklist and the Liquidity Trap

Here's where it gets really bad for the banks. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac maintain what's essentially a blacklist — they call it the "unavailable list" — of condo developments that don't meet their standards. If your building is on this list because of inadequate reserves, failed inspections, or insurance issues, conventional mortgages are simply not available.

As of our research, there are one thousand four hundred thirty-eight associations on this blacklist. About half of them — six hundred ninety-six — are concentrated in the Miami and Palm Beach corridor.

Think about what this means for a unit owner trying to sell. They can't get a conventional mortgage for their buyer. The only people who can purchase are cash buyers, who naturally demand a steep discount — we're talking twenty to forty percent below what the market value would otherwise be.

This creates what I'm calling a liquidity trap. Owners can't sell at a reasonable price. They can't refinance. They're trapped in a depreciating asset with an escalating monthly bill.

### The Cascade Mechanism

So here's how the cascade actually works in practice:

First, the SIRS deadline passes. Associations discover they have massive funding gaps.

Second, they have to pass a special assessment or take out a bridge loan — typically one to twenty-five million dollars for an association — to fund the repairs.

Third, those assessments hit unit owners. Many can't pay.

Fourth, delinquency spikes. In some South Florida buildings, we're seeing thirty percent or more of units sixty-plus days delinquent on their HOA dues.

Fifth, the association's cash flow collapses. They can't service their own loans. They can't pay for insurance. They can't maintain operations.

Sixth, the building goes into receivership — a court-appointed manager takes over.

Seventh — and here's the new wrinkle from the Biscayne 21 ruling — that building often can't be sold to a developer because the Florida Supreme Court just upheld that if the original declaration requires one hundred percent owner consent for termination, that's a binding contract that can't be amended away by a majority vote.

So developers who might otherwise buy these distressed buildings, tear them down, and build new luxury towers... they're now stuck. They can acquire eighty or ninety percent of the units, but they can't force the holdouts to sell. The buildings sit in limbo.

The result: workout timelines extending to six hundred days or more. Banks holding impaired assets for extended periods with no clear path to resolution.

### Valley National Bank: The Primary Target

Now let me explain why VLY — Valley National Bank — emerges from this research as the best single-name short candidate for expressing this thesis.

Valley has seven point four billion dollars in Florida and Alabama commercial real estate exposure. That represents twenty-eight percent of their total CRE book. But here's the critical detail: forty-five percent of that Florida exposure — three point three three billion dollars — is concentrated in the Miami metropolitan statistical area. That's ground zero for the condo crisis.

But it gets more specific. Valley has a dedicated community association lending division. They explicitly lend to HOAs and condo associations, providing loans and lines of credit for exactly the kind of repairs we've been discussing.

This creates what I'm calling "double exposure." Valley can have a loan to the association itself — let's say a five million dollar master loan for building repairs. And they can also hold individual mortgages on units within that same building. If the building goes into distress, *both* loan categories get hit. The association can't service the master loan, and the individual mortgages face collateral deterioration.

We can already see early stress in the numbers. Valley's non-accrual rate has been ticking up — from zero point seven two percent to zero point eight seven percent over the second half of twenty twenty-five. That's not crisis territory, but it's trending the wrong direction.

Their multifamily portfolio in Florida shows a debt service coverage ratio of only one point four one times. That's compressed. The reason? Insurance costs are eating into net operating income. When your operating expenses spike because insurance premiums went up thirty to fifty percent, the cash flow available for debt service shrinks.

Now, Valley is actively managing this risk. In December twenty twenty-four, they sold about a billion dollars in performing CRE loans to Brookfield Asset Management at only a one percent discount. That's a good sign — it means the assets they're selling are high quality, not fire sales. They've brought their CRE concentration ratio down from around four hundred percent to three hundred thirty-three percent.

But here's why Valley is still the target: unlike Eagle Bancorp, which is already priced for distress, Valley trades at a relatively normal valuation. And unlike Seacoast Banking — which is a pure Florida play — Valley doesn't have the same M&A speculation providing a floor under the stock. If the Florida thesis plays out, Valley has room to fall.

### The Bridge Loan Hidden Leverage Problem

There's one more piece of this that's important to understand: the bridge loan market for condo associations.

When associations need capital to fund SIRS-mandated repairs, they're increasingly taking out loans. The traditional banking channel — Valley, Popular Bank, Seacoast — offers term loans at six and a half to nine and a half percent over ten to fifteen years. The private bridge lenders — firms like Nexos and Insula — offer faster funding at nine to thirteen percent, but with shorter terms of six to twenty-four months.

Here's the problem: this debt is essentially invisible to the normal credit system.

When an association takes a five million dollar bridge loan to fix the building, that gets converted into a monthly assessment increase for each unit owner — maybe a hundred fifty dollars a month for ten years. But that assessment increase doesn't show up on the unit owner's credit report. When they go to refinance their personal mortgage, the underwriter doesn't see it. When they apply for a car loan, it's invisible.

So you have households whose actual debt burden has spiked, but it's not captured in traditional credit metrics. The banks lending to these households don't fully understand the stress they're under.

We're already seeing the first cracks. In January twenty twenty-six, Palm Greens at Villa Del Ray — a fifty-five-plus community in Palm Beach County — filed for Chapter Eleven bankruptcy with over forty-three million dollars in debt. That's the first major association bankruptcy of this cycle. It won't be the last.

### How This Fits the Broader Convergence

Let me tie this back to the overall thesis.

We originally identified eight channels of stress that all terminate at regional banks: CRE losses, auto fraud exposure through non-depository lenders, federal layoff impact on DC corridor banks, consumer credit deterioration, BDC credit line draws, migration-related regional stress, FHLB funding concentration, and Japan contagion through the CLO market.

Florida — what we're now calling the CORAL thesis — adds a ninth channel. And it's particularly important because it directly validates Valley National as a multi-channel target. VLY has Florida CRE exposure, consumer exposure, and BDC exposure. They have at least three independent paths to stress.

The convergence thesis says you don't need all channels to break. You just need two or three to correlate during the same period. With Florida, we now have a very specific, geographically concentrated, timing-aligned channel that feeds directly into our existing bank targets.

### What Would Change Our View

A few things could make us more bearish. A major insurer failure would reactivate the insurance amplification channel. More association bankruptcies obviously accelerate the timeline. A hurricane hitting the Miami urban corridor would be catastrophic.

On the other side, if the Florida legislature loosens the SIRS requirements in their twenty twenty-six session, that would provide relief. If insurance rates continue declining and more capacity enters the market, that reduces the amplification risk. If Valley's Q1 earnings come in clean with stable non-accruals and no HOA stress, we'd need to reassess the single-name thesis.

### Closing Thoughts

What we built today with CORAL is a dedicated monitoring system for what might be the most specific, trackable channel in our entire research operation. We know the exact numbers for Valley's Florida exposure. We know the geographic concentration. We know the mechanism by which stress transmits from the building level to the household level to the bank balance sheet.

The condo reserve cascade is not a theory — it's a mandatory compliance timeline that has already passed. Associations are already discovering their funding gaps. The blacklist is already at fourteen hundred buildings. The first bankruptcy has already happened.

We're not predicting a hurricane. We're not predicting an insurer failure. We're observing a structural repricing of Florida's aging condo stock that is already in progress, and tracking how that flows through to regional bank exposure.

Valley National is our best single-name expression of this thesis. They have the exposure, they have the geographic concentration, and they have no obvious floor under the stock. When Q1 earnings arrive in late April, we'll know a lot more about whether this thesis is playing out as expected.
