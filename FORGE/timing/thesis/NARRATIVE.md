## What We Believe

We believe the U.S. is entering a credit crisis that will accelerate between May and July 2026 and cascade through the financial system by Q4 2026. This is not a slow bleed. The consensus view — that private credit stress can be managed over 12-24 months through extend-and-pretend — is wrong, and we think we can show why.

The core argument is structural, not speculative. Private credit grew from near-zero to over $1.7 trillion (and $3-4 trillion in the broader ecosystem) in roughly a decade. It did so with almost no price discovery infrastructure, no standardized reporting, no regulatory framework comparable to banking, and a fee structure that actively rewards managers for avoiding markdowns. When 94% of defaults are distressed exchanges — meaning the lender and borrower quietly agree to defer the problem rather than recognize the loss — you don't have a functioning market. You have a system optimized for concealment.

This is not a new observation. What's new is that the concealment is breaking down from multiple directions simultaneously, and there is no policy backstop available to arrest it.

## Why Now, Not Later

Six things are converging in a window that has no modern precedent:

**First, the junk credit market is showing severe stress — but it's concentrated, not broad-based.** CCC-rated spreads hit 1,013 basis points. However, three-source independent analysis (Perplexity, Claude, Gemini — March 31) reveals this is substantially driven by sector concentration: cable/media names (DISH, Altice, Clear Channel) comprise ~24-30% of the CCC bond index and are in secular death spirals unrelated to the credit cycle, while PE-healthcare (~15%) and software (~5-8%) add further distortion. Stripping software alone drops CCC OAS to ~930-960bp; stripping software AND cable/media brings it to ~650-780bp — stressed but not December 2007 territory. The CCC/HY ratio of 2.96 is also inflated because the broad HY index is the highest quality in its history (BB now 58% of the index vs 38% in 2007), compressing the denominator. The closer CCC-specific analog is the 2015-16 energy sector blowout, not 2007 GFC.

**However, this does not mean the signal should be dismissed.** Even at ex-software levels, CCC is meaningfully elevated. First-lien recovery rates have collapsed to 37.7% vs a 25-year average of 62.3% — a broad deterioration, not sector-specific. And 40% of ALL CCC borrowers have operating cash flow coverage below 1.0x. The critical question is whether the concentrated stress transmits systemically via CLO mechanics (CCC bucket breaches → OC test failures → forced selling), BDC redemption cascades, and insurance re-rating. That's the transmission bet — not a bet on CCC as a standalone systemic indicator.

**Second, the Fed is trapped.** In 2007, the Fed had room to cut from 5.25% and did so aggressively — 325 basis points of cuts between September 2007 and March 2008. Those cuts produced real, if temporary, relief rallies. The largest compressed HY spreads by 98 basis points over 35 days. In 2026, PCE inflation is running hot, and the Fed cannot cut without abandoning its inflation mandate. This means every relief rally we get will be shallower (we estimate 40-60bp max vs. 2007's 98bp) and shorter (10-15 days vs. 27-35 days). There is no policy put under this market.

**Third, there's a simultaneous oil shock.** Brent crude is at $108 with the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed. Gas has breached the $4/gallon behavioral breakpoint — the level where consumers historically begin cutting discretionary spending. The Hamilton framework projects GDP drag of 3-5 percentage points at peak, with a 4-quarter lag placing maximum damage in Q1 2027. This compresses the macro timeline independently of credit: companies that might have survived an extend-and-pretend cycle with stable EBITDA will see revenue fall, making the math of loan extension impossible. You can't extend a loan when the borrower's cash flow is deteriorating.

**Fourth, the extend-and-pretend toolkit is largely exhausted.** The 2021-2022 vintage LBOs — underwritten at peak multiples, minimal covenants, near-zero rates — have already been amended and extended once. PIK rates at the worst names are running 14-15%, which means the loan balance doubles in five years while the borrower generates no cash to service it. The $270 billion in leveraged loans that were amended and extended in 2024 have been pushed to 2028-2031 maturities, but the underlying economics haven't improved. Each successive extension makes the eventual reckoning larger, not smaller.

**Fifth, the hidden amplifiers are larger than 2007's.** PE-controlled insurers now direct $700 billion in U.S. life insurance assets — roughly a third of the total. Athene (Apollo) runs at a 2.4% surplus ratio vs. the 7.2% industry average, with approximately one-fifth of investments flowing to affiliated PE funds. The FABN market — wholesale funding instruments issued by these insurers — has tripled since 2018 to $277 billion, and the Fed has classified them as "runnable money-like liabilities." The NAIC admitted it cannot independently verify this market's size. This is the monoline insurer problem of 2007 running through a different channel, at comparable scale, with even less transparency.

**Sixth, there are novel accelerants with no 2007 parallel.** The Egan-Jones ratings situation is the most significant. If the SEC proceedings (decision due August 12) result in systematic revocation of private credit ratings, $350 billion or more in insurer-held assets face immediate re-rating and potential forced capital calls. The NAIC's new SVO challenge authority — effective this year — could trigger the same cascade from a different direction. Neither of these mechanisms existed in 2007.

## The Sequence We Expect

The pattern we're tracking maps closely to 2007's denial-recognition-panic sequence, but compressed and without the cushion of rate cuts.

Right now — late March 2026 — we're in the late complacency phase. Seven funds have gated, $4.6 billion in capital is trapped, BDC stocks trade at 17-25% discounts to NAV, and the sell-side consensus is breaking (Goldman projects $45-70 billion in outflows over two years). But the broader market hasn't priced it. HY at 342 says "this is fine." The S&P is wobbling but hasn't cracked. Powell said on March 30 that he doesn't see systemic risk — language we note is remarkably similar to Bernanke's March 2007 "subprime is contained" statement.

We expect April and May to bring the first acceleration. Tomorrow's Treasury meeting with insurance regulators is a Stage 3 signal in our framework — the government engaging at a level 2007 didn't see until much later. CLO OC test failures will cascade in April reporting cycles. The oil shock will begin compressing borrower EBITDA in earnest. If the 2007 OAS overlay holds, HY could reach 400 by late April — a level that took just 24 days from 300 in 2007.

The summer — June through August — is where we think the real damage happens. Insurance companies begin forced selling (the Z.1 data shows a consistent one-quarter lag from the trigger event). The BOJ likely hikes in May, initiating the carry unwind. Labor data inflects as claims rise and NFP weakens. The NAIC begins exercising its new rating challenge authority. This is the period where credit leads equity by 100-120 days, meaning equity markets finally catch down to what credit has been pricing since March.

By Q4 2026, we expect the full cascade: institutional failures or forced mergers among mid-size alternative asset managers, bank credit contraction (Z.1 shows a three-quarter lag), and HY OAS in the 700+ range. December positions capture this window with the highest conviction.

Peak institutional selling — the final capitulation — arrives Q1-Q2 2027, consistent with the five-quarter cascade duration verified across four independent analyses of Federal Reserve Z.1 data.

## Where We Could Be Wrong

We state this explicitly because intellectual honesty requires it, and because a future version of me reading this needs to know what to watch for.

The biggest risk to the thesis is a geopolitical resolution that crashes oil prices. If Hormuz reopens and Brent drops below $85, the oil demand destruction leg disappears, consumer pressure eases, and the Fed potentially gains room to cut. This would delay the timeline by 3-6 months, though it wouldn't resolve the underlying private credit solvency problem — just postpone it.

The second risk is a BTFP 2.0 — an emergency Fed facility specifically backstopping private credit or the institutions that hold it. This would be the "kitchen sink" response that was the only thing that produced sustained spread compression in 2007-2008. It would require a financial stability override of the inflation mandate, which means things would need to get bad enough to justify it — meaning we'd likely be partially right before the intervention changes the trajectory.

The third risk is that CCC stress is genuinely idiosyncratic rather than systemic. If CCC tightens back below 800 while HY stays below 380, the divergence was sector-specific (software/PC) rather than a leading indicator of broad credit stress. We'd need to reassess the entire framework.

## What This Means for Positioning

We are short regional banks (KRE, WAL, OZK), alternative asset managers (APO, ARES), and broad credit (HYG, IWM) through put options at various expiries. The expiry structure reflects the thesis timeline: April positions are lottery tickets, June positions capture the front edge of acceleration, August-September positions sit in the highest-probability window, and December positions carry the highest conviction with the most runway.

The discipline is to hold through relief rallies — which we expect, and which historically last 10-35 days in credit crises — without mistaking a positioning squeeze for a thesis change. Every prior crisis had multiple rallies within the broader deterioration. The 2007 September-October rally compressed spreads 98 basis points over 35 days while the underlying credit quality continued to worsen. We expect something similar, just shallower and shorter.
