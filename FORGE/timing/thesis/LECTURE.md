# The Timeline — Lecture Format

*A financial history professor walks through when we believe each phase arrives and why. Evidence-based, not vibes.*

**Created:** 2026-03-31 | **Revised:** 2026-03-31

---

I want to talk about timing. Not what's going to happen — I think by now most serious people have a sense that private credit is in trouble, that the oil shock is real, that the Fed is stuck. The more interesting and more useful question is when. Because in markets, being right about direction but wrong about timing is functionally identical to being wrong.

So let me walk through the timeline we're working with, phase by phase, and — critically — tell you why we believe each window is when it is. Not based on intuition. Based on specific historical data, mechanical triggers, and structural lags that we've verified across multiple independent analyses.

---

We start with where we are right now — late March 2026 — and the single most important data point for calibrating our position on the clock.

High-yield credit spreads are at 342 basis points. On July 2nd, 2007, they were at 303. We crossed 300 on February 27th. In 2007, the move from 300 to 320 took 16 calendar days. In 2026, it took 14. Nearly identical velocity. This is not a loose analogy — this is daily FRED data, pulled directly, compared day by day. The script that generates this overlay is in our research files and can be re-run as new data arrives.

Now, here's what matters about July 2007: the move from 300 to 400 took just 24 days. Twenty-four days. If we apply that same pace to our February 27th starting point, 400 would have arrived in late March. We're slightly behind that pace — we're at 342, not 400 — which tells us either the analog is running a few weeks slower than 2007, or we're about to see an acceleration. Either way, the evidence says 400 is an April or May event. Not a summer event. Not a fall event. Weeks away, not months.

Why does 400 matter? Because that's historically the zone where high-yield bond issuance begins to freeze. Companies that need to refinance can't. The extend-and-pretend math — which depends on borrowers being able to roll their debt — starts breaking mechanically. It's not a psychological threshold. It's a market functioning threshold.

---

Let me move to April specifically, because there are three mechanical catalysts stacked in the next few weeks.

First: the Treasury is meeting with state insurance regulators tomorrow, April 1st. This is significant not because of what they'll announce — these meetings rarely produce immediate action — but because of what it signals about the government's own internal timeline. In the 2007 crisis, the government didn't engage with the insurance sector until much later in the cascade. Treasury engaging at this stage — while we're still at Stage 2-3 in our framework — either means they're being proactive, which would be unprecedented, or they see something worse than what's publicly visible. Either interpretation is bearish for the timeline.

Second: CLO overcollateralization test results for the March reporting period will begin publishing in April. We already have one failure — BlackRock's private credit CLO failed its OC test in November 2025. These tests are mechanical: if the collateral value drops below a threshold, cash flows get diverted from equity tranches to senior tranches. The PE sponsor's economics get destroyed. This forces restructuring rather than further extension. Each new failure establishes a market-clearing price that makes other managers' internal marks harder to defend. We're projecting five or more failures in this reporting cycle based on the trajectory of collateral deterioration tracked by Trepp.

Third: the OWL put expires April 2nd — that's a position-specific event — but more broadly, April is when Q1 earnings begin for the financial sector. BDC quarterly NAV reports will start arriving. BCRED — Blackstone's $82 billion private credit fund — posted its first-ever negative return in February, down 0.4%. If Q1 shows a second consecutive negative quarter, that's the reflexive signal that accelerates retail redemption requests. Goldman's $45 to $70 billion outflow projection is modeled on exactly this kind of sequential deterioration.

---

Now, May through July. This is where we believe the acceleration happens, and let me explain exactly why we anchor on this window.

The 2007 analog is the primary evidence. July 2007 was the explosion month — HY spreads averaged plus 5.5 basis points per day, with a peak single-day move of 42 basis points. The total move that month was plus 121 basis points. That month alone took spreads from the low 300s to the mid 400s. If we're running a few weeks behind the 2007 pace — which the data suggests — then our "July 2007 equivalent" falls in approximately May or June 2026.

But it's not just the OAS overlay. There are independent mechanical triggers converging on this same window.

The Bank of Japan is likely to hike rates to 1.0% at their May 1st meeting. The Summary of Opinions from their March meeting was the most hawkish in the entire normalization cycle — one member dissented in favor of hiking, others discussed "rapid tightening" and "larger than usual" hikes. Market pricing is at 65% for May. Shunto wage negotiations came in at 5.26% for the third consecutive year above 5%. When the BOJ hikes, it initiates the carry trade unwind. In 1998, the carry unwind took about two months to play through. CFTC data shows yen shorts at negative 67,800 contracts — 38% of the July 2024 level that preceded the last mini-unwind. The velocity of buildup is what matters.

The Z.1 Flow of Funds data — and this is based on four independent analyses of Federal Reserve data — shows that insurance companies began forced selling one quarter after the initial trigger event in 2007. The trigger in our framework was the gating cascade that began in February 2026. Plus one quarter puts insurance forced selling in the May to July window. This isn't a guess. It's a structural lag observed in the data. Insurance companies have slower decision cycles, regulatory constraints on portfolio changes, and board-level approval requirements. One quarter is the mechanical minimum.

The oil demand destruction timeline reinforces this window independently. James Hamilton's framework shows that the first third of total demand destruction from an oil shock arrives at approximately three months. We mark the shock onset at March 2026 — Hormuz closure, Brent above $100. Three months is June. That's when consumer spending data begins reflecting the behavioral change that the $4 gas threshold triggers. The two-thirds and full damage arrive later — which is why December positions carry higher conviction — but the first visible macro deterioration hits in this window.

And labor. JOLTS — the Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey — inverted below 1.0 in January 2026. In 2007, the lag from JOLTS inversion to initial claims inflection was approximately six months. Six months from January is July. Now, there's noise in this — DOGE federal workforce reductions create distortions that didn't exist in any prior cycle — but the directional signal is consistent. Claims are at 210,000 today, which looks green on the surface. But continuing claims duration has spiked to a four-year high, meaning people aren't finding new jobs — they're falling off benefits. That's a leading indicator that the headline claims number is about to deteriorate.

So when we say May through July for acceleration, it's not one signal. It's five independent signals — the OAS velocity analog, the BOJ hike timing, the Z.1 insurance selling lag, the Hamilton demand destruction timeline, and the JOLTS-to-claims lag — all pointing at the same window. That convergence is what gives us confidence.

---

The relief rally question is critical for anyone trying to manage positions through this, so let me address timing on that specifically.

In 2007, the largest relief rally began September 10th and lasted 35 calendar days — about 27 trading days. Spreads compressed 98 basis points, from 479 to 381. It was driven by the Fed's surprise 50-basis-point cut on September 18th. The market had priced 25 — getting 50 was genuine shock and awe. During those 35 days, the underlying credit quality continued deteriorating. Merrill Lynch was accumulating what would become $16 billion in writedowns. Citigroup's exposure was growing. Nothing fundamental improved. But the price action was real, and it shook out a lot of early shorts.

We expect a relief rally in this cycle. We don't know exactly when. But we can bound it.

The trigger in 2007 was a policy surprise. In 2026, the Fed can't cut, so the most likely triggers are: a geopolitical headline suggesting de-escalation on Iran, a weaker-than-expected inflation print that opens the door to future cuts, or simply technical positioning getting stretched enough that a squeeze ignites. The "Trump willing to end war without Hormuz" headline this morning is exactly the kind of thing that generates a one-to-three day bounce — but not a sustained multi-week rally, because nothing structural changed.

Our estimate for a real relief rally — one that lasts more than a few days — is 40 to 60 basis points of compression over 10 to 15 trading days. The evidence for this: in 2007, every rate cut after the September surprise produced diminishing returns. The October cut — 25 basis points, fully expected — produced zero compression. Spreads actually widened 137 basis points over the following 20 days. The January 2008 emergency 75-basis-point cut produced a sharp 54-basis-point tightening that reversed within two weeks. Without the surprise element, and without any cut at all, we're left with pure positioning dynamics, which historically produce shorter and shallower bounces.

The positioning rule we're working with: in 2007, you sold the rally 27 days after the surprise cut. In 2026, sell 10 to 15 days after any relief rally begins.

---

Now let me talk about August through October — what we're calling the full cascade phase.

The August 12th date is hard-coded. That's when the SEC issues its decision on Egan-Jones — the rating agency whose private letter ratings were found to average three notches above the NAIC's own internal assessments. If the SEC materially constrains Egan-Jones's ability to rate private credit — and the trajectory of the proceedings suggests this is more likely than not, with a 60% probability in our framework — then $350 billion or more in insurer-held private credit assets face re-rating. Insurance companies would need to hold more capital against these assets overnight, potentially triggering forced selling to meet solvency requirements. This is a date-certain catalyst with an asymmetric outcome.

The Z.1 data tells us that bank credit contraction — the point at which banks go from being net buyers of credit to net sellers — historically lags the initial trigger by three quarters. Three quarters from the February 2026 gating cascade puts bank pullback initiation in the August to November window. In 2007, banks were actually net buyers through Q3 and Q4 2007 — they were absorbing SIV portfolios onto their balance sheets, which most people don't realize. They didn't flip to net selling until Q2 2008. That three-quarter lag was structural — banks have slower balance sheet adjustment cycles, regulatory capital buffers, and institutional inertia. We see no reason it would be faster this time, and some reasons it could be slightly slower given that direct bank exposure to private credit is more distributed than their SIV exposure was in 2007.

The equity market catch-down should also arrive in this window. In 2007, the lag from HY OAS crossing 300 to the equity market peak was approximately 100 to 120 days. The Dow literally hit its all-time high on October 9th — 99 days after HY crossed 300 on July 2nd. Equities were oscillating within 8% of their peak the entire time credit was screaming. We crossed 300 on February 27th. Adding 120 days gives us late June. Adding the buffer for the fact that our pace is slightly slower than 2007 pushes it toward July or August. Either way, the equity market catching down to what credit is already pricing is a Q3 event.

---

Q4 2026 is where we place the institutional failure phase, and the evidence is primarily structural.

By this point, if the timeline holds, HY OAS would be in the 600 to 700 range — the zone where, in 2007, Citigroup was reporting $18 billion in writedowns, Merrill Lynch was being gutted, and Bear Stearns was months from collapse. The Z.1 cascade would be in full swing — insurance forced selling active for two quarters, bank pullback beginning, pension funds starting to reassess their 8% private credit allocations.

The Hamilton oil shock damage reaches its peak in this window as well. The four-quarter lag from the March 2026 shock onset places peak GDP drag in Q1 2027, but the market prices forward — the anticipation of that damage shows up in asset prices three to six months early, meaning Q4 2026.

December positions are our highest conviction for this reason. They capture: the full OAS widening trajectory, the Z.1 bank pullback, the Hamilton peak damage anticipation, the post-Egan-Jones fallout, and the compounding effect of six to nine months of deteriorating fundamentals with no policy offset. The December window doesn't require anything exceptional to happen. It just requires the existing trends to continue without a massive coordinated intervention.

---

Finally — Q1 to Q2 2027. Peak institutional selling.

The Z.1 data is unambiguous on this. Across four independent analyses, the cascade from first seller to peak selling took five quarters. Every time. The mechanism is structural: different types of institutions operate on different timescales. Money market funds and broker-dealers move immediately. Insurance companies lag by one quarter. Banks lag by three quarters. Mutual funds — and this surprised us — were actually net buyers through most of the 2007 crisis in aggregate. The selling only became a cliff after Lehman in Q4 2008, five quarters after the initial trigger. Private pensions, in some analyses, never sold at all.

Five quarters from our February 2026 trigger puts peak selling in Q1 to Q2 2027. This is where the full damage is realized, where recovery values are established, and where the next cycle begins.

We don't currently have positions that extend to this window, and we may not need them. The December expiries capture the acceleration and cascade phases. But knowing the full timeline matters for understanding what comes after — and for recognizing that even after our positions pay off, the underlying process will still be unfolding.

---

Let me close with the single most important calibration tool we have.

The CCC-to-HY ratio is at 2.96. In every prior episode — 2007, 2011, 2015, 2020 — this ratio compressed as the crisis broadened. In COVID, the ratio went from 2.83 to 1.80 in 45 trading days as HY widened 221%. The compression is what tells you the crisis has gone from sector-specific to systemic. It's the transition from "private credit has a problem" to "the financial system has a problem."

When that ratio starts falling — not because CCC is getting better, but because HY is getting worse — that's the confirmation that the timeline is activating. That's when May through July stops being a projection and starts being reality.

Watch the ratio. Everything else follows from it.
