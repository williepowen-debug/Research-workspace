# How Big Is the FABN Market and Who Holds It?

## What I Need

A factual breakdown of the Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (FABN) market — the wholesale funding channel that PE-controlled life insurers use to fund illiquid private credit investments.

## Context

Private equity firms now control ~$700B of US life insurance assets. Key players: Apollo/Athene, Blackstone/Evermore, KKR/Global Atlantic, Ares/Aspida. These insurers issue FABNs — securitized funding agreements sold to institutional investors — as a major funding source. They also borrow ~$160B from the Federal Home Loan Banks.

I'm trying to understand whether FABNs are a systemic vulnerability. If the issuing insurer gets downgraded, and FABNs are short-dated rollover instruments held by money market funds, that's a commercial-paper-style funding crisis. If they're long-dated and held by pensions, it's a slow bleed. The holder base and maturity profile determine the contagion channel.

## Questions

1. What is total FABN outstanding across all issuers? How has this grown over the past 5 years?
2. How much is issued by PE-controlled insurers specifically (Athene, Evermore, Global Atlantic, Aspida) vs traditional insurers (MetLife, Prudential)?
3. Who holds FABNs? Break down by: money market funds, bank portfolios, pension funds, foreign investors, other insurers. If MMFs hold a significant share, which funds and what % of their AUM?
4. What are typical maturities? Is this a rollover market (90-day to 1-year, like commercial paper) or termed out (3-5+ years)?
5. What are the rating requirements? At what issuer rating level do MMFs or other holders become forced sellers? Where are PE-insurers currently rated (AM Best + S&P) and how many notches from those thresholds?
6. What happened to FABN spreads and volumes during 2008 and March 2020?

## What I Want Back

A clear picture of FABN market size, growth, holder composition, maturity profile, and rating sensitivity. Specific numbers wherever possible. If data is sparse, tell me what's available and what isn't — that opacity itself is useful information.
