# Prompt: Zombie Lending in Private Credit — What Forces Recognition?

## Context

Sascha Steffen (Frankfurt School of Finance, formerly Bundesbank) has drawn an explicit comparison between US private credit's "extend-and-pretend" practices and Japanese zombie lending of the 1990s-2000s. The IMF has amplified this, finding that **nearly 50% of direct lending borrowers have negative free operating cash flows** — they are alive only because lenders keep extending.

**The mechanics of zombie lending in 2026 private credit:**

- **94% of defaults are distressed exchanges** (DBRS) — lenders swap debt terms rather than recognizing losses. Borrowers get PIK (paid-in-kind) toggles, maturity extensions, covenant relief. The loan stays "current" on paper.
- **PIK at extremes:** FS KKR at 14.7% PIK vs 6.3% peer median. PIK is literally capitalizing unpaid interest — making the loan larger while the borrower deteriorates.
- **"Amendment and extension" (A&E):** $270B in leveraged loans were amended and extended in 2024 alone. This pushes maturity walls out, delaying but compounding eventual losses.
- **NAV markdowns suppressed:** BDC managers have incentive to mark slowly (management fees based on NAV/AUM). Blue Owl marked at 99.7¢ while secondary market traded at ~85¢. BlackRock TCP eventually marked down 19% in a single quarter — implying prior quarters were overstated.
- **Distressed exchange = no default event for CLO purposes.** OC tests don't trip because the loan isn't technically in default. This suppresses the signal that should trigger protective mechanisms.

**Scale of the problem:**
- $1.7T direct lending + broader PC ecosystem $3-4T
- 12% of borrowers have negative cash flow; 13% have coverage ratios <1x (can't service debt from operations)
- 2021-2022 vintage LBOs (done at low rates, high leverage) are the worst cohort
- Software sector: 37 managers holding the same 15 distressed credits, $25B trading below 80¢
- DBRS: 16% of rated universe is CCC-C, average time to default 3.4 years from distressed exchange

**The Japanese precedent:**
- Japanese banks extended zombie loans for a DECADE (1991-2001) to avoid recognizing losses
- Peek & Rosengren (2005): zombie lending reduced productivity, prevented creative destruction, prolonged the "lost decade"
- Eventual recognition forced by: FSA inspections (2002-2003), Basel II capital requirements, and Takenaka's "financial revitalization program"
- Total recognized losses when forced: ~¥100T ($800B+)
- Key insight: the LONGER you delay recognition, the LARGER the eventual loss (because zombies consume resources and deteriorate further)
