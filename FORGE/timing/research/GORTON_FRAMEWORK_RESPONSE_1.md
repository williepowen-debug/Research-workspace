# Gorton's panic framework meets private credit's slow-motion run

**The 2026 private credit crisis maps onto Gary Gorton's 2007-2008 panic framework with uncomfortable precision — but with one crucial structural difference that changes everything about speed.** Gorton's central insight was that financial panics occur when "information-insensitive" debt — debt so safe nobody analyzes the collateral — crosses a threshold and becomes "information-sensitive," triggering a coordination failure where all holders simultaneously demand to know what's in their portfolios. In 2007, this transition destroyed the repo market in weeks. In 2026, **the same information regime shift is underway in private credit**, but structural circuit breakers (redemption gates, locked-up capital, discretionary marks) are converting what would have been an acute panic into a prolonged siege. The $1.7 trillion private credit market now exceeds 2007 subprime ($1.3 trillion) in raw size, 94% of defaults are distressed exchanges masking true impairment, and the first coordinated regulatory response — Treasury Secretary Bessent's March 2026 meetings with state insurance regulators — signals that policymakers see systemic risk building. The question is no longer whether Gorton's framework applies, but which stage of the cascade we occupy and what triggers the next phase.

---

## The 2007 chain mapped link-by-link to 2026

Gorton documented a precise transmission chain in 2007: deteriorating fundamentals in subprime mortgages were revealed by the ABX index (a traded price on opaque assets), which forced CDO mark-to-market losses, which impaired bank balance sheets, which froze the interbank and repo markets. Each link had a functional equivalent in the current crisis — though the instruments, institutions, and speeds differ materially.

| 2007 Link | 2007 Instrument/Institution | 2026 Equivalent | 2026 Instrument/Institution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deteriorating collateral** | Subprime mortgages (2/28 ARMs dependent on home price appreciation) | Overleveraged 2021-2022 vintage LBO loans | Floating-rate direct loans to mid-market companies at 5-6x leverage, underwritten at near-zero rates |
| **Transparent price on opaque assets** | ABX.HE index (launched Jan 2006; CDS on subprime RMBS) | BDC stock prices + semi-liquid fund redemption queues | CWBDC Index at 17% discount to NAV; BCRED 7.9% redemption requests; OBDC at 25% discount |
| **Forced mark-to-market losses** | CDO tranches marked to ABX-implied prices | CLO OC test failures + forced asset sales by gated funds | BlackRock Baker CLO 2021-1 OC breach; Blue Owl selling $1.4B at par; Goldman seeking to sell $800M-$1B |
| **Balance sheet impairment** | Bank writedowns (Citi, Merrill, UBS: $100B+ combined) | PE-controlled insurer asset deterioration | Athene/Apollo ($387B), Global Atlantic/KKR ($201B), Blackstone Credit ($484B) — 1/3 of assets are Level 3 |
| **Funding market freeze** | Repo haircut spiral ($10T market; haircuts went from 0% to ~50%) | Redemption gate cascade + bank withdrawal from fund financing | $4.6B trapped capital; $13B Q1 redemption requests; 7+ fund gates activated; bank NAV/warehouse lines tightening |
| **Amplification mechanism** | Synthetic CDOs, CDS ($62.2T notional) multiplied losses 5-50x | **No direct equivalent** — but insurance company allocations (~$700B PE-controlled) and bank-NBFI lending ($2.3T) create indirect amplification | Bank exposure to NBFIs = 16.2% of top-25 bank loans; insurance companies with 20% affiliated lending |

**Where we are in the cascade: between links 2 and 3.** The collateral deterioration (link 1) is well-established — Lincoln International data shows **70% of troubled private credit debt is from 2021-2022 vintages**, Fitch's default rate hit 9.2% for 2025, and 40% of private credit borrowers have negative free cash flow. The transparent price signals (link 2) are flashing — BDC discounts, fund gates, and software loan prices below 80 cents all function as the "ABX" revealing stress. We are now entering link 3: forced mark-to-market through actual transactions. Blue Owl's $1.4 billion asset sale and Goldman's $800M-$1B disposition are creating observable price points that will pressure other managers' discretionary marks. The critical question is whether link 4 (insurance company balance sheet impairment) and link 5 (funding market freeze) will follow.

## The opacity problem is worse than 2007

Gorton's deepest insight, developed with Bengt Holmström and Tri Vi Dang, was that opacity in debt markets is a *feature*, not a bug — until it isn't. As Holmström articulated: "Money markets are about obviating the need for price discovery using over-collateralized debt." The system functions through **"symmetric ignorance"** — when neither side has an informational advantage, markets are free of adverse selection fears and therefore liquid. This "blissful state" breaks when a public shock makes private information relevant, triggering a discontinuous regime shift from "no questions asked" to "no one will trade."

Private credit's opacity exceeds that of 2007's CDOs in several dimensions. CDOs were complex but eventually priceable — they referenced pools of mortgages with observable prepayment and delinquency data, and the ABX provided daily marks by January 2006. Private credit has **no equivalent pricing infrastructure**: loans are held at manager-determined marks using internal models, valuations are updated quarterly at best, there is no standardized reporting across funds, and the secondary market is essentially nonexistent. The IMF's April 2024 Global Financial Stability Report explicitly warned of "stale and potentially subjective valuations" and "unclear connections between participants." The FDIC's 2025 Risk Review noted that private credit valuations are "often stale given the lack of robust secondary markets and infrequent appraisals, raising the potential for sudden repricing events."

**The "nobody knows what's in the CDO" equivalent for 2026 is that nobody knows the true mark on a private credit portfolio.** The distressed exchange phenomenon crystallizes this opacity problem. When **94% of defaults are distressed exchanges** — PIK deferrals, covenant waivers, maturity extensions — rather than true restructurings with loss recognition, the system is actively suppressing information production. Lincoln International's data shows "bad PIK" (mid-contract conversion from cash to PIK) reached **6.4% of all deals** by Q4 2025, up from 2.5% three years earlier. Academic researcher Sascha Steffen compares this mechanism to Japanese "zombie lending" of the 1990s — contract-embedded forbearance that maintains the fiction of performing status while the underlying economics deteriorate.

The Gorton framework predicts that this accumulated opacity creates a *worse* eventual panic, not a milder one. Because no one has invested in understanding collateral quality during the "information-insensitive" period, when a shock forces the transition, the knowledge gap is enormous and cannot be bridged quickly. Every portfolio becomes suspect simultaneously. The IMF's April 2025 report found that **nearly half of direct lending borrowers had negative free operating cash flows**, "prolonging their reliance on payment-in-kind provisions and amend-and-extend" — precisely the kind of hidden deterioration that creates the conditions for a Gorton-style information cascade.

## Three signals are playing the ABX's role simultaneously

The ABX index was uniquely powerful as an early warning system because it combined four properties: it was traded (real-time price discovery), it referenced opaque assets (subprime RMBS), it was used as a benchmark (for marking CDO positions), and it created a feedback loop (ABX declines forced more selling, driving further ABX declines). No single 2026 instrument combines all four properties, but **a composite of three signals collectively performs the ABX function**.

**BDC stock prices are the primary equivalent.** Publicly traded BDCs hold private credit portfolios and trade on exchanges, providing real-time market pricing on otherwise opaque assets. The Cliffwater BDC Index at a **17% discount to NAV** — matching its June 2022 trough — is the single most-watched indicator. Individual BDC discounts tell a granular story: ARCC (the bellwether) at 9-10% discount, BXSL at 12%, OBDC at **25%**, and PSEC at 59%. When the market prices a BDC at 75 cents on the dollar of reported NAV, it is explicitly saying: the marks are wrong. J.P. Morgan, Goldman Sachs, and Morningstar are all using BDC price-to-NAV as their primary private credit health gauge.

**Semi-liquid fund redemption rates are the secondary equivalent — and arguably the most alarming.** In 2007, the ABX *declined in price*. In 2026, the equivalent signal is funds *refusing to return capital*. The Congressional Research Service documented seven major fund gates in a single March 2026 report: BCRED ($82B, 7.9% redemptions vs. 5% cap), Apollo Debt Solutions ($15.1B, 11.2% vs. 5% cap), Ares Strategic Income ($10.7B, 11.6% vs. 5% cap), and four others. Total Q1 redemption requests exceeded **$13 billion**, with only about two-thirds honored. This creates a potent feedback loop: each gate activation generates media coverage, which triggers more redemption requests at other funds, which triggers more gates. Goldman Sachs projects the retail private credit sector could shed **$45-70 billion** over two years.

**Leveraged loan prices — particularly the software sub-index — are the tertiary equivalent**, functioning as the closest analogue to the ABX's direct pricing of underlying collateral. The Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index fell 0.78% in February 2026 (worst monthly performance in three-plus years), with the software segment down **7.9% from its January peak**. A record $25 billion of software-sector loans now trade below the distress threshold of 80 cents on the dollar. Since software comprises 10-13% of CLO assets and an even higher share of recent private credit origination, these observable prices are forcing mark-to-market recognition in a way that closed private credit portfolios cannot avoid forever.

## PE insurers are the new monolines — but the transmission mechanism differs

In 2007, monoline insurers (AMBAC, MBIA) were hidden amplifiers. They guaranteed **$3.5 trillion** in total debt including over $1 trillion in CDOs, with razor-thin capital ($5.6 billion equity at Ambac against $32 billion in CDO exposure). When they lost their AAA ratings, it cascaded instantly: banks had to re-mark every CDO tranche the monolines had wrapped, municipal bond markets froze, and counterparty chains broke. The structural parallel to PE-controlled insurers in 2026 is real but operates through different channels.

PE firms now control approximately **$700 billion in life insurance assets** across 137-139 identified PE-owned US insurers. The largest — Apollo/Athene ($387B gross invested assets), KKR/Global Atlantic ($201B), Blackstone Credit & Insurance (~$484B AUM), Ares/Aspida ($27B) — hold significant private credit allocations, with roughly **one-third of Athene's and Global Atlantic's total assets classified as Level 3** (no observable market inputs). AM Best found that approximately **20% of investments** at both Athene and Global Atlantic flow to affiliated PE funds, creating correlated risk between the asset manager and the insurance company.

The BIS published a landmark October 2025 paper finding that private credit ratings used by insurers may be **"systematically inflated"** by smaller rating agencies, and estimated that publicly traded North American life insurers face a potential **$150 billion capital shortfall** in a severe downturn — more than triple the figure two decades ago. UBS Chairman Colm Kelleher explicitly warned in November 2025 that the insurance industry's "ratings arbitrage" represents a "looming systemic risk" akin to pre-2008 subprime practices.

The transmission mechanism differs from monolines in critical ways. Monoline failure was *instantaneous* — a downgrade immediately repriced all wrapped securities. PE insurer stress would be *gradual but potentially more prolonged*: asset deterioration forces mark-downs, which reduce solvency ratios, which trigger regulatory action, which could force portfolio sales into an illiquid market, creating a self-reinforcing spiral. The PHL Variable Insurance Company case — a PE-affiliated insurer (Golden Gate Capital/Nassau Financial) that was found to have **negative $900 million** in capital, heading toward liquidation with $1.46 billion owed to policyholders — demonstrates that the risk is not theoretical. Perhaps most importantly, there is **no federal backstop** for insurance companies (unlike FDIC for banks); state guaranty funds provide only $250,000-$500,000 per policy.

Treasury Secretary Bessent's March 29-30, 2026 meetings with state insurance regulators represent the first coordinated federal response. Bessent warned in February that the administration would not allow retirement accounts to become "a dumping ground for rotten assets." The NAIC is simultaneously restructuring its oversight framework, with new authority for the SVO to challenge private credit ratings that differ by three or more notches from its own analysis — a power that, if exercised broadly, could itself trigger the ratings cliff that BIS has warned about.

## The crisis will unfold in slow motion — unless it doesn't

The structural comparison between 2007 and 2026 reveals that the two most powerful acceleration mechanisms from 2007 are absent. **First, there is no repo market equivalent** that can freeze overnight. In 2007, Gorton calculated that repo haircuts rising from 0% to 30% created a $3 trillion margin call that shadow banks simply could not meet. Private credit funds raise capital from institutional LPs with multi-year lockups; semi-liquid vehicles have 5% quarterly redemption gates. Goldman Sachs characterizes the likely trajectory as "a slow bleed, not a cliff." **Second, there is no synthetic amplification layer.** In 2007, $1.2 trillion of subprime mortgages generated $5+ trillion in synthetic CDO exposure and a $62.2 trillion CDS market. AIG alone wrote $400 billion in CDS protection it could not honor. Private credit has no equivalent derivatives chain multiplying losses.

These structural differences suggest the acute phase would unfold **2-3x slower than 2007** — a 12-24 month credit correction rather than the concentrated six-week panic between BNP Paribas (August 9, 2007) and the full interbank freeze. However, several factors unique to 2026 could compress this timeline:

- **The Fed is handcuffed.** At 3.5-3.75% with PCE inflation revised to 2.7% (and the OECD forecasting 4.2%), futures markets price a 52% probability of a rate *hike* by year-end — the polar opposite of 2007, when the Fed had 525 basis points of cutting room. If borrower stress accelerates and the Fed cannot respond, the "slow burn" of floating-rate interest costs consuming borrower cash flow intensifies rather than being cushioned.
- **The Iran-Hormuz oil shock is an unprecedented simultaneous stressor.** With Brent crude above $100/bbl following the March 4 Strait of Hormuz closure, the Dallas Fed estimates a **2.9 percentage point drag on annualized GDP growth**. In 2007, the financial crisis evolved in isolation before causing recession. In 2026, the credit stress and the real economic shock are compounding simultaneously.
- **The $500-600 billion leveraged loan maturity wall creates fixed deadlines.** Unlike 2007, where stress built organically, hundreds of billions in 2020-2022 vintage leveraged loans must refinance in 2026-2028. If CLO formation stalls and private credit funds are gating redemptions, a refinancing freeze emerges on a defined, immovable schedule.
- **No Fed backstop exists for non-bank lenders.** Banks accessed the discount window; non-bank private credit funds have no equivalent emergency facility. Any systemic response would require *new* emergency powers.

| Feature | 2007-2008 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw collateral pool** | $1.3T subprime | $1.7T private credit |
| **Synthetic amplification** | $62.2T CDS notional; 5-50x leverage on underlying | None — losses confined to deployed capital |
| **Funding vulnerability** | Overnight repo; ABCP (run-prone) | LP lockups; 5% quarterly gates (run-resistant) |
| **Valuation regime** | Mark-to-market (forced selling spiral) | Amortized cost / model-marked (extend and pretend) |
| **Fed policy room** | 525 bps of cuts available | ~75 bps at most; hike probability 52% |
| **Exogenous shocks** | None initially | Iran/oil shock, tariffs, AI disruption |
| **Federal backstop** | Fed facilities (PDCF, TALF, TARP) for banks | No backstop for non-banks |
| **Time from first signal to systemic crisis** | ~30 months (early 2006 → Sep 2008) | TBD — likely 18-36 months if it follows the pattern |
| **"Information sensitivity" threshold** | Crossed Aug 9, 2007 (BNP Paribas) | Partially crossed — fund gates and BDC discounts signal transition underway |

## What triggers the full information-sensitivity threshold crossing

Gorton's framework identifies a specific regime shift: the moment when enough agents begin producing information about collateral quality that *uninformed* agents fear adverse selection and refuse to trade entirely. In 2007, this threshold crossed in two phases — partially in June-July 2007 (Bear Stearns hedge funds), and fully on August 9, 2007 (BNP Paribas fund freeze). The question for 2026 is what event completes the transition from "some funds are gating" to "all private credit is suspect."

The most likely triggers, in approximate order of probability:

**A major BDC or semi-liquid fund marks down NAV sharply (15%+) in a single quarter.** Currently, managers have discretion over marks, and the incentive is to mark gradually. But if a fund faces forced asset sales at prices materially below reported NAV — as nearly happened when Saba Capital bid for OBDC II shares at a 35% discount — the observed transaction price would force other managers to re-evaluate their own marks. If BCRED, the $82 billion bellwether, reported a significant quarterly NAV decline, it would function as the "BNP Paribas moment" — transforming vague unease into concrete evidence that marks are wrong across the industry.

**The NAIC exercises its new authority to challenge private credit ratings broadly.** The SVO's power to challenge ratings differing by three or more notches could trigger a sector-wide re-rating. If hundreds of private credit positions at PE-affiliated insurers are simultaneously downgraded, the capital charge increases would force portfolio sales into a market with no natural buyers — creating exactly the fire-sale dynamic that BIS has warned about.

**A CLO AAA downgrade wave.** While CLO AAA tranches remain well-protected (requiring ~30% cumulative defaults to impair), the BlackRock Baker CLO 2021-1 case — where the senior OC test failed, an event "unusual for higher-rated tranches" — shows cracks forming. If multiple CLO AAAs are placed on review, it would signal that the protective structure itself is insufficient, triggering risk reassessment across all structured private credit.

**An insurance company liquidity event.** The PHL Variable case ($900M negative capital) involved a smaller insurer. A liquidity crisis at a Tier 1 PE-affiliated insurer — Athene, Global Atlantic, or Blackstone's insurance platform — would be qualitatively different, given their $200-$400 billion balance sheets and the interconnectedness with their PE parent firms' fund portfolios. A surrender wave (policyholders demanding annuity payouts) combined with illiquid private credit assets would be the insurance-sector equivalent of a bank run.

**The Iran-oil shock persists through Q3 2026, triggering recession.** If the Strait of Hormuz remains restricted and Brent stays above $100, the Dallas Fed's 2.9pp GDP drag would push the economy into recession. In recession, the 40% of private credit borrowers with negative free cash flow become functionally insolvent. The "extend and pretend" strategy collapses because there is nothing to extend toward — no recovery in revenue to justify the deferral. At that point, Gorton's information-sensitivity threshold crosses definitively: every private credit holder needs to know exactly what's in their portfolio, and the "symmetric ignorance" that made the market function evaporates.

## Conclusion: Rhyming history with structural variants

The application of Gorton's framework to the 2026 private credit crisis reveals a system that has crossed the *early* stages of the information-sensitivity transition but has not yet reached the full panic threshold. The **"symmetric ignorance" that sustained $1.7 trillion in private credit lending is breaking down**: BDC discounts of 17-25% are the market's explicit statement that reported NAVs are wrong, fund gates affecting $4.6 billion in trapped capital are the visible manifestation of liquidity withdrawal, and the 94% distressed-exchange rate is evidence of systematic information suppression. The academic literature — particularly the IMF's April 2024 and April 2025 Global Financial Stability Reports, the Fed's May 2025 FEDS Notes, and the BIS's October 2025 insurance sector analysis — confirms that the structural vulnerabilities map precisely onto Gorton's preconditions for panic: opacity, layered complexity, interconnectedness, and information-insensitive debt backed by deteriorating collateral.

Three novel insights emerge from this analysis. **First**, the absence of synthetic amplification (no CDS/CDO chain) is partially offset by the PE-insurer channel — $700 billion in insurance assets with 20% affiliated lending creates correlated losses between asset managers and the institutions holding retirement savings, a channel that did not exist in 2007. **Second**, the "extend and pretend" dynamic is not merely delaying losses but *actively preventing the information production* that Gorton identifies as the market's self-correcting mechanism — meaning the eventual repricing, when it comes, will reflect accumulated rather than current-period losses. **Third**, the combination of the Iran oil shock, tariff uncertainty, and AI-driven software sector disruption means the private credit system faces multiple simultaneous fundamental shocks rather than the single-vector (housing) shock of 2007, compressing the timeline between "early warning" and "information-sensitivity threshold crossing" even as structural features (gates, lockups) slow the *acute panic* phase.

The most dangerous scenario is not a Lehman-style sudden collapse but rather a Japanese-style prolonged credit stagnation: years of extend-and-pretend, zombie borrowers, trapped capital, insurance company opacity, and gradual economic drag — punctuated by episodic acute crises when individual funds or insurers exhaust their ability to defer recognition. The institutional research from the IMF, BIS, Fed, and FSB unanimously identifies the preconditions for this scenario as present. Whether it materializes depends on variables — the duration of the oil shock, Fed policy flexibility, and the NAIC's willingness to force re-rating — that remain genuinely uncertain as of March 30, 2026.
