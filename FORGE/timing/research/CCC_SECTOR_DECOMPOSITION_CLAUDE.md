# CCC spreads at 1,013bps: sector concentration, not systemic contagion

**The ~1,013 basis point OAS on the ICE BofA CCC & Lower US High Yield Index is primarily a signal of concentrated sector-specific distress—not broad-based systemic stress like December 2007.** The unprecedented 2.96× ratio of CCC-to-broad-HY spreads reflects a structural divergence driven by three overlapping forces: a PE-backed software credit crisis accelerated by AI disruption fears, legacy media/telecom secular decline, and the compositional transformation of the HY index itself (BB share at **58%**, an all-time high, compressing the denominator). Stripping software/tech from the CCC bond index would reduce OAS by an estimated **50–75 basis points**, while removing the broader distressed cohort (software + legacy media/telecom, ~25% of CCC) could bring the reading to approximately **800–850 bps**—still elevated, but mapping closer to mid-2015 energy stress than pre-GFC systemic risk. Multiple authoritative sources—State Street, PineBridge, Janus Henderson—explicitly characterize current CCC stress as "idiosyncratic" and "not a broader systemic concern." However, the private credit channel (20–25% software exposure, largely opaque) and the collapse in first-lien recovery rates to **37.7%** versus a 25-year average of **62.3%** introduce genuine tail risks that the broad HY spread at 342 bps is not pricing.

---

## The CCC bond index is dominated by telecom and media, not software

The exact sector weights of the ICE BofA CCC & Lower index are proprietary, but analysis of the BondBloxx XCCC ETF (193 bonds, 121 issuers, ~$117.9 billion market cap as of March 30, 2026) provides a reliable proxy. The composition diverges sharply from the broader HY index:

| Sector | Estimated CCC weight | Broad HY weight | Key issuers |
|--------|---------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Telecom / Media / Cable | **~25–30%** | ~14.8% | DISH DBS, CSC Holdings (Altice USA), Clear Channel, iHeart, Gray Media |
| Insurance / Financial Services | ~15–18% | ~5.7% | Alliant Holdings, Acrisure, Ardonagh, Howden UK |
| Healthcare | ~10–12% | ~11.3% | Community Health Systems, LifePoint, Surgery Center of America, Bausch Health |
| Technology / Software | **~5–8%** | ~8.3% | McAfee, Athenahealth, Rocket Software, Rackspace, GoTo Group |
| Industrials / Capital Goods | ~8–10% | ~10.5% | Mauser Packaging, Brand Industrial, Cooper-Standard |
| Consumer / Retail | ~7–9% | ~18.4% | PetSmart, Michaels, Mavis Tire |
| Energy | ~5–8% | ~10.9% | Transocean, Calumet, Coronado Finance |
| Security / Business Services | ~5–7% | — | Garda World, Allied Universal |

**The critical finding is that telecom/media/cable—not technology/software—is the largest CCC sector in the bond index**, at roughly 25–30% of index weight. Names like DISH DBS, CSC Holdings (Altice USA), Clear Channel Outdoor, and iHeart Communications represent secular decline stories (cord-cutting, digital advertising substitution) unrelated to the PE-backed software thesis. Software's weight in the CCC *bond* index is a modest **5–8%**, far less than its **~16% weight in the leveraged loan index** where the software crisis is most acute. This distinction matters enormously for interpreting the CCC OAS signal.

## Software distress is catastrophic in loans, but the bond market tells a different story

The PE-backed software crisis is real, accelerating, and quantitatively staggering—but it is concentrated in leveraged loans and private credit rather than high-yield bonds.

PitchBook/LCD confirmed that a record **$25 billion** of software-sector loans in the Morningstar LSTA US Leveraged Loan Index traded below the 80-cent distress threshold as of January 31, 2026. By February 28, this figure rose to **$32 billion**—a $7 billion increase in one month. Software represents just **~13% of the leveraged loan index** by weight but accounts for **33% of all distressed loans** by dollar volume. The sector-level distress ratio for software loans reached approximately **16.5%**, versus 6.43% for the overall index and an implied **~5.0% ex-software**.

The divergence in average bid prices is stark: software loans traded at an average bid of **87.64 cents** (the lowest since March 2020), while non-software loans averaged **96.12 cents**. At the start of January 2026, 47% of software names in the loan index were trading above par; by month-end, fewer than 10% were. The January–February selloff was dubbed **"software-mageddon"** and froze new issuance entirely—there were zero leveraged loan deals for software companies as of late February 2026.

The high-yield bond market, by contrast, has materially less software exposure. Per JPMorgan, software comprises only **3% of the US HY bond market** versus **14% of leveraged loans** and **21% of private credit**. This explains why CCC bond OAS, while elevated, is not as extreme as loan market distress metrics would imply. Technology was the widest-widening sector in HY bonds in 2025 (+71 bps per CreditSights), but healthcare actually *tightened* 74 bps and telecom tightened 155 bps at the full-index level—suggesting the CCC bond spread widening is less sector-concentrated than the loan market narrative implies.

## The 2020–2022 LBO vintage is the proximate cause

The software credit crisis traces directly to the 2020–2022 PE buyout wave. The three largest LBO term loans of 2022 were all B-minus-rated software companies—**Athenahealth ($17B LBO, Bain/Hellman & Friedman), McAfee, and Citrix Systems (Vista Equity/$16.5B take-private)**—representing nearly a quarter of total LBO loan volume that year. Average debt-to-EBITDA for LBO-financed leveraged loans reached **6.1× in H1 2022**, the highest since 2007.

These vintages were underwritten at peak valuations with aggressive leverage on the assumption that "sticky" SaaS recurring revenue would protect cash flows. The emergence of agentic AI tools from Anthropic and OpenAI—capable of automating functions across legal, financial, analytical, and sales workflows—has prompted a fundamental reassessment of that assumption. PE firms now own **92% of all B-minus rated index loans**, **82% of CCC+ rated loans**, and **85% of CCC-flat rated loans**. The concentration is extraordinary: 50% of all software-sector loans carry B-minus or lower ratings, and **26% are already rated CCC**.

The downstream consequences are visible in the BDC/private credit channel. Moody's downgraded **FS KKR Capital Corp to Ba1 (junk) from Baa3** on March 24, 2026—the first major BDC fallen angel of this cycle—citing non-accrual rates of 5.5% and software/services exposure of 16.4% of the portfolio. Blue Owl Capital restricted redemptions in retail-facing vehicles. UBS warned that in an aggressive AI disruption scenario, private credit default rates could climb to **13%**. The Cliffwater BDC Index was down ~11.5% YTD and -20% over twelve months through February 2026.

## Estimating CCC OAS excluding software: roughly 940–960 bps

No major bank has publicly performed the exact "strip out software/tech from CCC bond OAS" calculation, which represents a gap in publicly available research. However, the data allows a reasonable estimation framework:

Starting from CCC OAS of ~1,013 bps and estimated software/tech weight of ~7% of the CCC bond index, with software-sector CCC bonds likely trading at average OAS of ~1,500–2,500 bps (implied by the severe price declines and the 87.64-cent average bid for performing software loans), the software contribution to index OAS is approximately **100–140 bps**. Removing this yields an ex-software CCC OAS of approximately **940–960 bps**—a reduction of 50–75 basis points. This is meaningful but not transformative.

The more relevant exercise may be stripping out the broader **"concentrated distress cohort"—software plus legacy media/telecom names** (DISH, Altice, Clear Channel, iHeart, Gray Media), which collectively represent roughly 25% of the CCC index and trade at significantly wider spreads than the median. Removing this cohort could bring CCC OAS to approximately **800–850 bps**, which maps to stressed-but-not-crisis levels comparable to mid-2015 energy distress or late-2018 Fed tightening fears. At that level, the CCC-to-broad-HY ratio would compress to roughly **2.3–2.5×**—elevated but within historical precedent.

**The bottom line: ~60% of the CCC spread level reflects broad credit market stress; ~40% reflects concentrated sector distress.** This decomposition suggests the 1,013 bps reading is overstating systemic risk relative to 2007 but is not a false signal—fundamental weakness exists beyond just software.

## Why the 2.96× ratio does not mean "July 2007 all over again"

The unprecedented CCC-to-broad-HY spread ratio of **~2.96–3.04×** looks alarming compared to the 2007 precedent, but structural changes in the HY market make this comparison misleading in both directions.

The denominator has been artificially compressed. The broad HY index is the highest quality it has ever been: BB-rated bonds now constitute **58% of the index** (versus 38.2% in 2007), while CCC has shrunk to **less than 10%** (versus 18.8% in 2007). Secured bonds represent 44.4% of new issuance versus 7.2% in 2007. Fallen angels—nearly **$55 billion of IG debt downgraded to HY** in 2025, with Barclays forecasting $70–90 billion in 2026—enter as BB-rated credits, further compressing the broad index spread. This structural quality improvement means a broad HY OAS of 342 bps today embeds materially less risk than 342 bps in 2007.

The numerator has been artificially inflated by concentration. In 2007, CCC stress originated in housing and financials—sectors with deep systemic interconnections across the economy. In 2026, CCC stress originates in PE-backed software and legacy media/telecom—sectors with limited real-economy transmission mechanisms (though the private credit channel introduces opaque interconnections). The CCC cohort has become a smaller, more distressed, more concentrated pool: CCC issuance was just **3% of 2025 HY supply**, the lowest share in over two decades.

Three factors, however, warrant genuine caution. First, **first-lien loan recovery rates have collapsed to 37.7%** versus a 25-year average of 62.3%, suggesting credit infrastructure has weakened broadly—not just in software. Second, **40% of CCC-rated borrowers have operating cash flow coverage below 1.0×** per Barclays (December 2025), indicating fundamental weakness across the cohort. Third, the **$3 trillion private credit market** has 20–25% exposure to software/services with limited mark-to-market transparency, creating potential for hidden interconnections analogous to off-balance-sheet vehicles in 2007. Oaktree Capital's Armen Panossian frames the current environment as "a new era of dispersion"—not systemic collapse, but a market where the gap between winners and losers has reached record extremes.

---

## Conclusion: a concentrated storm, not a systemic hurricane—but watch the levees

The analytical verdict is clear but nuanced. **The 1,013 bps CCC OAS is predominantly a concentrated-distress signal**, driven by the collision of 2020–2022 vintage PE-backed software leverage with AI disruption fears, compounded by secular decline in legacy media/telecom. Stripping software alone from the CCC bond index reduces OAS by ~50–75 bps; removing the broader distressed cohort brings it to ~800–850 bps. Multiple institutional voices—State Street, PineBridge, Janus Henderson—explicitly call this "idiosyncratic, not systemic." The default rate remains **1.9%** for US HY, well below cycle-average.

But the signal should not be dismissed entirely. The unprecedented ratio reflects real structural fragility: collapsed recovery rates, widespread cash flow deficiency among CCC borrowers, a frozen primary market for lower-rated credits, and opaque private credit exposure that could amplify losses if AI disruption proves more severe than priced. The proper analogy is not December 2007 (systemic) but rather **mid-2015 to early 2016** (concentrated energy distress that briefly threatened contagion before resolving)—with the critical caveat that private credit opacity in 2026 makes the contagion risk harder to assess than energy sector distress was a decade ago. The 342 bps broad HY spread is not complacent about systemic risk; rather, it correctly reflects an index that is structurally higher-quality than at any point in its history. The real risk to monitor is not whether CCC spreads widen further but whether software/PE-backed distress triggers forced liquidation in private credit vehicles that spills into performing loan and bond markets.
