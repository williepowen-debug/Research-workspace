# The $277 billion shadow funding market behind life insurance

**Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (FABNs) have grown into a $277 billion wholesale funding channel for U.S. life insurers, nearly tripling since 2018 — yet regulators cannot independently verify the market's size.** This explosive growth is driven by PE-controlled insurers seeking cheap capital to fund illiquid private credit investments, with Athene (Apollo) alone accounting for roughly $64 billion in outstanding funding agreements by mid-2025. The Federal Reserve has classified FABS as "runnable money-like financial liabilities" in every Financial Stability Report since 2023, and Federal Reserve economists have published landmark empirical evidence showing these instruments are vulnerable to self-fulfilling runs. The NAIC admitted in July 2025 that it must rely on Federal Reserve data because it lacks its own reporting framework for FABNs — a remarkable admission for the primary regulator of the industry issuing these instruments.

---

## A market that nearly tripled in seven years

Total FABN outstanding has grown from **$88 billion in 2018 to $277 billion by September 2025**, according to Federal Reserve Enhanced Financial Accounts data cited in the NAIC Capital Markets Bureau's February 2026 primer. The growth trajectory accelerated sharply: outstanding balances rose 22% in 2020, 34% in 2021, and 18% in 2024. Annual issuance hit **$58 billion in 2024** (a 70% jump from 2023), and the first half of 2025 alone saw $43.2 billion across 24 issuers.

When combined with Federal Home Loan Bank advances to insurers (**$161 billion** at year-end 2024), total wholesale funding channels for life insurers exceeded **$378 billion** — representing roughly 10% of invested assets, 106% of capital and surplus, and **three times cash holdings** for companies using both channels. Fitch Ratings issued a specific warning in October 2025 titled "U.S. Life Insurers' Increasing Reliance on Funding Agreement-Backed Notes Creates Risk."

A significant data discrepancy exists even among authoritative sources: Bloomberg shows only ~$135 billion in USD FABNs outstanding at year-end 2024, versus the Fed's $217 billion figure. The gap is partly explained by FX-denominated FABNs (~$48 billion per Bloomberg/Fitch) and methodological differences. The euro FABN market more than doubled in 2025, with issuers increasingly tapping multi-currency programs in AUD, CAD, CHF, EUR, GBP, HKD, and NOK.

## PE-controlled insurers punch above their weight

Athene (Apollo) is the **dominant single FABN issuer**, with $11.2 billion in 2024 issuance — roughly 19% of the entire market's volume — and approximately **$64 billion in total funding agreements outstanding** by mid-2025, nearly double the ~$34 billion a year earlier. Athene's $35 billion shelf program (Athene Global Funding) is the largest in the market, and funding agreements represented 38-44% of Athene's new business inflows in recent quarters. Athene is also the largest single FHLB borrower among insurers at **$17.2 billion** in advances as of March 2025.

The PE-backed issuer base is expanding rapidly. **F&G Annuities** (FNF/Blackstone legacy) entered the FABN market with an inaugural $750 million note in June 2021. **Global Atlantic** (KKR) operates GA Global Funding Trust and has been noted for going "unusually long" in tenor. **Fortitude Re** (Carlyle/T&D) launched its inaugural $500 million FABN program in September 2025. **American National** (Brookfield) is expanding its program. **Constellation/AuguStar** (La Caisse/Ontario Teachers') issued its first $350 million FABN in October 2025. PE-controlled insurers likely account for **25–40% of annual FABN issuance**, and this share is growing faster than the traditional segment.

Traditional and mutual insurers still form the market's backbone. **New York Life** was the second-largest issuer in 2024 at ~$10 billion, leveraging the industry's highest ratings (A++/AAA/Aaa/AA+). **Pacific Life** has issued $16.3 billion since launching its program in 2020 across eight currencies. **Protective Life** (Dai-ichi) maintains $9.3 billion in active FABN contracts. **MetLife** operates both a medium-term FABN program and a short-term FABCP program (MetLife Short Term Funding LLC). **Prudential** and **TIAA** maintain active programs. No FABN programs were identified for MassMutual, Jackson National, Principal, or Unum in public filings — these firms rely more heavily on FHLB advances and traditional channels.

## Who actually holds these instruments

The buyer base for FABNs is **primarily institutional asset managers, pension funds, and official institutions** — not money market funds. This represents a critical structural shift from the pre-crisis era. The NAIC describes the most common investors as "money market funds, pension funds, and asset managers," but the current reality skews heavily toward the latter two categories.

**Money market funds are no longer meaningful holders of FABNs.** Standard FABNs carry 3-5 year maturities, far exceeding the 397-day maximum permitted under SEC Rule 2a-7 for money market fund purchases. The short-dated FABS product designed for MMFs — extendible FABNs (XFABNs), which carried initial 397-day maturities with monthly extension options — collapsed during the 2007 crisis and **has never been revived**. According to Federal Reserve data confirmed by the NAIC, there are currently **zero FABNs outstanding with optionality**. Funding Agreement-Backed Commercial Paper (FABCP) exists but is minimal (~$3 billion at its post-crisis peak). In aggregate Crane Data reporting on prime MMF holdings (covering 988 funds and $7.7 trillion in assets), funding agreements do not even appear as a named percentage category, suggesting allocation is well under 1% of total prime MMF assets.

The institutional buyer base increasingly includes **European investors** (euro-denominated issuance more than doubled in 2025), **sovereign wealth funds and central banks** attracted to high-quality, low-volatility characteristics, and **corporate treasuries** seeking yield above covered bonds with comparable credit quality. TD Securities characterizes FABNs as "a surrogate for cash or Treasuries with an additional yield." All FABNs are Rule 144A securities sold exclusively to Qualified Institutional Buyers (managing $100M+ in securities), inherently restricting the investor pool.

Bank and pension fund FABN holdings are **essentially invisible in regulatory filings**. FABNs have no dedicated line item on bank Call Reports (FFIEC 031/041/051) and would be buried under generic categories like "Other domestic debt securities." Pension funds lack any specific FABN reporting requirement; holdings appear within general fixed-income allocations in Form 5500 filings.

## Term-certain bullets, not rollover paper

Modern FABNs are **emphatically not a rollover market**. This is the single most important structural change since the 2007 crisis. Current FABNs are overwhelmingly **bullet-maturity, fixed-term, non-puttable instruments** with typical maturities of **3-5 years** (some ranging up to 12 years). The median maturity at issuance historically was 8 years (1997-2013 data from Federal Reserve). The underlying funding agreement typically carries a maturity of approximately 10 years. The majority are fixed-rate, though floating-rate FABNs are a rising share.

The structural mechanics are straightforward: a life insurer issues a funding agreement (a deposit-type insurance contract) to a bankruptcy-remote SPV (typically a Delaware statutory trust). The SPV's sole asset is that funding agreement. The SPV issues notes to investors in smaller denominations. The funding agreement is a **direct, senior obligation of the insurance company**, ranking pari passu with policyholder claims — senior to general creditors and senior unsecured bondholders. Assets are only notionally segregated in the insurer's general account; there is no separate collateral pool.

At maturity, the insurer pays off the funding agreement, the SPV terminates, and investors receive their principal. There is no structural rollover requirement, no put option, and no investor-driven acceleration. However, **issuers typically seek to maintain or grow their programs**, creating a de facto refinancing need. If market conditions deteriorate and an insurer cannot issue new FABNs at acceptable economics, it must either fund maturing notes from other sources or shrink its balance sheet. The FHLB system historically served as the backstop during the 2007-2008 crisis when FABS markets froze, and roughly three-quarters of the surge in FHLB advances to insurers during the crisis corresponded to the FABS market contraction.

## Ratings cushions range from comfortable to thin

For the short-term FABS products relevant to money market funds (FABCP, theoretically XFABNs), Rule 2a-7 requires securities to present "minimal credit risks" — practically corresponding to the top two short-term rating categories (A-1/P-1/F1 for First Tier; A-2/P-2/F2 for Second Tier). Second Tier securities face severe concentration limits: just **3% of total MMF assets** in aggregate and **0.5% per issuer**, with maturities capped at **45 days**. A downgrade below A-2/P-2 would make the security ineligible entirely, requiring disposal "as soon as practicable."

For the medium-term FABNs that dominate today's market (3-5 year maturities, sold to institutional investors, not MMFs), there is no formal regulatory minimum rating, but **market access practically requires investment-grade ratings** (BBB- or above), with most institutional buyers demanding A-range ratings.

The rating landscape reveals meaningful stratification between PE-controlled and traditional insurers:

The thinnest margins belong to Aspida and Security Benefit at A-, approximately 4 notches above loss of investment-grade status. **Everlake (Blackstone) was downgraded from A+ to A by AM Best in May 2024**, the most notable recent adverse action among PE-controlled insurers.

AM Best consistently flags PE-controlled insurers for **"elevated allocations to less-liquid investments," "increased allocations to Level III assets,"** and investment portfolios with higher risk due to structured securities and non-traditional fixed income. For Everlake, AM Best specifically noted that "significant use of non-affiliate and affiliate retrocession" with offshore Cayman Islands reinsurers "negatively impacts AM Best's view of quality of capital."

## Stress history: the 2007 run that rewired the market

The defining stress episode for FABNs was the **self-fulfilling run on XFABNs beginning in summer 2007**. Federal Reserve economists Foley-Fisher, Narajabad, and Verani documented this in a landmark paper published in the *Journal of Political Economy* (2020), finding that **at least 40% of the $18 billion in withdrawals** from life insurers' XFABN programs was driven by self-fulfilling expectations — investors withdrew because they expected other investors to withdraw, not purely because of fundamental deterioration.

XFABN outstanding collapsed from **~$27 billion at peak to zero by 2011**. The FABS market overall shrank from a peak of **$170-178 billion to roughly $60 billion by 2013** — a loss of nearly two-thirds. FABN issuance froze entirely; even insurers earning 75-100 basis point spreads could not access the market. Hartford Financial ($4 billion program), Genworth ($5 billion program), and others saw their funding agreement programs disrupted. Hartford and Genworth both attempted to acquire depository institutions to access TARP funding. GE Capital, while primarily a commercial paper and term debt issuer, experienced a complete overnight funding freeze and required $12 billion in emergency preferred stock purchases and FDIC Temporary Liquidity Guarantee Program access ($74 billion by Q1 2009).

An earlier and more dramatic precedent was the **1999 seizure of General American Life Insurance**. The $30 billion insurer had **$6.8 billion in funding agreements, of which $5 billion carried 7-day put options** held by money market funds. A single-notch Moody's downgrade triggered mass puts, and **within days the Missouri Department of Insurance seized the company**. MetLife acquired it at a steep discount. Even after MetLife's acquisition restored the insurer's high rating, MMFs still demanded their money back — demonstrating that run dynamics, once triggered, are difficult to arrest.

The **March 2020 COVID crisis had minimal impact** on the FABN market. By then, the puttable XFABN structure had been completely eliminated, outstanding levels were still modest, and all FABNs were term-certain with no investor acceleration rights. Any spread widening was purely mark-to-market. The Fed's March 23, 2020 announcement of corporate bond purchases rapidly restored confidence across credit markets.

## Regulators sound alarms but lack data

The Federal Reserve has been the most vocal and rigorous in flagging FABN-related risks. **Every Financial Stability Report from 2023 through April 2025** warns that "life insurers continued to face funding risk owing to their reliance on nontraditional liabilities in combination with an increasing share of investments in less-liquid assets." FABS are explicitly listed as a component of **"runnable money-like financial liabilities"** in the FSR's Table 4.1, alongside repos, commercial paper, and MMF shares — a category totaling over $23 trillion. The Fed's April 2025 FSR states the combination of growing nontraditional liabilities and declining asset liquidity "could make it challenging for life insurers to meet a sudden rise in withdrawals or other claims."

The NAIC's Macroprudential Working Group and Financial Stability Task Force have made FABNs a focal point since 2022. The NAIC's PE-owned insurer special reports track 137 PE-owned insurers with $704.3 billion in invested assets, noting their elevated allocations to less-liquid investments. The IAIS published application papers on liquidity risk management requiring insurers to stress-test reductions in wholesale funding access. The Geneva Association published a comprehensive liquidity risk report in July 2024 examining funding agreements and FHLB advances. FSOC's November 2023 updated framework for nonbank SIFI designations explicitly identifies insurance companies as a priority.

**A critical gap persists: no paper specifically connects PE-insurer business models to FABN rollover risk.** The Federal Reserve's research body (particularly the Foley-Fisher team) has extensively studied FABS run risk and life insurers' nontraditional liabilities, and AM Best repeatedly flags PE insurers' illiquid investment portfolios. But these research streams have not been formally joined. This gap matters because PE-controlled insurers use FABNs to fund precisely the illiquid private credit investments that are hardest to liquidate under stress — the very asset-liability mismatch regulators worry about.

## The opacity is the story

The most significant finding may be what cannot be found. **The NAIC — the primary regulator of insurance companies issuing FABNs — admitted in July 2025 that "we have to rely on the Fed for this data" and "we do not have a complete data set, which could influence our risk assessment."** This is extraordinary for a $277 billion market backed by policyholder-priority obligations of regulated insurance companies.

Current statutory reporting (SSAP No. 52, Exhibit 7 Deposit-Type Contracts) captures funding agreements only in aggregate, without distinguishing FABN-backing funding agreements from FHLB funding agreements, direct placements, or GICs. **Issuer-level FABN outstanding data is not publicly aggregated.** Fitch, S&P, Deutsche Bank, and J.P. Morgan track this in proprietary, paywalled research. The Fed's data shows $48 billion more than Bloomberg's figures, with neither source fully reconcilable. Foreign-currency FABNs represent a significant blind spot: Fitch reports ~$48 billion in FX FABNs while the Fed shows only $357 million.

New disclosure requirements have been proposed for year-end 2026 annual statements via amendments to SSAP No. 52, which would require insurers to separately report FABN-backing funding agreements. Until these take effect, the market operates with **less regulatory transparency than the commercial paper market, the repo market, or the money market fund industry** — all of which have dedicated Federal Reserve, SEC, or OFR monitoring frameworks. The FABN market's rapid growth to nearly $300 billion, its concentration among a handful of issuers with varying rating cushions, and its use to fund illiquid private credit assets make this opacity itself a systemic vulnerability.

## Conclusion

The FABN market has been fundamentally restructured since the 2007 crisis: puttable structures are gone, maturities are termed out, and money market funds are no longer meaningful holders. These changes materially reduce classic run risk. But a new vulnerability has emerged. **The market has nearly tripled since 2018, PE-controlled insurers are its fastest-growing segment, and these issuers deploy FABN proceeds into illiquid private credit** — creating a liquidity mismatch that is acknowledged by every major financial regulator but inadequately measured by any of them. Athene alone has $64 billion in funding agreements — more than many insurers' total invested assets. The 1999 General American failure demonstrated that a single-notch downgrade can trigger a fatal run on puttable funding agreements within days. While today's non-puttable structures prevent that exact scenario, a sufficiently severe downgrade of a major PE-controlled issuer would still shut it out of new issuance, creating a slow-motion funding squeeze against an illiquid asset base. The fact that regulators cannot even agree on whether the market is $135 billion or $277 billion suggests the surveillance infrastructure has not kept pace with the risk.
