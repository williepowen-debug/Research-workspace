# Primary dealer capacity cannot keep pace with Treasury supply growth

**The $30 trillion US Treasury market's intermediation infrastructure is structurally undersized for the debt it must absorb.** Since 2007, primary dealer balance sheets per dollar of Treasuries outstanding have shrunk by a factor of four. For every $1 increase in Treasury supply, dealers add just **4.5 cents** in gross positions — meaning 95.5 cents of every new dollar must find a home elsewhere. With reserves at Q1 2019 stress levels, the ON RRP buffer fully exhausted, and the basis trade exceeding $1 trillion in leveraged notional, the system's shock-absorption margin is the thinnest since the pre-pandemic repo crisis. This report quantifies every dimension of that structural mismatch with hard numbers from Federal Reserve data, FRBNY primary dealer statistics, and the academic literature.

---

## The numbers: dealer positions versus a market that nearly tripled

Primary dealer aggregate net Treasury positions reached a record **$477 billion** at end-2025, per FRBNY FR 2004 data compiled by Brookings researchers Zhu and Liang. This sounds large until measured against a market that has tripled. Treasury securities held by the public (excluding Fed SOMA holdings) grew from **$8.3 trillion in 2012 to $22.9 trillion by July 2024** — a 176% increase. Over that same period, dealer gross positions grew only **80%** and secured financing grew just **43%**, according to the October 2024 Federal Reserve FEDS Note on dealer capacity.

The regression sensitivity tells the real story. The Fed Board staff found that for every **$1 increase** in publicly-held Treasuries (January 2012–July 2024), primary dealer gross positions increased by only **1.9 cents**. During the QT2 period (June 2022–July 2024), when dealers had to absorb more supply as the Fed stepped back, the sensitivity rose to **4.5 cents per dollar** for positions and **7.3 cents** for financing — still leaving the vast majority of marginal supply to be absorbed by other buyers. Darrell Duffie documented the headline finding in his 2023 Jackson Hole paper and 2025 *Journal of Economic Perspectives* article: since 2007, primary dealer balance sheets per dollar of Treasuries outstanding have **shrunk by a factor of nearly four**.

The record progression in dealer net positions reflects this structural lag: **$361 billion** (August 7, 2024, then a record), **$409 billion** (February 2025), and **$477 billion** (end-2025). Each was a new all-time high, yet each represented a smaller fraction of the outstanding market. Primary dealer share of long-term Treasury auction awards collapsed from **71% in 2000 to just 11% by 2025**, per Somogyi, Wallen, and Xu's Harvard Business School research.

## SLR is the structural constraint; VaR limits are the crisis constraint

The Supplementary Leverage Ratio treats Treasuries identically to high-yield corporate bonds in its denominator — a non-risk-sensitive design that penalizes exactly the low-margin, high-volume intermediation the Treasury market requires. SIFMA's econometric analysis of FR Y-9C data found that every **$1 billion in UST holdings lowers a G-SIB's SLR by 4.87 basis points**. As of Q3 2024, the Bank Policy Institute documented that **3 of the 6 largest BHCs with primary dealers are bound by the enhanced SLR** (the 5% minimum including the 2% GSIB buffer), with effectively zero capacity for additional Treasury holdings. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley operated at SLRs of just **5.45% and 5.46%** — barely above the 5% floor.

The COVID-era SLR exemption (April 2020–March 2021) provides the natural experiment. By excluding Treasuries and reserves from the denominator, the Fed freed approximately **$1.6 trillion** in leverage exposure capacity and boosted median GSIB SLRs by over **1 percentage point**. Boston Fed researchers Bräuning and Stein found strong causal effects: for every 1 percentage point of SLR relief, more-constrained dealers increased Treasury positions by approximately **$3.4 billion in the first week** and expanded turnover by roughly **$146 billion**. They also cut profit margins by **10 percentage points per 1 pp of relief**, indicating willingness to intermediate at tighter spreads when not constrained. The shadow cost of dealer constraints was estimated at **as high as one-third of dealers' total intermediation margin**.

A critical but underappreciated nuance, flagged by BPI: Treasuries held at the bank level for liquidity compliance (LCR/NSFR) consume SLR capacity but are **not available** for broker-dealer intermediation. US Treasuries rose from **3% to 11% of large banks' total assets** between 2013 and 2024 — almost entirely for regulatory liquidity purposes, not market-making. A bank holding company can be awash in Treasury securities while its broker-dealer subsidiary simultaneously starves for balance sheet space.

Under stress conditions, the binding constraint shifts from SLR to **internal VaR limits**. The Boston Fed found that a one-standard-deviation tightening in VaR limits causes a **2.1% fall in net positions** and **1.7% fall in turnover**, while bid-ask spreads widen by **2.4%**. Since June 2022, elevated interest rate volatility has made VaR limits more constraining even without position changes — a static portfolio's VaR rises mechanically with volatility, consuming risk budgets.

The November 2025 eSLR reform (effective April 1, 2026) replaces the flat 2% buffer with 50% of each GSIB's Method 1 surcharge, yielding requirements of **3.5%–4.25%** instead of a uniform 5%. This frees roughly **$210 billion** in depository-institution-level capital but does **not** exempt Treasuries from the denominator. The Congressional Research Service estimates an upper bound of **$2.1 trillion** in additional capacity to hold Treasuries under the reform — meaningful but modest against $52 trillion in projected 2035 debt.

## Demand has become five times more inelastic since 2010

The structural capacity gap is compounded by a dramatic decline in Treasury demand elasticity. Somogyi, Wallen, and Xu (Harvard Business School Working Paper 26-033, December 2025) documented that Treasury demand shifted from an elasticity of **-0.9 (1992–2010) to -0.2 (2011–2025)** — nearly five times more inelastic. The price impact of supply changes jumped accordingly: a **1% increase in Treasury supply** now moves yields by approximately **9 basis points**, versus just **2 basis points** historically.

Before 2010, long-term yields declined on average by **1.5 basis points** after auctions with strong demand. After 2010, yields **no longer fall** after auctions. The erosion was driven primarily by investors, not dealers, becoming more inelastic — but it coincided with the collapse in dealer participation from 71% to 11% of long-term auction awards. Foreign holdings also declined from a peak of **34% in 2015 to 24% in 2025**. Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen's foundational work (2012, *Journal of Political Economy*) established that Treasury yields carry a **73 basis point** average convenience yield from liquidity and safety attributes — a premium that compresses as supply grows and the demand curve steepens.

## Four dislocations reveal the capacity gap in real time

Each Treasury market dislocation since 2014 exposed a progressively wider gap between selling pressure and dealer absorption capacity.

| Episode | Date(s) | Trigger Volume | Price/Yield Impact | Bid-Ask Impact | Duration | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flash Crash | Oct 15, 2014 | $946B cash volume (record) | 37bp 10Y intraday range | Dealer offers vanished | ~12 minutes | Self-correcting; no Fed action |
| Repo Crisis | Sep 16–17, 2019 | ~$100B+ reserve drain (tax + settlement) | SOFR: 2.43% → 5.25% (10% intraday) | 700bp rate dispersion | 2 days acute; months to normalize | Fed $75B/day repo; $60B/mo bill purchases |
| COVID Dash for Cash | Mar 9–24, 2020 | ~$780B+ selling (Q1); $240B new issuance | 10Y: +60bp (0.54% → 1.18%); yields rose alongside equities | 30Y: 6x normal; depth at 10% of average | ~2–3 weeks acute | Fed purchased $1T in 3 weeks; SLR exemption |
| Term Premium Repricing | Aug–Oct 2023 | Sustained supply shock ($776B Q4 borrowing) | 10Y: +164bp (3.35% → 4.99%) | Record 5.3bp 30Y auction tail; dealer takedown 24.7% vs 12.7% avg | ~3–5 months | November QRA stabilized issuance expectations |

The March 2020 episode is the definitive stress test. Dealers initially absorbed approximately **$50 billion** in net holdings and expanded repo financing by **$400 billion** — then hit balance sheet capacity. The Fed offered **$1.5 trillion** in repo funding on March 12, but take-up was minimal, confirming the constraint was balance sheet space, not funding access. Duffie et al. measured dealer-to-customer bid-offer spreads and market depth worsening by **over 10-fold**. Customer-to-dealer trade volumes surged to **10 times** their 2017–2022 medians. The capacity gap ratio — selling pressure versus dealer absorption — was roughly **15:1**. The market was restored only when the Fed purchased **$775 billion in Treasuries** between March 15 and 31 and committed to open-ended purchases on March 23.

The October 2023 episode revealed a different failure mode. The sell-off was orderly but relentless, driven by supply expectations after the August 2 Quarterly Refunding Announcement signaled significant coupon issuance increases. The November 9 30-year auction tailed by **5.3 basis points** — the largest tail on record — with primary dealers absorbing **24.7%** of the award versus a 12.7% six-auction average, indicating deeply weak end-user demand. No Fed intervention was needed; Treasury's own issuance policy (the November 1 QRA holding coupon supply roughly constant) resolved the sell-off and triggered a 100+ basis point rally into year-end.

## Current plumbing: every buffer is depleted

As of March 2026, every major liquidity buffer in the Treasury market ecosystem has been drawn down to or near its structural floor.

**Reserves have lost their cushion.** Bank reserves stand at approximately **$3.02 trillion** (FRED, week of March 18, 2026), roughly **$300 billion above** Governor Waller's July 2025 estimate that **$2.7 trillion** (9% of GDP) represents the lower bound of "ample." The Fed ended QT on December 1, 2025 and initiated Reserve Management Purchases of **$40 billion/month** in T-bills, which has rebuilt reserves by about $83 billion since December. But Roberto Perli, the SOMA Manager, stated on March 26, 2026 that reserve ampleness indicators — including the share of repo transactions above IORB and late-in-day bank payments — have "approached levels seen in Q1 2019," the quarter that preceded the September 2019 repo crisis.

**The ON RRP buffer is fully exhausted.** The overnight reverse repo facility drained from its peak of **~$2.55 trillion** (late 2022) to effectively **$0 billion** by October 2025, as money market funds rationally shifted to T-bills yielding 10+ basis points above the ON RRP rate. This buffer previously absorbed hundreds of billions in liquidity fluctuations; its depletion means all funding shocks now fall directly on bank reserves.

**The Standing Repo Facility is being used as intended — which itself is a signal.** SRF usage hit a record **$74.6 billion** on December 31, 2025 ($31.5B in Treasuries, $43.1B in MBS), reflecting acute dealer balance sheet constraints at the regulatory reporting date. At the December 2025 FOMC, the Fed removed the $500 billion daily aggregate cap, moving to full allotment. The facility's usage pattern — massive quarter-end spikes followed by immediate collapse to zero — confirms that dealers systematically pull back from repo intermediation to manage leverage ratios at reporting dates, creating predictable but potentially dangerous funding gaps.

**Money market funds now dominate the plumbing.** MMF assets reached a record **$7.86 trillion** (ICI, March 18, 2026; ~$8.27 trillion by Crane Data's broader measure), having grown **$883 billion (+12.9%)** in 2025 alone. MMFs are the largest providers of cash to the approximately **$12 trillion** US repo market and have become the dominant counterparty for dealer financing — a concentration that creates single-point-of-failure risk during stress episodes.

**The basis trade adds $1+ trillion in latent fragility.** Hedge fund Treasury basis trade positions reached approximately **$1.0–1.4 trillion** in gross notional by early 2026, leveraged at roughly **20x** and concentrated in 6–8 major funds. This is more than double the estimated $500 billion level that unwound violently in March 2020, generating margin calls and forced selling that overwhelmed dealer capacity. Kashyap, Stein, Wallen, and Younger (Brookings Papers, Spring 2025) found that the March 2020 basis/repo spread spiked by **9 standard deviations** and that fragility increases mechanically as Treasury supply grows while dealer capacity remains fixed.

## The structural arithmetic through 2035

The CBO projects Treasury debt held by the public growing from **$30.1 trillion (2025) to $52.1 trillion (2035)** — an 85% increase requiring 5.7% annual growth. GSIB total assets grew only **3.8% annually** from 2013–2023, or roughly two-thirds the rate needed to keep pace. Without structural reform, the mismatch will widen further.

The Duffie et al. capacity model (NY Fed Staff Report 1070/BIS WP 1138) established that Treasury market illiquidity becomes **highly nonlinear** when dealer balance sheet utilization exceeds 40–80% of historical maxima. Under normal conditions, yield volatility explains 89% of illiquidity variation. At capacity limits, the relationship breaks down entirely — March 2020 illiquidity was far worse than volatility alone would predict.

The eSLR reform effective April 2026 is a first step but insufficient. It frees an estimated **$210 billion** in depository-level capital and up to **$2.1 trillion** in theoretical Treasury capacity — against a market growing by $2+ trillion per year. The reform does not exempt Treasuries from the SLR denominator, which was the policy change that produced the strongest empirical effects during COVID. More fundamental reforms — all-to-all trading, mandatory central clearing (SEC rule effective December 31, 2026 for cash; June 30, 2027 for repo), and a dedicated market-function purchase facility separate from QE — remain in various stages of implementation.

## Conclusion

Three hard numbers frame the structural problem. First, **4.5 cents**: for every dollar of new Treasury supply during QT, dealers absorbed less than a nickel in positions. Second, **9 basis points**: a 1% supply increase now moves yields 4.5 times more than it did pre-2010, because both dealers and investors have become dramatically less elastic. Third, **15:1**: the ratio of selling pressure to dealer absorption capacity in March 2020, the most recent severe stress test. The system's current buffer — roughly $300 billion in reserves above Waller's floor, zero ON RRP, and a basis trade twice the size of 2020 — leaves little margin for the next shock in a market that must absorb $2+ trillion in new issuance annually. The Fed's role as backstop buyer of last resort is not a design feature of the Treasury market; it is a structural dependency created by the growing gap between supply growth and intermediation capacity.
