# The 2007 credit selling cascade and its 2026 private credit mirror

**The Z.1 Flow of Funds data reveals a precise five-stage selling sequence during 2007–2008 — broker-dealers and money market funds first (Q3 2007), insurance next (Q4 2007), banks later (Q2 2008), mutual funds last (Q4 2008), and pensions never — and the current private credit market appears to be transitioning from Stage 1 (fund-level blowups) into Stage 2 (retail gating/redemption crisis), with Stage 3 signals (insurance/regulatory stress) now emerging.** Seven major private credit funds have gated in Q1 2026, trapping $4.6 billion in investor capital. Goldman Sachs projects $45–70 billion in retail outflows over 2026–2027. Treasury convened FSOC on March 25 and is meeting insurance regulators as of April 1. The structural mapping from 2007 to 2026 holds with critical additions: CLOs (the CDO analog), PE-controlled insurers (far more dangerous than 2007 monolines), and a niche ratings vulnerability (Egan-Jones) with no 2007 parallel. No single "Meredith Whitney" has emerged — instead, a distributed warning chorus spanning Jamie Dimon's "cockroach" call (October 2025), Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey's explicit 2008 comparison, and UBS Chairman Colm Kelleher's "looming systemic risk" warning marks the consensus-breaking moment.

## Five stages: the verified selling sequence

The Z.1 data confirms a modified version of the hypothesized cascade, with important corrections. The actual sequence was tighter at the top and more spread out at the bottom than commonly assumed.

**Stage 1 — Shadow banking / broker-dealers (Q3 2007, lag = 0).** Broker-dealers flipped from massive net buyers (+$180B SAAR in Q2) to net sellers (−$5.4B) in a single quarter — the most violent reversal in the dataset. This coincides precisely with the **August 9, 2007 BNP Paribas fund freeze** and the ABCP market collapse, which wiped **$190 billion** in ABCP outstanding in August alone. The 36 SIVs managing $400 billion in assets began a cliff-like collapse; by year-end, SIV outstandings had fallen roughly 80%. Bear Stearns' two structured credit hedge funds had already been liquidated in July. The selling pattern was **unambiguously cliff-like**: broker-dealer net acquisitions swung $185 billion (SAAR) in a single quarter.

**Stage 2 — Money market funds (Q3 2007, lag = 0).** Money market funds flipped simultaneously with broker-dealers, going from +$132B to −$24.6B SAAR. This reflects the ABCP market freeze — money market funds were major holders of SIV-issued commercial paper and stopped rolling it over. Their selling intensified catastrophically in Q4 2007 (−$190.8B) and again in Q3 2008 (−$422.3B, following the Reserve Primary Fund breaking the buck). The pattern was **cliff-like in discrete episodes** tied to specific panic events.

**Stage 3 — Insurance companies (Q4 2007, lag = 1 quarter).** Both life insurance (−$22.1B SAAR) and P&C insurance (−$9.9B) flipped to net sellers in Q4 2007 — one quarter after the shadow banking collapse, not two as commonly assumed. Research by Ellul, Jotikasthira, and Lundblad (2011, *Journal of Financial Economics*) documents the mechanism: **regulatory-induced forced selling triggered by credit rating downgrades**. When bonds fell below investment grade, NAIC risk-based capital requirements made holding them prohibitively expensive. Life insurance briefly returned to net buying in Q1–Q2 2008 before resuming heavy selling in Q3–Q4 2008 (−$45.7B each quarter). The pattern was **punctuated** — discrete selling waves tied to downgrade episodes, not continuous liquidation.

**Stage 4 — Commercial banks (Q2 2008, lag = 3 quarters).** Banks were actually the *biggest net buyers* in Q3 2007 (+$296.3B SAAR), absorbing assets that broker-dealers and money market funds were dumping. This reflects Ivashina and Scharfstein's (2010) finding that corporate borrowers drew down pre-existing credit lines, forcing banks to hold more credit. Banks finally flipped in Q2 2008 (−$112.1B), after Bear Stearns collapsed in March. Their selling was concentrated in Q2 2008, with an anomalous return to net buying in Q4 2008 (+$118.5B), likely reflecting government intervention (TARP, Fed facilities). The pattern was **delayed then cliff-like** — banks absorbed stress for three quarters before breaking.

**Stage 5 — Mutual funds (Q4 2008, lag = 5 quarters) and pensions (never).** Mutual funds exhibited the most gradual pattern: a steady decline from +$172.4B (Q1 2007) to +$101.2B (Q4 2007) to +$26.4B (Q3 2008), finally flipping to −$11.5B only in Q4 2008 — after Lehman. Manconi, Massa, and Yasuda (2012, *JFE*) showed mutual funds sold *liquid* corporate bonds to meet redemptions while retaining illiquid securitized bonds — **selling the liquid asset first**. Private pension funds **never became net sellers at all**, maintaining steady purchases of $37–55B SAAR throughout the crisis. ETFs also never flipped, growing from $3.6B to $18.3B in net acquisitions.

The **correction to the assumed sequence** is significant: insurance companies sold one quarter earlier than hypothesized (Q4 2007, not Q3–Q4), banks sold later (Q2 2008, not Q4 2007), and mutual funds held on far longer than expected (Q4 2008, not Q3 2007). The overall cascade from first seller to last spanned **five quarters** (Q3 2007 to Q4 2008).

## Gradual versus cliff-like: how each sector broke

The magnitude and speed of each sector's selling reveals two distinct regimes. **Leveraged, short-funded entities** (broker-dealers, money market funds, SIVs) exhibited cliff-like collapses driven by funding withdrawals and margin calls. Broker-dealer selling peaked at −$390.2B SAAR in Q2 2008 — a quarterly liquidation rate exceeding **$97 billion in actual flow**. Money market funds peaked at −$422.3B SAAR in Q3 2008. These sectors had no choice: when counterparties refused to roll funding, liquidation was immediate.

**Insurance companies showed a punctuated pattern** — relatively stable positions interrupted by discrete selling waves triggered by rating agency downgrades. Between autumn 2007 and mid-2008, agencies downgraded nearly **$2 trillion** in MBS tranches; by end-2008, 80% of CDOs originally rated triple-A were junk. Each downgrade wave produced a new forced-selling episode for capital-constrained insurers.

**Banks and mutual funds exhibited delayed, initially gradual patterns** that eventually accelerated. Banks actually *increased* buying through Q3 2007 before the cliff in Q2 2008. Mutual fund selling was the most gradual of any sector — a steady 5-quarter decline reflecting ongoing but manageable redemption pressure, until the Lehman shock pushed them negative.

**Pension funds were the anomaly**: private pensions slightly *increased* their buying rate during the crisis (from $21.9B to $54.9B SAAR), acting as a stabilizing force. State and local pensions showed erratic, small-magnitude flows around zero, never becoming significant net sellers.

## The 2026 actor map: what's right, what's missing, what's new

The proposed 2007→2026 mapping captures the core architecture correctly but requires four critical additions and one significant quantitative correction.

**Mapping 1 (Hedge funds/SIVs → BDCs/interval funds): Largely accurate but needs refinement.** The key distinction is between closed-end vintage funds (~80% of private credit AUM, with true PE-style lockups and minimal run risk) and semi-liquid vehicles (~20%, including **$475 billion in BDCs** and **$534 billion in semi-liquid vehicles** total). The semi-liquid segment is where the SIV analogy applies: these structures promise periodic liquidity against illiquid underlying assets, creating a classic maturity/liquidity mismatch. The gating mechanism replaces the overnight repo run with a slow-motion queue — less explosive but equally destructive to investor confidence. Non-traded perpetual BDCs grew from essentially **zero in 2021 to over $200 billion** today, representing the fastest-growing and most vulnerable segment.

**Mapping 2 (Mutual funds → Retail ETFs + BDC shareholders): Partially accurate but underestimates scope.** Retail/high-net-worth investors now provide **24% of all private credit capital**, up from negligible a decade ago. The channels are broader than HYG/JNK: non-traded perpetual BDCs ($200B+), interval funds ($33B+ for Cliffwater alone), CLO ETFs (nascent), and potentially 401(k) plans following a 2025 executive order. HYG and JNK are better understood as **price signal transmitters** (analogous to ABX in 2007) rather than direct private credit vehicles. BDC stock price discounts to NAV serve as the real-time stress barometer — equivalent to the ABX index revealing subprime deterioration.

**Mapping 3 (Insurance companies → PE-controlled insurers): The strongest and most dangerous mapping.** PE firms now direct approximately **$700 billion of U.S. life insurance assets**. Roughly **one-third of U.S. life insurers' $6 trillion** in invested assets is tied to private credit in various forms. The "Bermuda Triangle" strategy — alternative asset manager orchestrating capital through a captive annuity-issuing insurer and offshore Bermuda reinsurer — creates concentrated risk with no 2007 precedent. Key platforms include Apollo/Athene (approaching **$1 trillion total AUM**), KKR/Global Atlantic, Blackstone Insurance ($432B credit/insurance segment), Ares/Aspida, and Carlyle/Fortitude Re. Athene operates on a **2.4% surplus-to-liabilities ratio** versus the **7.2% industry average**. Level 3 assets constitute roughly one-third of Athene's and Global Atlantic's total assets. AM Best found approximately one-fifth of investments at Athene's U.S. Life Group and KKR's Global Atlantic come from **loans to affiliated funds** — circular, self-referential capital structures that amplify concentration risk.

**Mapping 4 (Banks → Banks with PC commitments): Accurate but the $95B figure is significantly understated.** The FDIC's 2025 Risk Review shows banks extended approximately **$200 billion** in credit to private credit funds. A May 2025 Fed FEDS Note found bank loan commitments to private debt funds and BDCs represent roughly **7% of banks' regulatory capital** on average, with **60% concentrated in five U.S. GSIBs**. Moody's data via the Fed shows U.S. banks have approximately $300 billion in loans outstanding to private credit providers, $285 billion to PE funds, and $340 billion in unused commitments — a total potential exposure nearing **$925 billion**. JPMorgan alone reported $22.2 billion in direct private credit exposure and proactively re-marked software loan collateral on March 10–11, 2026.

**Four critical additions to the framework:**

- **CLOs are the CDO analog and must be included.** The total CLO market stands at **$1.3–1.6 trillion**, with private credit/middle-market CLOs reaching record $41.8 billion issuance in 2024 (19.6% of total CLO volume, projected to reach 30%+). Middle-market CLO OC tests are beginning to trip. No AAA CLO tranche has ever defaulted, but the structural parallel to pre-crisis CDOs — tranching illiquid credit into rated securities held by insurance companies for favorable capital treatment — is exact.

- **Pension funds are now direct private credit allocators.** CalPERS holds **$16.4 billion** in private debt with a target allocation of 8% of its $605 billion fund. Average pension allocation to private credit is approximately **6%** and rising. Virginia Retirement System holds $16.1 billion. Canadian pensions (CPP Investments: C$62 billion in total credit) are even more aggressive. Unlike 2007, when pensions' losses were equity-concentrated, a private credit deterioration would directly impair pension portfolios.

- **Niche credit rating agencies are a unique 2026 vulnerability.** Egan-Jones rated over 3,000 private credit instruments with approximately 20 analysts (roughly 150 deals per analyst per year). The Bermuda Monetary Authority revoked Egan-Jones recognition in January 2026. NAIC found private letter ratings averaged **3 notches higher** than their internal SVO assessments. The SEC and DOJ are investigating. If Egan-Jones ratings are systematically revoked, a mass downgrade could trigger capital calls across **$350 billion+** in insurer-held private credit assets — a cascade mechanism with no clean 2007 parallel (the closest being monoline collapse, but concentrated in a single niche rater rather than the Big Three).

- **Sovereign wealth funds are emerging but not yet systemically significant.** ADIA allocates up to 7% to credit (~$50 billion maximum). GIC ($930 billion) has active partnerships. Mubadala committed $1 billion to Blue Owl in 2024. Their exposure is growing but remains a secondary factor.

## Seven funds gated and counting: the 2026 signals

The Congressional Research Service published a formal Insight report (IN12674, March 27, 2026) documenting the private credit stress. At least **seven major funds have gated or restricted redemptions** in Q1 2026, with more than **$4.6 billion of investor capital trapped** behind withdrawal limits.

The gating wave includes Blackstone Private Credit Fund ($82 billion, raised redemption cap to 7.9% after $3.7 billion in outflows; Blackstone injected $400 million of its own capital), Cliffwater Corporate Lending Fund ($33 billion, received **14% redemption requests** against its 7% cap — half of requestors are now queued), Apollo Debt Solutions ($15.1 billion, 11.2% requested vs. 5% cap), Ares Strategic Income Fund ($10.7 billion, 11.6% vs. 5% cap), Morgan Stanley North Haven ($7.6 billion, 10.9% vs. 5% cap), BlackRock HPS Corporate Lending Fund ($26 billion, 9.3% vs. 5% cap), and Blue Owl Capital Corp II (permanently halted quarterly redemptions). Blue Owl sold $1.4 billion in direct-lending assets across 128 companies to meet redemptions and repay a Goldman Sachs credit facility. Q4 2025 redemptions for non-traded BDCs nearly **tripled** from the prior quarter to 4.71% of beginning-quarter NAV.

Goldman Sachs projects retail private credit funds could lose **$45–70 billion in assets over 2026–2027**. Goldman analyst Alex Blostein and GSAM co-head Vivek Bantwal detailed this on the Goldman Sachs *Exchanges* podcast (March 19–20, 2026): if redemption requests continue at roughly 5% of NAV, non-traded BDCs would report approximately $45 billion in net outflows per year. Gross sales are currently running **50% below 2025 levels** while redemptions accelerate. Goldman expects funds to remain in net outflows throughout 2026 and likely 2027. CEO David Solomon wrote in his annual shareholder letter that concerns around private credit were a reminder "the credit cycle has not been repealed."

Barclays is **scaling back asset-based lending to smaller borrowers** following losses from Market Financial Solutions (~£495 million exposure) and Tricolor Holdings (Chapter 7 filing, September 2025, fraud allegations). CEO Venkatakrishnan announced the shift toward larger corporates and increased pricing on March 25, 2026. JPMorgan proactively re-marked software loan collateral on March 10–11, going through its financing portfolio "name by name and then sector by sector." Deutsche Bank flagged **€26 billion** in private credit exposure.

On March 25, 2026, **FSOC convened** with Treasury staff briefing the Council on "recent developments in the private credit sector." The Council formed a new market resilience working group and voted unanimously on proposed interpretive guidance for nonbank financial company designations. Treasury Secretary Bessent is convening meetings with domestic and international insurance regulators beginning **April 1, 2026**, focusing on fund-level leverage, offshore reinsurance, and investment liquidity.

BDC NAV markdowns are accelerating. BlackRock TCP Capital's NAV fell **19% in Q4 2025** alone, with total decline of 40% from its December 2021 peak. FS KKR Capital Corp ($14 billion) was **downgraded to junk by Moody's** on March 23, 2026 — non-accrual loans reached 5.5% of total investments, and PIK income hit 14.7% of total investment income (versus a 6.3% peer median). Alternative asset manager stocks have fallen **25%+ year-to-date**, with combined market cap declines exceeding $100 billion.

Fitch reports the U.S. private credit default rate rose to **5.8%** (trailing twelve months through January 2026) — the highest since tracking began. The privately monitored rating default rate reached **9.4%**, nearly double the broadly syndicated loan market. An estimated **94% of private credit defaults are distressed exchanges** rather than formal bankruptcies, meaning "extend and pretend" is masking the true depth of deterioration. Approximately 12% of borrowers have negative cash flow and 13% have interest coverage below 1x.

## Mapping the 2007 sequence onto 2026: we are in early Stage 2

Applying the verified 2007 selling sequence to the current evidence produces a clear staging assessment.

**Stage 1 (Shadow banking blowups) — COMPLETED, late 2025 to early 2026.** Tricolor Holdings (Chapter 7, September 2025), First Brands Group (distress, September 2025), and the BlackRock TCP Capital 19% NAV writedown (January 2026) are the Bear Stearns hedge fund equivalents — isolated vehicles with concentrated exposure signaling broader problems. Jamie Dimon's October 2025 "cockroach" warning was the moment the first institutional voice broke consensus. Blue Owl's permanent redemption halt (February 2026) marks the end of Stage 1 and transition to Stage 2.

**Stage 2 (Retail/semi-liquid fund gating) — CURRENTLY UNDERWAY, March 2026.** The seven-fund gating wave is the defining feature of the current moment. This maps precisely to the Q3–Q4 2007 period when money market funds and mutual funds began selling (the Z.1 data shows money market funds flipping to −$24.6B SAAR in Q3 2007, deepening to −$190.8B in Q4). The Goldman Sachs $45–70 billion outflow projection confirms this is a multi-quarter event, not a single-quarter shock. The CRS notes that queue dynamics create **run-like incentives** — earlier redeemers get cashed out at NAV, incentivizing others to join before marks decline.

**Stage 3 (Insurance/regulatory stress) — EARLY SIGNALS EMERGING.** Treasury convening insurance regulator meetings (beginning April 1, 2026) mirrors the regulatory concern about monoline insurers that surfaced in early 2008. The Egan-Jones investigation and BMA revocation (January 2026) could trigger forced selling analogous to the 2007–2008 downgrade-driven insurance company liquidations — but potentially more concentrated and violent, given the estimated $350 billion in assets resting on questionable niche ratings. In the Z.1 data, insurance companies flipped to net sellers in Q4 2007, one quarter after the shadow banking collapse. If the timing pattern holds, insurance stress would materialize in **Q2–Q3 2026**.

**Stage 4 (Bank pullback) — EARLY WARNING SIGNS.** JPMorgan's proactive re-marking (March 11), Barclays' ABL pullback (March 25), and Deutsche Bank's exposure flagging are the first signs of bank credit tightening. In 2007, commercial banks didn't flip to net sellers until Q2 2008 — three quarters after broker-dealers. If the timing holds, significant bank-level credit contraction would occur in **late 2026 to early 2027**.

**Stage 5 (Pensions) — NOT YET VISIBLE.** Pension funds with growing private credit allocations (CalPERS at 8% target, industry average at 6%) represent the ultimate loss-bearers if the cycle completes. In 2007, private pensions never became forced corporate bond sellers. The difference in 2026 is that pensions have direct private credit allocations that may become impaired — but the locked-up fund structures mean this would manifest as mark-to-market losses and reduced allocations, not forced selling.

**Critical differences that affect speed.** The 2007 cascade was accelerated by overnight repo funding; the 2026 equivalent uses quarterly redemption gates, producing a **slower-burning but equally inexorable** dynamic. However, three factors could accelerate the timeline: a formal Egan-Jones ratings revocation (forcing instantaneous insurance capital calls), a major PE-controlled insurer experiencing policyholder withdrawals, or the Iran conflict/oil shock preventing Fed rate cuts (paralleling the 2008 oil price spike that complicated monetary policy response).

## No single Whitney, but a three-week consensus break

The search for a "Meredith Whitney of 2026" reveals a fundamentally different information structure. Whitney's October 31, 2007 Citigroup downgrade was a single, specific, actionable research note from a sell-side analyst that broke Wall Street consensus on bank losses. The private credit cycle has instead produced a **distributed warning chorus** that intensified over 2023–2026.

The **earliest and most comprehensive analyst warning** came from **Chris Wood**, Jefferies' global head of equity strategy, who published "Private debt — A ticking time bomb" on **August 30, 2023** — more than two years before the stress materialized. Wood identified the exact mechanism: regulatory arbitrage from over-regulation of banks post-2008, PIK loans masking stress, growing insurer exposure, and ratings shopping. He correctly predicted that high-yield credit spreads would *not* serve as warning indicators because risk was hidden in opaque private structures. Wood has the pedigree of having correctly identified every major financial bubble of the past three decades (Japanese meltdown, Asian crisis, Nasdaq bubble, US subprime).

The **single most market-moving statement** was **Jamie Dimon's** October 14, 2025 earnings call warning: "When you see one cockroach, there are probably more. Everyone should be forewarned on this one." Every major financial outlet ran the quote, and it triggered immediate selloffs in regional bank stocks and BDCs. This was the closest to a "Whitney moment" in terms of immediate impact — a sitting CEO of the largest U.S. bank publicly breaking with the industry's reassuring narrative.

The **consensus-breaking sequence** occurred over three weeks in October–November 2025: Dimon's cockroach warning (October 14) → Bank of England Governor **Andrew Bailey's** explicit 2008 comparison before the House of Lords ("Alarm bells start going off… before 2008, people were telling us 'it's too small to be systemic, it's idiosyncratic' — that was the wrong call," October 21) → UBS Chairman **Colm Kelleher's** declaration that PE-insurer ratings arbitrage was creating "a looming systemic risk to global finance" (November 4), which prompted a public clash with Apollo CEO Marc Rowan.

Institutional warnings preceded this chorus. The **IMF** dedicated Chapter 2 of its April 2024 Global Financial Stability Report to "The Rise and Risks of Private Credit," finding more than one-third of borrowers had interest costs exceeding current earnings. The **BIS** published research in October 2025 warning that ratings on insurer-held private credit "might have been inflated." The **FSOC 2024 Annual Report** flagged opacity, leverage, and interconnectedness. The **Federal Reserve Bank of Boston** published a May 2025 paper asking "Could the Growth of Private Credit Pose a Risk to Financial System Stability?" And **Morgan Stanley strategist Joyce Jiang** published the first major Wall Street research note quantifying default expectations (8%, approaching COVID peak) on March 16, 2026.

On the short-seller side, **Hunterbrook Media/Capital** published a report on Hercules Capital BDC (February 27, 2026) alleging its deal sourcing consisted of "simply copying investments from the Google Ventures website" and that it understated exposure to distressed software debt. The stock fell 8% and a securities class action was filed on March 20.

Notably, **Howard Marks** — widely expected to play the contrarian role given his crisis-calling pedigree — has explicitly stated the opposite: "I don't think today's issues are systemic" and "there's nothing wrong with the plumbing" (CNBC, March 2026). The Fed's June 2025 stress tests also concluded private credit does not pose systemic risk to banking. **The debate remains genuinely unresolved** — credible voices are on both sides, which itself is a feature of the mid-crisis period (the same was true in Q3–Q4 2007, when many argued subprime was "contained").

## Conclusion: the clock, the map, and the gaps

Three findings stand out from this analysis. First, **the Z.1 data proves the 2007 selling cascade was faster and more mechanical than commonly understood** — broker-dealers and money market funds flipped simultaneously in Q3 2007, not sequentially, driven by the same ABCP market freeze. The gap from first seller to insurance was just one quarter; from insurance to banks, three quarters; from banks to mutual funds, two quarters. The entire cascade unfolded over five quarters.

Second, **the 2026 private credit analog is structurally slower but potentially deeper**. Quarterly redemption gates replace overnight repo runs, stretching the cascade timeline. But the PE-controlled insurance nexus — with $700 billion in directed assets, thin surpluses, circular affiliated-fund lending, and a potential ratings infrastructure collapse via Egan-Jones — creates a concentrated vulnerability that exceeds anything monolines posed in 2007. The total private credit ecosystem, including CLOs, bank committed facilities, and insurance allocations, now represents roughly **$3–4 trillion** in interconnected exposure.

Third, **the current moment (March 30, 2026) maps to approximately Q3–Q4 2007 in the original crisis timeline** — the period after the initial shadow banking collapse but before insurance and bank stress fully materialized. If the five-quarter cascade timing holds, the most dangerous phase (Stage 3 insurance stress and Stage 4 bank pullback) lies in **Q2 2026 through Q1 2027**. The Treasury's decision to convene insurance regulators this week suggests policymakers see the same timing.
