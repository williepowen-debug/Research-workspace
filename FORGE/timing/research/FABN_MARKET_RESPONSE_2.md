# **Comprehensive Analysis of the Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (FABN) Market: Systemic Vulnerability, Issuer Profiles, and Contagion Mechanics**

## **1\. Introduction: The Convergence of Private Equity and Life Insurance**

The intersection of private equity (PE) and the United States life insurance industry represents one of the most profound structural shifts in modern capital markets. Over the past decade, alternative asset managers—most notably Apollo Global Management, KKR, Blackstone, and Ares Management—have aggressively acquired or partnered with life insurance and annuity carriers. These private equity sponsors now control or exert significant influence over approximately $700 billion in U.S. life insurance assets.1 This consolidation is driven by a distinct strategic mandate: utilizing the permanent, long-dated capital of life insurance general accounts to absorb the bespoke, illiquid private credit assets originated by the sponsors' asset management platforms.

To execute this strategy, these PE-controlled insurers require vast, scalable, and cost-effective wholesale funding. This demand has catalyzed the explosive growth of the Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (FABN) market. FABNs are securitized instruments backed by deposit-type contracts issued by life insurers, sold primarily to institutional investors to generate spread-based earnings.2 Working in tandem with advances from the Federal Home Loan Bank (FHLB) system—which now total over $161 billion for the industry—FABNs represent a massive pool of non-traditional, wholesale insurance liabilities.3

The core inquiry surrounding the FABN market is whether its rapid expansion and the evolving nature of its issuers constitute a latent systemic vulnerability. Does this market harbor the potential for a commercial-paper-style funding crisis akin to the events of 2008, or does it represent a structurally sound, duration-matched extension of traditional asset-liability management (ALM)? The analysis indicates that the systemic risk profile of the FABN ecosystem is highly bifurcated. It is dictated entirely by two interlinked variables: the maturity profile of the issued notes and the composition of the holder base.

Short-dated instruments, particularly those with embedded optionality held by Money Market Funds (MMFs), present acute rollover and run-risk vulnerabilities. In this scenario, a credit rating downgrade acts as an immediate tripwire, severing access to short-term institutional capital and forcing emergency asset liquidation. Conversely, medium-term notes held by pension funds and asset managers represent a "slow bleed" vulnerability, heavily dependent on the ultimate credit performance of the underlying illiquid private credit assets.

This report provides an exhaustive examination of the FABN market, detailing its aggregate scale, the opacity of its reporting, the demographic breakdown of its investor base, maturity and rating sensitivities, and its historical performance during the acute liquidity shocks of 2008 and March 2020\.

## **2\. Market Sizing, Trajectory, and the Opacity Challenge**

Quantifying the exact size and composition of the FABN market is inherently complicated by a severe lack of standardized statutory reporting. This opacity is not merely an analytical hurdle; it is a recognized macroprudential vulnerability.

### **2.1 The Regulatory Blind Spot and Data Divergence**

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) Macroprudential Working Group has explicitly stated that the Financial Stability Task Force (FSTF) is currently "unable to quantify and monitor the activity in the FABN market to identify transmission channels of potential risk" due to reporting deficits.4 State insurance regulators currently do not receive detailed statutory reporting that identifies aggregate FABN issuance, the types of notes issued (e.g., puttable versus fixed-term), or the duration matching between the funding agreement and the underlying investment.4

Consequently, market participants and regulators must rely on fragmented data from the Federal Reserve's Enhanced Financial Accounts (EFA) project and commercial data aggregators like Bloomberg. These sources frequently present conflicting figures. For instance, at year-end 2024, the Federal Reserve recorded $217 billion in outstanding FABNs, whereas Bloomberg reported only $135 billion.4

This divergence is even more pronounced regarding Foreign Exchange (FX) FABNs—notes issued in foreign currencies and hedged back to U.S. dollars via cross-currency swaps. A recent Fitch Ratings report, utilizing Bloomberg data, identified approximately $48 billion in outstanding FX FABNs. In stark contrast, the Federal Reserve data recorded a mere $357 million in this sub-category.4 Because insurers must hedge FX Funding Agreements with currency swaps, this activity introduces significant derivative counterparty credit risk that is presently escaping comprehensive regulatory quantification.4 Furthermore, because funding agreements are treated as deposit-type contracts and insurance liabilities rather than traditional corporate debt in statutory accounting, life insurers can exclude them from standard leverage metrics, masking the true extent of off-balance-sheet leverage employed to fund illiquid assets.3

### **2.2 Aggregate Volume and Historical Growth**

Despite these data discrepancies, the growth trajectory of the FABN market is unambiguously steep. Federal Reserve data illustrates a dramatic expansion over the past eight years, driven by a prolonged search for yield and the aggressive entry of PE-backed market participants scaling their spread-lending businesses. ## **3\. Structural Mechanics of the FABN Channel**

To properly evaluate the contagion risk embedded in this $239 billion market, it is necessary to deconstruct the operational and legal mechanics of the FABN channel. Funding agreements are general account investment contracts that guarantee a return of principal and predictable fixed or floating interest payments over a specified term.5 Unlike traditional life insurance policies, they contain absolutely no mortality or morbidity contingencies, rendering them pure financial spread-lending instruments.7

In a standard FABN securitization, the life insurance company does not issue notes directly to the public. Instead, the insurer sells a single, large-denomination funding agreement to a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)—typically a statutory trust organized in Delaware (e.g., Athene Global Funding or GA Global Funding Trust). This SPV acts as a bankruptcy-remote financing conduit.2 The funding agreement becomes the SPV's sole asset. The SPV then issues smaller-denomination FABNs to institutional investors.7 The payments of principal and interest on the notes are serviced entirely by the cash flows generated from the underlying funding agreement.8

The strategic appeal to institutional investors lies in the instrument's pari passu status. In the event of an insurer insolvency or liquidation, the funding agreement—and by extension the claims of the FABN holders—ranks equally with the claims of traditional retail life insurance and annuity policyholders.2 This super-senior claim on the insurer's general account is what allows insurers to secure 'A' or 'AA' equivalent ratings on the notes, permitting them to borrow at a substantially lower cost of capital than they could via traditional unsecured holding company debt.2 The insurer takes the cash proceeds from the FABN issuance, co-mingles them in the general account, and invests them in higher-yielding assets, capturing the net investment spread.6

## **4\. The Issuer Landscape: PE-Controlled vs. Traditional Insurers**

The composition of FABN issuers has undergone a paradigm shift over the last decade. Historically, the market was dominated by traditional, legacy life insurers such as MetLife and Prudential Financial. Today, the market's growth and velocity are increasingly driven by PE-controlled or PE-affiliated insurers. Firms like Apollo, KKR, Blackstone, and Ares have engineered highly efficient capital models that utilize the life insurance balance sheet to accumulate assets, which are then directed into bespoke, proprietary private credit investments originated by the parent entity.1

### **4.1 Asset Allocation Shifts by PE Insurers**

The systemic implications of FABNs are intrinsically tied to the assets they fund. Private equity-owned insurers allocate significantly heavier weightings to privately placed asset-backed securities (ABS), middle-market loans, and complex corporate receivables than their traditional counterparts. Between 2017 and 2024, the private placement share of general account assets for PE-owned insurers expanded from 6% to 14%—an 8 percentage point increase, which is double the growth rate observed in non-PE-owned traditional insurers.1

A substantial portion of this growth is tied directly to the affiliates of these insurers. Of the $82 billion aggregate increase in privately placed ABS holdings across the industry from 2017 to 2024, approximately $50 billion was issued by financing vehicles affiliated with PE-owned life insurers.1 Apollo's insurance arm, Athene, accounted for roughly $21 billion of this growth in privately placed ABS holdings, while KKR's Global Atlantic contributed $18 billion, and Blackstone's Everlake Life Insurance Company added around $10 billion.1

These private market investments are highly attractive for spread-lending purposes. Analysis indicates that these private placements earn yields up to 80 basis points higher than comparable public corporate bonds, and for ABS private placements, the spread differential is a substantial 156 basis points compared to public ABS.1 This premium compensates the insurer for the illiquidity of the asset. The core systemic question is whether the liquid FABN liabilities are properly duration-matched against these highly illiquid private credit assets.

## **5\. Holder Composition and Contagion Channels**

The systemic vulnerability of the FABN market cannot be accurately assessed by aggregate volume alone; the risk profile is dictated entirely by *who* holds the paper. The investor base determines the speed, severity, and ultimate transmission mechanism of any potential run.

Because there is no centralized statutory registry detailing the specific holders of FABNs, market composition must be pieced together from mutual fund filings, asset manager disclosures, and regulatory white papers. FABNs are generally underwritten by major investment banks and sold in large blocks to institutional investors. The primary holders fall into three distinct categories: Money Market Funds (MMFs), pension funds, and asset management portfolios (including ETFs and bank portfolios).2

### **5.1 Money Market Funds (MMFs) and the Immediate Run Risk**

MMFs represent a critical and highly sensitive buyer base for the short-term variants of FABNs. For MMFs, the stability of principal is the absolute paramount objective.29 Under SEC Rule 2a-7, which governs registered MMFs, these funds are heavily restricted regarding their credit exposure, liquidity limits, and portfolio maturity.30 They are mandated to hold only the highest tier of short-term credit ratings (e.g., A-1, P-1, or F1).

If MMFs hold a significant share of an insurer's FABN issuance, the contagion risk mimics a traditional commercial-paper-style funding crisis. Major institutional funds are active participants in the short-term insurance paper and repo markets. For example, Fidelity's Government Money Market Fund (SPAXX) and various JPMorgan and Vanguard portfolios actively manage hundreds of billions in short-term liquidity, utilizing government securities and repurchase agreements.32 It is worth noting that following the market stress of 2020, massive funds like the Vanguard Prime Money Market Fund ($125 billion AUM) strategically restructured into the Vanguard Cash Reserves Federal Money Market Fund, moving away from prime commercial paper and insurance credit risk toward pure government and repo exposure to prioritize capital preservation.35

Despite this shift by some mega-funds, prime MMFs and short-duration fixed-income ETFs remain heavy buyers of FABNs due to their attractive yield spreads over Treasuries. Detailed SEC Form N-MFP and N-PORT filings reveal that major bond and income ETFs, such as the Janus Henderson Short Duration Income ETF and the Columbia Threadneedle Core Plus Bond Fund, hold significant, multi-million dollar positions in Athene Global Funding and Global Atlantic notes, typically capturing yields ranging from 4.8% to 5.5%.37

The systemic risk here is acute rollover failure. MMFs and short-duration ETFs do not hold assets through distress. If an insurer suffers a credit downgrade or a broad macroeconomic panic ensues, these funds will immediately cease rolling over short-term notes or exercising extension options. This abrupt halt forces the insurer to find immediate cash to redeem the principal, creating a severe liquidity drain.4

### **5.2 Pension Funds, Bond Portfolios, and the "Slow Bleed"**

For traditional medium-term FABNs (fixed maturities of 3 to 10 years), the primary buyers are long-horizon institutional investors. This includes pension funds, nuclear decommissioning trusts, municipalities, state investment pools, and securities lending cash collateral portfolios.6

If these long-dated instruments form the bulk of an insurer's FABN liability stack, the risk transitions from an immediate, catastrophic bank-run to a "slow bleed." Because standard FABNs are not puttable—meaning investors cannot demand their cash back outside of normal, scheduled maturity dates—the insurer is insulated from sudden liquidity shocks.4

However, this insulation is entirely dependent on the performance of the asset side of the balance sheet. If the insurer's underlying private credit investments (the ABS and middle-market loans) default, suffer severe credit migration, or fail to generate the expected cash flows, the insurer is still legally obligated to service the fixed interest payments on the FABNs. A slow bleed occurs as the insurer is forced to deplete its statutory capital and surplus to cover the spread deficit over the remaining 3-to-5-year life of the notes.

## **6\. Maturity Profiles: Duration Matching vs. Rollover Risk**

The structural stability of the FABN market is sharply divided by maturity, which directly influences the Asset-Liability Management (ALM) risk profile of the issuing insurers.

### **6.1 Termed-Out Debt: Medium-Term FABNs**

The vast majority of traditional FABNs are issued with fixed maturities ranging between three and five years, though terms can occasionally extend up to 10 years.2 Historically, data tracking FABS (Funding Agreement-Backed Securities) indicated a median maturity at issuance of 8 years for standard notes.5 PE-controlled insurers utilize these medium-term structures to strategically fund longer-dated assets without incurring immediate refinancing risk. For instance, Global Atlantic recently priced a 7-year sterling note maturing in 2032 19, and Athene routinely issues paper in the 3-to-5-year duration band.37

In theory, robust ALM practice requires the insurer to perfectly match the duration and cash flows of the funding agreement liability with the underlying private credit investments.4 The NAIC notes that "substantiation of appropriate ALM at the entity level, ensuring duration and cash flow matching," is the primary mitigant against ALM risk.4 However, the NAIC simultaneously admits a critical regulatory blind spot: state regulators currently lack the detailed statutory reporting required to verify whether this duration matching actually occurs in practice across the industry.4

### **6.2 The Rollover Market: FABCP and XFABNs**

The true systemic threat resides at the shorter end of the maturity spectrum. To cater specifically to the liquidity requirements of MMFs and short-term corporate cash pools, insurers issue two highly sensitive instruments:

1. **Funding Agreement-Backed Commercial Paper (FABCP):** These are fixed-term, highly liquid contracts with maturities generally ranging from one week to six months.5 They function identically to standard corporate commercial paper but are backed by the super-senior claim of a funding agreement.  
2. **Extendible Funding Agreement-Backed Notes (XFABN):** XFABNs are particularly insidious in a stress scenario. They typically feature an initial maturity of exactly 397 days—a precise timeframe designed to keep them eligible for purchase by registered MMFs under SEC Rule 2a-7.5 The defining feature of an XFABN is an embedded monthly put option that allows the investor to incrementally extend the maturity by one month.4

Under normal market conditions, XFABNs act like long-term debt because MMFs continuously exercise the extension option to maintain yield. However, if investors lose confidence in the insurer or the broader credit market, they simply stop extending the note. Per the contractual terms, the failure to extend triggers a "spin-off"—the note converts into a new security set to mature at a hard, fixed date, typically exactly one year after the withdrawal notice.5

This mechanism creates a ticking clock for the insurer. The liability transforms from a perpetually rolling instrument into an impending, unavoidable principal repayment. Because regulators explicitly state that they "don't know if Extendable FABNs (XFABN) with embedded put options are issued and/or outstanding," the true volume of put risk embedded in the $239 billion market is currently unknown.4

## **7\. Credit Rating Sensitivities and the Forced-Seller Threshold**

The interconnectedness between short-term FABNs and MMFs creates an absolute, inflexible reliance on credit ratings. FABN ratings are intrinsically linked to the issuing insurance company's financial strength rating or the equivalent rating of the insurer's senior debt.2

### **7.1 Current Ratings of PE-Controlled Insurers**

PE-affiliated insurers have aggressively pursued and achieved high investment-grade ratings to ensure their FABNs remain attractive to institutional buyers, and their status as reliable counterparties remains intact.

* **Athene:** Rated A+ (S\&P), A1 (Moody's), A+ (Fitch), and A+ (AM Best).40  
* **Global Atlantic:** Rated "Excellent" (AM Best) and generally operates securely within the 'A' band across major agencies.41  
* **Fidelity & Guaranty (F\&G):** Following its acquisition by Fidelity National Financial and partnership with Blackstone, F\&G secured upgrades, currently holding ratings of A- (S\&P), A3 (Moody's), and A- (Fitch).20

### **7.2 The Mechanics of Forced Selling and the "Ratings Cliff"**

To remain eligible for purchase by prime MMFs, short-term FABNs and FABCP must carry top-tier short-term credit ratings (e.g., A-1 by S\&P, P-1 by Moody's, or F1 by Fitch). Fitch Ratings, for example, dictates that MMFs rated AAAmmf must restrict their investment universe exclusively to the highest-rated portion of the short-term market.43 Many institutional cash pools and municipal treasuries explicitly mandate by charter that investments must be rated A-1/P-1/F1 by at least two agencies to be eligible for purchase.44

The threshold for forced selling is precise and unforgiving. In standard rating agency methodologies, an issuer generally needs a long-term financial strength rating of at least 'A-' or 'A' to secure and maintain an A-1/P-1 short-term rating.

This creates a perilous "ratings cliff." If an insurer like F\&G (currently rated A-) suffers a single-notch downgrade to BBB+, its short-term rating will automatically fall to A-2/P-2 or lower. The moment this downgrade is published, prime MMFs and conservative institutional cash pools are legally bound by Rule 2a-7 and internal mandates to become forced sellers, or, at minimum, they are prohibited from rolling over maturing paper.44

Because PE-controlled insurers are carrying balance sheets heavily weighted toward private ABS, middle-market corporate loans, and complex structured credit, a severe macroeconomic shock that triggers fundamental downgrades across their asset portfolios would likely result in an issuer downgrade by the rating agencies. This would instantly cut them off from the short-term wholesale funding market. The insurer would face a sudden liquidity crisis, forced to sell highly illiquid private assets at distressed fire-sale prices to meet the cash demands of fleeing MMFs.

## **8\. Stress Testing the Channel: Lessons from 2008 and March 2020**

The behavior of the FABN and broader wholesale funding markets during past crises provides the ultimate blueprint for understanding current systemic risk. The causes and consequences of the 2008 mortgage meltdown and the 2020 COVID-19 shock were vastly different—one a fundamental credit contagion, the other an exogenous liquidity shock—but both severely tested the resilience of short-term institutional funding channels.46

### **8.1 The 2008 Financial Crisis: The XFABN Run**

In the years leading up to 2008, the Funding Agreement-Backed Securities (FABS) market had grown aggressively, peaking at over $170 billion, a size comparable to the auto ABS market at the time.5 As the subprime mortgage crisis metastasized, the market witnessed a textbook "self-fulfilling run" focused intensely on the short-term XFABN segment.5

Starting in the summer of 2007, institutional investors lost confidence in the opacity of financial institutions' balance sheets. Reacting to the uncertainty, MMFs and other investors suddenly stopped exercising their monthly extension options on XFABNs. By contractual design, these instruments immediately converted into "spin-offs" with hard one-year maturity dates.5

This triggered a slow-motion run. Life insurers faced a massive wall of impending maturities but found the capital markets entirely frozen; they could not issue new paper to refinance the old. Consequently, the aggregate outstanding FABS market collapsed dramatically, falling from $170 billion down to roughly $60 billion by the end of 2013\.5

During this period, credit spreads blew out to catastrophic levels. Treasury Option-Adjusted Spreads (OAS) for current-coupon agency MBS jumped from a historical baseline of 50 basis points to over 150 basis points in the fall of 2008\.48 The life insurance industry survived the XFABN run largely by utilizing regulatory arbitrage: they substituted frozen FABN funding with direct borrowing from the Federal Home Loan Banks (FHLB). The FHLB acted as the lender of last resort, providing the cash necessary for insurers to redeem the maturing FABNs without resorting to catastrophic fire sales of their general account assets.5

### **8.2 The March 2020 Market Shock: Spread Spikes and Long-Dated Defenses**

The March 2020 crisis presented a different challenge: it was an acute, violent liquidity shock rather than a fundamental credit collapse. As the global pandemic triggered lockdowns, a massive "dash for cash" ensued. The corporate bond market, representing $7 trillion in funding, experienced intense selling pressure from investors desperate for immediate liquidity.49

Credit spreads gapped out violently across all rating tiers. High-quality investment-grade bonds suffered unprecedented price drops. For example, the bond yield spread for Alphabet (Google)—an entity with a pristine AA rating and massive cash reserves—spiked from 25 basis points in February 2020 to 150 basis points by mid-March. This movement was entirely disconnected from actual default risk, as evidenced by Google's CDS spreads, which barely moved during the same period.49 Aggregate investment-grade corporate credit spreads widened by nearly 300 basis points in the early weeks of the crisis.50

In the FABN space, primary issuance ground to a virtual standstill in mid-March as investors hoarded cash.51 For PE-controlled insurers heavily invested in structured credit and Collateralized Loan Obligations (CLOs), the mark-to-market damage on the asset side was brutal. However, because PE-insurers had successfully structured the bulk of their liabilities (including medium-term FABNs) with persistent, long-term durations, they avoided the trap of becoming forced sellers. As Athene noted in its investor disclosures regarding the 2020 volatility, their long-term liabilities allowed them to hold CLO positions to maturity, averting the need to liquidate assets into a seizing market.52

Once again, the system held because of massive federal intervention. The Federal Reserve established emergency liquidity facilities (such as the Commercial Paper Funding Facility) that implicitly backstopped short-term funding, and the FHLB system provided necessary advances to insurers facing localized cash crunches.3

### **8.3 Synthesizing the Contagion Risk**

The lessons from 2008 and 2020 highlight the core vulnerabilities inherent in the modern FABN market, particularly given its shift toward PE-sponsors funding private credit.

First, the **MMF Trigger** remains potent. If MMFs hold short-dated FABCP or XFABNs and face redemptions (as they did in both 2008 and 2020), they will unequivocally refuse to roll over insurer paper. Second, the **Spread Shock** is a severe threat to the spread-lending business model. If an insurer is earning a 150 basis point spread on a private ABS, and their wholesale funding costs (FABN spreads) blow out by 300 basis points during a panic, they operate at a massive negative carry if forced to refinance.

Third, the **Illiquidity Trap** is much greater today than in 2008\. Unlike traditional insurers holding liquid public corporate bonds, PE-insurers are increasingly holding privately placed ABS, middle-market loans, and complex structured credit.1 These assets cannot be readily sold in a stress event without absorbing catastrophic, surplus-destroying losses. Finally, the system relies on an **Ultimate Backstop**. The industry currently relies on the FHLB to act as a bailout mechanism when the FABN market freezes. The combined $378 billion exposure (comprising $217B in FABNs and $161B in FHLB advances) suggests that a failure in the FABN market would immediately transfer massive systemic stress directly onto the balance sheet of the FHLB system, linking the fate of life insurers directly to federal liquidity.4

## **9\. Conclusion**

The Funding Agreement-Backed Notes market represents a highly efficient, sophisticated mechanism for life insurers to generate spread-based earnings by bridging vast institutional cash pools with long-term credit assets. Under the stewardship of advanced PE-controlled insurers like Athene and Global Atlantic, the market has expanded dramatically, transforming from a niche liability management tool into a primary engine for funding the private credit boom.

However, this explosive growth has significantly outpaced regulatory visibility. The lack of standardized, granular statutory reporting creates a severe macroprudential blind spot regarding the true volume of puttable, short-dated notes and foreign-exchange derivatives embedded within the $239 billion outstanding total.

The systemic vulnerability of this market is not monolithic. For standard, termed-out FABNs held by pension funds and asset managers, the risk is largely contained to the fundamental credit performance of the insurer's underlying private ABS portfolio—a manageable "slow bleed" scenario where losses materialize over years.

Conversely, for the short-term FABCP and XFABN segments held by Money Market Funds, the risk is acute and volatile. This segment relies entirely on the maintenance of top-tier credit ratings and the uninterrupted functioning of short-term liquidity markets. A macroeconomic shock that triggers even a single-notch downgrade for a major PE-insurer could sever its access to MMF capital via Rule 2a-7 restrictions, precipitating a sudden funding crisis that forces fire sales of highly illiquid private assets. As demonstrated conclusively in both 2008 and 2020, when the FABN market freezes, the contagion immediately migrates to the Federal Home Loan Bank system, binding the stability of the private equity life insurance model directly to the resilience of federal liquidity backstops.
