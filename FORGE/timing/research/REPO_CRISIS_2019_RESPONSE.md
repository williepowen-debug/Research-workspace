# The September 2019 Repo Crisis: A Chronological Reconstruction
## From the Initial Shock Through the 2019 Year-End Near-Miss to Early 2020

*Research Date: March 31, 2026*

## Executive Summary

The September 2019 repo crisis represents a critical structural break in U.S. money markets, revealing fundamental vulnerabilities in the Federal Reserve's "ample reserves" framework. This reconstruction demonstrates that while proximate causes (corporate tax payments and Treasury settlement) triggered the crisis, deeper structural issues—concentrated reserves, regulatory constraints on bank lending, and the Fed's shrinking balance sheet—created the conditions for an outsized market reaction. The crisis required unprecedented Fed intervention that continued through early 2020, raising questions about whether structural problems were truly solved or merely masked by subsequent COVID stimulus.

## Key Findings

1. **The Crisis Timeline**: September 16-17, 2019 saw repo rates spike from ~2% to 10%, with the federal funds rate breaching the FOMC target range for the first time since 2008.

2. **Structural Vulnerabilities**: Four major banks held 50% of all Treasury securities but only 25% of reserves, creating distribution mismatches that amplified market stress.

3. **Regulatory Constraints**: Bank lending into repo markets was constrained by regulatory requirements (SLR, LCR) and supervisory expectations, preventing normal arbitrage mechanisms from functioning.

4. **Fed Response Scale**: Initial $75B injection expanded to $500B+ by year-end 2019, with operations continuing through March 2020 (pre-COVID).

5. **December 2019 Near-Miss**: Year-end funding pressures required additional Fed intervention despite months of preparation, suggesting structural problems persisted.

## Chronological Reconstruction

### Monday, September 16, 2019: The Pressure Builds

**Market Conditions at Open:**
- Reserves fell by ~$120 billion over two business days due to quarterly corporate tax payments ($86B) and Treasury settlement ($54B)
- System reserves reached $1.34 trillion, lowest level since 2012
- Primary dealers holding elevated Treasury inventory from new issuance

**Intraday Developments:**
- SOFR opened at 2.43%, up 13bp from Friday (2.30%)
- EFFR printed at 2.25%, 11bp above Friday and at the top of FOMC target range (2.00-2.25%)
- Repo market exhibited broad rate dispersion as cash became scarce
- Banks with excess reserves did not increase lending despite attractive rates

**Why Banks Didn't Respond:**
According to Bank Policy Institute analysis and Fed staff reports:
- Regulatory constraints: Substituting reserves for reverse repo would violate supervisory expectations about liquidity buffer composition
- LCR requirements: While technically neutral, supervisory guidance favored holding reserves over Treasury securities
- Discount window stigma: Banks held excess reserves specifically to avoid ever needing emergency borrowing

### Tuesday, September 17, 2019: The Breaking Point

**Morning Crisis:**
- SOFR spiked above 5% intraday, with some transactions reaching 10%
- EFFR moved to 2.30%, breaching the top of the FOMC target range
- Intraday EFFR range expanded to nearly 200bp (vs. typical <10bp)
- Fed funds market saw minimal activity until Fed intervention

**Fed Response - 9:30 AM:**
- NY Fed announced first overnight repo operation since 2008
- Offered $75 billion against Treasury, agency, and agency MBS collateral
- Operation provided $53.2 billion in reserves
- Immediate rate decline following announcement

**Market Dynamics:**
- Distribution of trades expanded significantly in both repo and fed funds markets
- Borrowers maintained stable volumes despite rate spikes (inelastic demand)
- Lenders reluctant to increase activity despite profit opportunities
- Money market fund exposure limits constrained additional lending

### September 18-20, 2019: Initial Stabilization

**Fed Operations Continue:**
- September 18: $75B overnight repo, fully subscribed
- September 19: $75B overnight repo, fully subscribed  
- September 20: Fed announced daily operations through October 10

**Technical Adjustments (September 19):**
- IOER lowered by 5bp to 20bp below top of target range
- ON RRP rate lowered by 5bp to 5bp below bottom of target range
- Designed to reduce risk of EFFR moving above target range

**Term Operations Announced (September 20):**
- Schedule of term repo operations spanning September quarter-end
- Addressed potential year-end funding stress
- Recognition that overnight operations alone insufficient

### October 2019: Persistent Intervention Required

**October 11 - Major Policy Announcement:**
The FOMC announced two critical measures:
1. **Treasury Bill Purchases**: $60 billion per month to offset growth in non-reserve liabilities
2. **Extended Repo Operations**: Overnight and term operations through at least January 2020

**Scale of Operations:**
- Daily overnight repos: Initially $75B, increased to $120B on October 23
- Term repos: Increased from $35B to $45B per operation
- Total outstanding repo operations averaged ~$190B daily in October

**Lorie Logan's Diagnosis (November 4, 2019):**
NY Fed Senior VP identified structural issues:
- "The quantity of reserves needed to maintain an ample reserves framework is subject to uncertainty"
- Reserves distribution inefficiencies between banks
- Growth in non-reserve liabilities (currency, TGA) requiring ongoing intervention
- Recognition that $60B monthly T-bill purchases would continue indefinitely

### November-December 2019: Preparing for Year-End

**Ongoing Operations:**
- Daily overnight repos of at least $120B
- Bi-weekly term repos of at least $35B
- Weekly 4-6 week term repos of at least $15B

**December 2019 Near-Miss:**
Despite extensive preparation with pre-positioned term repos and T-bill purchases, year-end funding pressures still required significant Fed intervention:
- Term repos expanded to cover year-end period
- Additional liquidity provisions as banks managed balance sheet constraints
- Suggests structural problems not fully resolved by ongoing operations

## Analysis: Proximate vs. Structural Causes

### Proximate Triggers (September 16-17):
1. **Corporate Tax Payments**: $86B quarterly payments drained reserves
2. **Treasury Settlement**: $54B long-term debt settlement increased dealer inventory
3. **Total Reserve Drain**: ~$120B over two business days

### Structural Vulnerabilities:

#### 1. Reserve Distribution Problem
- Four largest banks held 50% of Treasury securities but only 25% of reserves
- Banks became net lenders to repo market as rates rose above IOER in 2018
- Concentration created systemic dependencies on few institutions

#### 2. Regulatory Constraints on Bank Arbitrage
**Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR)**:
- Borrowing to fund repo lending would reduce leverage ratios
- Quarterly point-in-time measurements discouraged temporary expansion
- GSIB surcharge calculations included cross-jurisdictional activity

**Liquidity Requirements**:
- LCR technically neutral between reserves and Treasury reverse repo
- Supervisory expectations strongly favored reserves over securities
- Internal stress tests assumed significant discounts on Treasury liquidation

**Resolution Planning**:
- Non-public liquidity requirements associated with living wills
- Examiner preferences for cash over securities explicitly communicated

#### 3. Market Structure Changes
**Post-Crisis Evolution**:
- Money market activity reduced during QE period (2008-2017)
- Hysteresis effects: Loss of market-making capabilities and staff expertise
- Reduced willingness to respond quickly to arbitrage opportunities

**Counterparty Limits**:
- Money market funds facing exposure limits to hedge funds and dealers
- FICC-cleared bilateral market growth created new dependencies
- Clearing arrangements concentrated credit risk at dealer sponsors

## The December 2019 Near-Miss

**Background:**
- Year-end balance sheet management typically creates funding stress
- Fed pre-positioned significant liquidity through term operations
- T-bill purchase program running at $60B/month since October

**What Happened:**
Despite extensive preparation, year-end 2019 still required additional Fed intervention:
- Term repos expanded beyond planned levels
- Additional operations to manage balance sheet effects
- Markets remained fragile despite months of continuous support

**Implications:**
The December near-miss demonstrated that:
1. Structural problems were not resolved by ongoing operations
2. Markets remained dependent on Fed support
3. Balance sheet constraints continued to bind at quarter-ends
4. The "ample reserves" level was still insufficient for smooth functioning

## Early 2020: Pre-COVID Assessment

### January-February 2020: Continued Dependence

**Operations Continue:**
- Daily overnight repos through at least January (later extended)
- T-bill purchases ongoing at $60B/month
- Term operations maintained around key dates

**Fed Assessment:**
- Operations described as successful in maintaining stability
- Federal funds rate remained well within target range
- Markets functioned smoothly even on Treasury issuance dates

**Structural Questions Unresolved:**
By early 2020, fundamental questions remained:
1. What was the true "ample reserves" level needed for smooth functioning?
2. Had regulatory constraints on bank intermediation been addressed?
3. Were markets genuinely stable or dependent on Fed support?

### March 2020: COVID Disruption

The COVID crisis beginning in March 2020 dramatically altered market dynamics:
- Massive fiscal stimulus increased reserves
- Emergency repo facilities expanded further
- Regulatory relief provided (including SLR relief)
- Questions about structural repo market stability became moot in new environment

## Assessment: Structural Problems Solved or Masked?

### Evidence for "Solved":
1. Fed operations successfully maintained stability through early 2020
2. Federal funds rate stayed within target range
3. Money market volatility decreased after initial interventions
4. T-bill purchase program offset structural reserve drains

### Evidence for "Masked":
1. December 2019 near-miss despite extensive preparation
2. Continued dependence on Fed operations through March 2020
3. Regulatory constraints on bank lending never directly addressed
4. Reserve concentration issues unchanged
5. COVID stimulus made assessment impossible by dramatically expanding reserves

## Lessons for Current Context (2026)

**Critical Relevance:**
Fed's Perli (March 26, 2026) noted that reserve ampleness metrics have returned to Q1 2019 levels—the exact conditions that preceded the September 2019 crisis.

**Key Risk Factors Still Present:**
1. **Reserve Concentration**: Banking sector consolidation continues
2. **Regulatory Constraints**: SLR and LCR requirements largely unchanged
3. **Balance Sheet Seasonality**: Treasury issuance and tax payment cycles continue
4. **Market Structure**: Repo market remains segmented and concentrated

**Policy Implications:**
1. Current reserve levels may be inadequate for smooth market functioning
2. Standing repo facility may be necessary but doesn't address underlying structural issues
3. Regulatory reforms may be needed to restore bank intermediation capacity
4. Careful monitoring of reserve distribution across institutions essential

## Conclusion

The September 2019 repo crisis revealed fundamental structural vulnerabilities in U.S. money markets that went beyond temporary liquidity shortages. While the Fed's response successfully stabilized markets through early 2020, the December 2019 near-miss and continued dependence on Fed operations suggest that structural problems were managed rather than solved. The subsequent COVID-driven expansion of reserves masked these vulnerabilities, but their return to Q1 2019 levels in 2026 suggests the same structural risks may be re-emerging.

The crisis demonstrates that "ample reserves" is not simply a quantity but depends on distribution, regulatory constraints, and market structure. Without addressing the underlying regulatory obstacles to bank intermediation and the concentration of reserves in the banking system, markets may remain vulnerable to similar disruptions when reserves fall to apparently "ample" levels.
