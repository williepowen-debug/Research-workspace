# The Houthis will outlast Iran — and the Red Sea will never be the same

**The Houthis represent a persistent maritime threat that will survive the collapse of Iranian support, though in degraded form.** Across every dimension — financial independence, indigenous production, territorial control, ideological autonomy — Ansar Allah is structurally different from other Iranian proxies. Their critical vulnerability is technological, not financial or political: Iran supplies the guidance seekers, rocket motors, and turbojet engines that power the Houthis' most advanced weapons. Without these components, the group loses long-range precision strike capability but retains the geographic chokepoint leverage, mine-laying capacity, drone swarms, and institutional resilience to threaten Red Sea commerce indefinitely. As of March 22, 2026, the Houthis have conducted zero kinetic operations since the Iran war began on February 28 — a deliberate "fleet in being" strategy that generates deterrence value without expenditure. This restraint is the strongest evidence yet that Houthi leadership is already planning for a post-Iran future.

---

## A hybrid arsenal: what the Houthis can and cannot build alone

The Houthis operate the most sophisticated military-industrial base of any non-state actor in the world, yet it remains fundamentally a **final-assembly operation** for advanced systems. A 2026 Century International study of 140 weapons seizures found that over **80% of intercepted items were manufacturing inputs** — seekers, engines, guidance modules — rather than finished weapons. This confirms the pipeline has evolved from smuggling complete systems to sustaining domestic production lines. But the inputs remain irreplaceable.

**Anti-ship ballistic missiles** — the weapons that made the Houthis the first non-state actor to fire ASBMs in combat — illustrate the dependency clearly. At least six ASBM types have been identified, all of Iranian design lineage. The Houthis can assemble some variants domestically (the Asef from Iranian conversion kits, the Mohit from converted legacy S-75 airframes), but they **cannot independently produce** the electro-optical/infrared terminal seekers, GNSS-aided guidance packages, or maneuverable reentry vehicles that make these weapons effective against moving ships. RUSI analysts note that MARV technology "could not be easily moved from Iran," which may explain the poor hit rate against maneuvering vessels.

**Drones represent the area of greatest indigenous capability.** The Houthis have mass-produced the Samad series largely independently, and one analyst assessed them as "now capable of mass-producing the Samad series drones independently." Production rates are estimated in the dozens per month — in March 2021 alone, 70 missiles and drones were launched at Saudi Arabia, with 340 total that year. The most advanced variants (Waid-2, based on the Shahed-136) still require Iranian-supplied engines and targeting systems, but simpler models use commercially available piston engines purchasable online for approximately $29,000. An August 2025 seizure at Aden revealed Chinese-sourced equipment for a "semi-industrial drone production line" — described as the first clear evidence of an attempt to establish self-sustaining manufacturing.

**Naval mines are the most indigenized weapons category.** Over **3,452 marine mines** have been found and disposed of on Yemeni beaches and islands. The Houthis have displayed at least 17 mine models including contact, acoustic, magnetic, and moored variants. Most are locally produced with Iranian expertise. This capability matters enormously: the Houthis could mine Bab el-Mandeb at scale. Their decision not to during 2024 reflected strategic restraint, not limitation.

**Anti-ship cruise missiles and ballistic missiles remain overwhelmingly Iranian-dependent.** The turbojet engines powering cruise missiles (Iranian Tolu-4 series) and the liquid/solid-fuel rocket motors for ballistic missiles cannot be produced in Yemen. Only five ballistic missile motors have ever been captured across the entire war — from a single January 2024 shipment. Between September 2024 and July 2025, UN experts counted **101 Houthi ballistic missiles fired at Israel, of which 38 failed outright**, suggesting quality control issues even with Iranian components.

## The supply chain has globalized beyond Iran

The most significant finding from recent seizure data is that **China has surpassed Iran as the largest single source of Houthi weapons components.** Century International's analysis of 2021–2025 seizures found **35% of items originated in China** compared to 21% from Iran, with the remainder from India, Germany, the Netherlands, South Korea, and other countries. This is not a Beijing-directed effort but a decentralized procurement network: Houthi operatives based in eastern China's export hubs purchase dual-use components — drone engines, propellers, navigation modules, CNC equipment — through commercial channels and Yemeni-run logistics companies.

Four confirmed smuggling routes sustain the pipeline. Maritime shipments run directly from Bandar Abbas to Hodeidah, or via the Horn of Africa (Iran to Djibouti/Somalia to Yemen). Overland routes cross Oman's border at Shahan and Sarfayt — in March 2025, 800 Chinese drone propellers were seized at Sarfayt. A fourth route runs through Eritrea and Sudan to Red Sea islands. The UK government found that **500 unsearched ships** serviced Hodeidah port during eight months of 2024 alone.

**Russia is actively positioning as an alternative patron**, though it cannot replicate the depth of Iranian support. U.S. Treasury sanctioned Houthi leaders for arranging weapons deals with Moscow. Russian GRU advisors reportedly operate in Sana'a under humanitarian cover. The Wall Street Journal reported Houthis received Russian satellite data for targeting Red Sea shipping. Viktor Bout negotiated a $10 million small arms deal. But Moscow's engagement remains constrained by its desire to maintain Saudi and UAE relationships.

If Iranian supply is cut for **six months**, analysts project drone production continues at reduced rate from stockpiled components and Chinese commercial parts, while ASBM and ballistic missile launches deplete rapidly. At **twelve months**, ballistic missile capability is severely degraded, advanced drone variants slow significantly, and the Houthis shift toward simpler, shorter-range systems. At **eighteen months**, strategic strike capability against distant targets is likely exhausted — but the group remains a significant regional threat concentrated in shorter-range drones, mines, explosive boats, and geographic chokepoint control. As one senior U.S. defense official acknowledged: "There's some debate as to what's in their magazine."

## Red Sea interdiction: disruption, not closure, but disruption is enough

The Houthis conducted approximately **150 attacks on commercial vessels in 2024** (ACLED data), sinking three ships (Rubymar, Tutor, Magic Seas), seizing one (Galaxy Leader), and killing at least **18 mariners**. They became the first non-state actor to deploy anti-ship ballistic missiles, unmanned surface vehicles, and multi-domain swarming attacks against commercial shipping. At peak disruption, container shipping through the Red Sea fell by **90%**, Suez Canal transits dropped 58%, and Bab el-Mandeb transits hit a record low — down **65% from pre-crisis levels**.

The Houthis cannot physically close the 16–20-mile-wide Bab el-Mandeb strait the way Iran can close Hormuz. They lack submarines, a conventional navy, or the geographic position to block passage. But this distinction is largely academic. **What they demonstrated in 2024 is that you don't need to physically close a strait — you need to make transit commercially unviable.** War risk insurance premiums surged from effectively zero to **0.7–1.0% of hull value** (translating to $500,000–$1 million per transit for a $100 million vessel). At those rates, the Cape of Good Hope route — despite adding 10–14 days and $1 million in fuel costs — became the rational economic choice. The Washington Institute assessed the Houthis had "effectively turned the Bab al-Mandab chokepoint into an anti-access/area-denial zone."

Attack tempo dropped **84%** in 2025 — only 7 commercial vessel attacks versus 150 the prior year — but this reflected strategic recalibration, not diminished capacity. The Houthis shifted to long-range strikes on Israel (125 strikes in the first 11 months of 2025, a 120% increase over 2024). After the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire, all operations paused. Since the Iran war began on February 28, 2026, the Houthis have issued threats but conducted **zero confirmed kinetic operations**. The Soufan Center reported in March 2026 that Houthi officials told AP their weapons stockpile is "running low" after the 2023–2025 campaign, while Iran's war has "further disrupted supplies" — though they retain a "significant stockpile of drones."

The anti-shipping capability remains latent and potent. As a 2026 Global Security Review concluded: "The current situation does not reflect the active Red Sea crisis levels of 2024–2025. However, it reflects something equally important: institutionalized readiness."

## The strategic reserve: why the Houthis are sitting out their patron's war

The strongest consensus across Western, Israeli, and Arab think tanks is that the Houthis are the Iranian proxy **most likely to survive Iranian collapse** — and that Houthi leadership knows this. The analytical framework is the "strategic reserve" theory: the Houthis are deliberately conserving capability, maintaining deterrence value without expenditure, and preserving the Red Sea disruption option as an escalation card that grows more powerful as the war continues.

Five interlocking factors explain the restraint. First, **stockpile conservation**: weapons are running low and Iran can no longer resupply, so every missile fired is irreplaceable. Second, **signature avoidance**: as Foreign Policy noted in March 2026, "the moment they launch a visible military operation, they generate the signatures that enable targeting — communications, movement, and electronic emissions. Holding rallies does not produce those signatures. Firing missiles does." The August 2025 Israeli decapitation strikes that killed Prime Minister Ahmed al-Rahawi and 12 cabinet members demonstrated the lethal consequence of operational signatures. Third, **the Saudi détente**: with Hormuz closed, Saudi Arabia now depends on Red Sea routes for oil exports via the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu — making Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping a direct threat to Riyadh. Saudi Arabia is reportedly communicating this as a red line. Fourth, **domestic legitimacy**: the moral imperative that made Gaza-related attacks popular does not extend to fighting Iran's war, which many Yemenis view as another foreign power's quarrel. Fifth, **IRGC direction**: some analysts speculate the IRGC itself may be discouraging Houthi entry, recognizing that preserving the Houthi card maintains Iran's last asymmetric option.

This "fleet in being" logic is economically rational. As one analyst framed it: "Every day that the Houthis hold back while maintaining the posture of imminent action, the threat generates value without expenditure. Shipping insurance premiums remain elevated. Saudi planning must account for a possible southern offensive." The Houthis are running a double lever — tying down U.S. and Israeli military assets in defensive posture while expending nothing.

The historical comparison to other proxies is instructive. Hezbollah, created by Iran and doctrinally committed to velayat-e faqih, faces existential crisis if Iran falls — and has already been severely degraded by Israeli operations. Iraqi PMF factions have mixed dependence. Hamas, a Sunni organization, has alternative backers in Qatar and Turkey. **The Houthis predate their Iranian relationship by decades**, rooted in Zaydism — a branch of Shia Islam distinct from Iranian Twelver Shiism — and the thousand-year Zaydi imamate tradition of northern Yemen. AEI's Michael Rubin captured the distinction: "The Iranians did not create every proxy; they co-opted many. Those they did create — Hezbollah, and many of the Iraqi groups — will wither. But those whose creation predates the Islamic Republic might manage to survive."

## Financial independence makes this a self-sustaining threat

**Iranian direct cash transfers to the Houthis — estimated at $100–300 million annually — represent only 3–7% of total Houthi revenue.** This is the most underappreciated dimension of Houthi independence. The group operates a multi-billion-dollar war economy that dwarfs Iranian financial support.

The largest revenue stream is port customs and fuel import taxes. The Houthis control 80% of Yemen's imports through Hodeidah, Al-Salif, and Ras Issa. The UN Panel of Experts documented **$789 million** in tax and customs revenues from oil derivatives and commodities in just 13 months (May 2023–June 2024), with total income from the fuel and port sector estimated at **$5.5 billion** in the same period. Telecommunications generates approximately $92 million annually. The banking sector, controlled through CBY-Sana'a, provides additional hundreds of millions — the Houthis seized roughly $5 billion in foreign currency reserves and froze $120 million belonging to Yemenia Airways. The Houthi cryptocurrency operation has processed **over $900 million** in outflows across sanctioned wallet addresses, according to TRM Labs.

Most striking is the Red Sea "safe transit" fee model. The UN Security Council Panel of Experts reported the Houthis collected an estimated **$180 million per month** from ships transiting Bab el-Mandeb — potentially over $2 billion annually when active. This revenue stream, which creates a self-reinforcing incentive to maintain the threat, was suspended after the October 2025 ceasefire but could resume instantly.

Total estimated annual Houthi revenue ranges from **$4–8 billion** at peak capacity — though U.S. and Israeli airstrikes on ports, the April 2025 FTO designation, and the petroleum import ban have significantly curtailed several streams. The critical point remains: **the dependency on Iran is technological, not financial.** The Houthis can fund governance and basic military operations indefinitely from domestic revenue. What they cannot fund is the replacement of precision-guided missiles and advanced seekers.

## After Iran: degraded but dangerous, and permanently positioned

If the Iranian regime collapses or is severely weakened, the analytical consensus holds that the Houthis become **less capable but not less threatening** — and potentially less predictable. The arguments cut both ways, but the weight of evidence favors persistent danger.

On the "more dangerous" side: an unconstrained Houthi movement, freed from Iranian strategic direction, could act more recklessly to demonstrate value to potential new patrons or extract revenue from shipping disruption. Brookings has noted it is "extremely difficult, if not impossible, to create a 'deterrence equation' with the Houthis, who have little to lose." The safe-transit fee model provides a powerful independent financial incentive to continue maritime attacks regardless of patron status. And the Houthis' anti-Western, anti-Israel ideology is indigenous — it predates the Iranian relationship and does not disappear with it.

On the "less dangerous" side: precision-guided ballistic missiles, advanced anti-ship cruise missiles, and sophisticated long-range drones degrade without Iranian components. Technical advisors leave. New procurement networks take years to establish. The group reverts from a power-projection force capable of striking Israel and deep Saudi targets to a regional threat concentrated in shorter-range systems.

**The most likely post-Iran Houthi threat profile combines degraded advanced capability with enhanced lower-tech maritime disruption.** Sea mines — the most indigenized weapons category — can be produced locally at scale. Explosive-laden unmanned surface vehicles use commercially available components. FPV drones with Chinese-sourced parts cost a fraction of ballistic missiles. Fast boats with RPGs and ATGMs require no foreign patron. And the geographic advantage is permanent: whoever controls Yemen's Red Sea coast controls leverage over Bab el-Mandeb.

Russia is the most plausible partial replacement patron, with GRU advisors already present, satellite data already shared, and weapons procurement channels already sanctioned. China will maintain deniable transactional ties — dual-use components, commercial drone parts — but is highly unlikely to become an overt patron given its enormous stake in Red Sea trade stability. Neither can replicate the depth of Iranian support, but the Houthis may not need them to. A degraded Houthi maritime threat — operating from underground facilities, using commercially sourced drone swarms, collecting transit fees from shipping companies — is self-sustaining.

## The dual-chokepoint crisis is already here

The simultaneous disruption scenario is no longer hypothetical. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since March 2, 2026, with near-zero AIS traffic, approximately **170 containerships and 63 VLCCs trapped inside the Gulf**, and war risk premiums reaching **5% of vessel value** — $5 million for a $100 million ship. The IEA called it "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market," with Rapidan Energy Group estimating 20% of global oil supply disrupted — more than double the Suez Crisis record.

The architectural vulnerability is precise and devastating. Saudi Arabia activated its East-West Pipeline to bypass Hormuz, routing crude to the Red Sea port of Yanbu, with 24–30 large crude carriers heading there by mid-March. **But those tankers must transit south through Bab el-Mandeb to reach Asian buyers** — who receive 84% of Hormuz crude. The workaround for Hormuz depends entirely on Bab el-Mandeb staying open. If the Houthis close it, there is no maritime alternative. Total pipeline bypass capacity (Saudi East-West plus UAE's ADCOP) maxes at roughly **5–5.5 million barrels per day** — covering only 25–33% of lost Hormuz flows, and even those barrels face transit risk.

Oil prices surged from approximately $70 to over **$103 per barrel** within two weeks. The Dallas Federal Reserve modeled a one-quarter Hormuz closure as raising WTI to $98/barrel and reducing global GDP growth by **2.9 percentage points annualized**. A prolonged dual-chokepoint closure pushes estimates to $135–150/barrel, with analysts describing a "guaranteed global recession." Strategic petroleum reserves — even the largest coordinated IEA release in history at 400 million barrels — cover only approximately 4 days of global consumption, and SPR drawdown rates of 1.4 mb/d cover just 15% of lost Hormuz supply.

The insurance market has become the primary transmission mechanism. As RBC Capital Markets' Helima Croft observed, Iran didn't need a naval blockade — "several drone strikes" sufficed to make insurers withdraw. Lloyd's Joint War Committee expanded the designated war zone. The International Group of P&I Clubs voided existing Hormuz war risk coverage. Finding hull insurance for Hormuz transit became, in S&P Global's assessment, "almost impossible." If the Houthis resume Red Sea attacks, the entire Arabian Peninsula maritime zone becomes uninsurable at commercial rates — an unprecedented situation exceeding anything in the 1980s Tanker War, when the strait never actually closed and disruption affected less than 2% of Gulf traffic.

## Conclusion: a permanent feature of the maritime landscape

The core analytical question has a clear answer: **the Houthis represent a persistent maritime threat that will outlast the current Iran war, though the nature of that threat transforms.** The group transitions from a precision-strike force capable of hitting targets at 1,500+ kilometers to a chokepoint-exploitation force relying on mines, drone swarms, explosive boats, and geographic leverage. This is still enormously consequential — the 2024 campaign demonstrated that commercial deterrence requires far less than military dominance.

Three factors make this threat structurally permanent. First, financial self-sufficiency: the Houthis generate billions annually from domestic taxation and the war economy, with Iranian cash representing a small fraction. Second, geographic permanence: control of Yemen's Red Sea coast is a non-depreciating strategic asset. Third, ideological independence: Houthi motivations are indigenous to Yemeni politics and Zaydi identity, not imported from Tehran.

The most dangerous near-term scenario is not Houthi entry into the Iran war but the inverse — the Houthis' calculated restraint positioning them as the sole surviving node of the Axis of Resistance, armed with the demonstrated ability to impose billions in costs on global trade, and incentivized by a revenue model that rewards disruption. **The Red Sea security environment of 2019 — routine commercial transit through Bab el-Mandeb at negligible insurance cost — is unlikely to return regardless of the Iran war's outcome.** The question is whether the new normal is periodic disruption at Houthi discretion or negotiated accommodation with a de facto maritime toll authority. Neither outcome was imaginable a decade ago. Both are now plausible features of the international order.
