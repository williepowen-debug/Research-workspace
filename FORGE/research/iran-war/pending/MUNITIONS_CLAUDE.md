# America's munitions math doesn't add up

**The US-Israeli air campaign against Iran is consuming years of weapons production in days, and the defense industrial base cannot close the gap before stockpiles of critical interceptors run dry.** Operation Epic Fury, launched February 28, 2026, expended approximately **5,197 munitions across 35 weapon types in its first 96 hours** — a replacement bill of $10–16 billion that would cost an estimated $32 billion to actually reproduce. While the campaign has shifted to cheaper gravity bombs to sustain offensive operations, the binding constraint is not bombs but interceptors: the Patriot, THAAD, SM-3, and Arrow missiles that keep US and allied bases alive under Iranian retaliation. These systems are being consumed at rates that dwarf annual production by orders of magnitude, and the industrial base — hobbled by sole-source suppliers, Chinese mineral dependencies, and 24-month manufacturing lead times — cannot surge fast enough to matter during this conflict.

The implications extend far beyond Iran. Every high-end interceptor fired in the Middle East is one fewer available for a Taiwan contingency. As FPRI analysts warned: the coalition can keep bombing because gravity bombs are plentiful, but "the strategic constraint sits in the invisible parts — the interceptors that keep bases alive, the long-range weapons that hold enemy launchers at risk."

## 5,197 munitions in 96 hours revealed the depth of the problem

The opening phase of Epic Fury was the most intensive air campaign in modern history. Iran launched over **800 drones and 350 ballistic missiles on Day 1 alone**, triggering a massive defensive response across the Gulf. Within the first 36 hours, the US-Israeli coalition consumed more than **3,000 precision-guided munitions and interceptors at a cost of approximately $5.6 billion**. By the 96-hour mark, FPRI's Payne Institute analysis — the most granular open-source accounting available — documented expenditure across every major weapon class.

The most alarming consumption rates were in air defense interceptors. Coalition forces fired **943 Patriot PAC-3 rounds in 96 hours** — equivalent to **18 months of Lockheed Martin's entire annual production** consumed in four days. THAAD interceptors, already depleted by 20–50% during the June 2025 Twelve-Day War, lost another third or more of remaining inventory. Arrow-3 interceptors — Israel's primary ballistic missile shield — saw over half their inventory consumed in the same period. SM-3 stocks, already down ~20% from 2024–2025 operations against Houthi missiles and the October 2024 Iranian attack on Israel, took further hits.

Offensive standoff weapons also burned fast. Approximately **375 Tomahawk cruise missiles** were fired in the first 96 hours — against an annual production rate of just 85 missiles per year. At that consumption rate, it would take **53 months to replace four days of shooting**. JASSM/JASSM-ER stocks, estimated at ~3,500 pre-war, were drawn down but remain healthier due to larger inventories. Eight GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrators — the only weapon capable of reaching Iran's deepest bunkers — were expended from a pre-war inventory of roughly 35, with no replacements expected before 2028.

The shift that followed was telling. By Day 4, General Dan Caine announced a "point of munitions transition" — moving from standoff weapons fired at range to direct-attack gravity bombs dropped from aircraft overhead. Defense Secretary Hegseth framed the pivot as tactical progress enabled by air supremacy, but *The American Prospect* and *The Washington Post* independently reported the shift was driven by rapidly depleting stocks of expensive precision weapons. Retired Colonel Mark Gunzinger of the Mitchell Institute confirmed the underlying logic: "We have a much, much larger stockpile — tens of thousands certainly — of JDAMs and small diameter bombs." The unit economics explain the urgency. A JDAM kit costs **$25,000** versus **$2 million** for a Tomahawk or **$1.5 million** for a JASSM-ER.

## Production rates measured in hundreds cannot replace consumption measured in thousands

The defense industrial base was not built for this tempo of conflict. Decades of "just-in-time" manufacturing optimization created an ecosystem designed for peacetime efficiency, not wartime resilience. The mismatch between consumption and production is stark across every critical system.

The Trump administration recognized the problem before the war began. In January and February 2026, the Pentagon signed landmark multi-year framework agreements with defense primes to dramatically increase production: PAC-3 MSE from 620 to **2,000 per year**, THAAD from 96 to **400 per year**, Tomahawk from 85 to over **1,000 per year**, and SM-6 from 125 to over **500 per year**. These are transformational targets. They are also years from realization. No new THAAD interceptors are expected to be delivered until **April 2027**. The Tomahawk ramp to 1,000/year requires facility expansion, supply chain qualification, and workforce hiring that takes 24–36 months.

Within one week of the war's start, White House officials summoned executives from Boeing, Lockheed Martin, RTX, Northrop Grumman, and General Dynamics to discuss accelerating production. The Pentagon reportedly requested a **$200 billion supplemental** appropriation for campaign costs and munitions replenishment. The reconciliation bill already allocated $25.3 billion for munitions, and the FY2026 NDAA authorized over $25 billion more. But as FPRI analysts observed: **"Congress can appropriate $16 billion overnight. It cannot appropriate gallium, neodymium, or ammonium perchlorate into existence."**

## Seven chokepoints that money alone cannot fix

The munitions production bottleneck is not primarily a funding problem. It is a physics, chemistry, and industrial architecture problem. A series of sole-source suppliers and constrained material flows create sequential chokepoints, any one of which can independently halt production.

**Ammonium perchlorate** — the oxidizer in virtually every solid rocket motor — has a single domestic source: AMPAC in Cedar City, Utah. The 600 tons needed to reload one 96-hour operation represents **6.7% of the plant's entire annual capacity**. Every Patriot, THAAD, Arrow, SM-3, SM-6, ATACMS, and GMLRS round requires this material. There is no second source.

**RDX and HMX high explosives** are produced at a single facility: the Holston Army Ammunition Plant in Kingsport, Tennessee, operated by BAE Systems and built during World War II. As of March 12 — two weeks into the war — **Holston had not received orders to increase production**. Every missile warhead, torpedo, and precision-guided munition depends on this plant.

**Williams International in Pontiac, Michigan** is the sole manufacturer of the F107 turbofan engine that powers the Tomahawk, JASSM, JASSM-ER, and LRASM. No amount of funding can widen this bottleneck without years of facility expansion or qualification of a second source. **Boeing's PAC-3 seeker assembly** line in Huntsville, Alabama — expanded by 33% in 2025 — remains the binding constraint on PAC-3 production regardless of how fast other components can be manufactured.

The most strategically dangerous dependency is on **Chinese-controlled critical minerals**. China controls **98% of global gallium production**, 90% of neodymium processing, and 99% of dysprosium — all essential for seeker heads, guidance systems, and radar modules. China banned gallium exports to the US in December 2024 and imposed sweeping rare earth export controls in 2025, including a "foreign direct product rule" requiring Chinese government approval for any product containing trace amounts of Chinese-origin rare earth materials. FPRI calculated that replacing the radar systems destroyed in the campaign's first days requires **more gallium than replacing all 5,197 munitions combined** — a single AN/FPS-132 early warning radar contains approximately 75 kg of gallium, versus 18 kg for the entire munitions expenditure.

The solid rocket motor supply chain — shared across nearly every missile program — has only two established primes (L3Harris/Aerojet Rocketdyne and Northrop Grumman), with Anduril qualifying as a third supplier in August 2025. Sub-tier bottlenecks in nozzles, insulation, steel motor cases, and ignition safety devices cascade throughout the system. The Pentagon invested $1 billion directly in L3Harris and allocated $500 million for SRM industrial base expansion, but these investments will take years to yield meaningful production increases.

## Iran's $30,000 drones versus $12 million interceptors

The cost-exchange ratio at the heart of this conflict represents a fundamental strategic asymmetry that favors Iran. A Shahed-136 drone costs an estimated **$20,000–$50,000** to produce — possibly less, given Iran's emphasis on mass production using commercial components from Texas Instruments, Analog Devices, and other widely available suppliers. The interceptors used to shoot them down cost between **$4 million (Patriot PAC-3 MSE)** and **$12–15 million (THAAD)**. Even at conservative estimates, this creates a cost ratio of **100:1 to 450:1 in favor of the attacker**.

Iran exploited this asymmetry aggressively in the campaign's opening days. Over **2,000 Shahed-136 drones** were launched into the Gulf region in the first six days, with the UAE alone facing 1,422 detected drones and 246 missiles in eight days. The coalition achieved impressive intercept rates — 94% against drone waves — but at ruinous cost. The value of air defense interceptors expended in the first 100 hours alone was estimated at **$1.7–3.7 billion**.

The Iranian attack rate declined sharply after the first week — ballistic missile launches fell over 90% from 350 on Day 1 to approximately 25 by Day 15, and drone launches dropped from 800+ to roughly 75. Analysts disagree on whether this reflects successful suppression of Iranian launch infrastructure or deliberate conservation for a longer war. War on the Rocks analysis argued the pattern was "more consistent with recalibration than capacity constraints." Iran's pre-war production capacity was estimated at 50–100 ballistic missiles per month and 200–500 drones per month. Underground production facilities remain largely intact.

The emerging counter to this asymmetry is interceptor drones. Ukraine's battlefield-tested Merops drone — costing approximately **$15,000 per unit** with a 95% hit rate against Shaheds — represents a potential reset of the cost equation. The UK announced deployment of Ukrainian drone experts to Gulf states to replicate this model. The US deployed its own LUCAS drone (a reverse-engineered Shahed-136 clone at ~$35,000/unit) for its combat debut in the offensive role. Directed energy weapons offer even more dramatic economics — Israel's Iron Beam laser system costs roughly **$3.50 per shot** in energy — but the Pentagon acknowledged these systems are 36 months from fielding at scale.

## The invisible bleed: Pacific readiness eroding in real time

The most consequential impact of Epic Fury's munitions consumption may not be felt in the Middle East but in the Western Pacific. The Pentagon is **redeploying THAAD batteries from the Indo-Pacific to the Middle East**, directly degrading the deterrent posture against China. Every SM-3 and SM-6 fired against Iranian missiles is one fewer available for a Taiwan Strait contingency — a scenario where CSIS war games consistently showed US forces running out of key munitions within one week.

The Heritage Foundation's January 2026 assessment warned that US forces would enter the main phase of combat in a Pacific conflict "in a logistically degraded state" within 30 days, with some interceptor systems exhausted after just 2–3 PLA salvoes. With THAAD stocks already diminished by the Twelve-Day War and now further depleted by Epic Fury, and with no new deliveries expected until April 2027, the math is unforgiving. CNAS analysts stated plainly: "Every round expended carries a near-term opportunity cost measured in Pacific readiness."

FPRI's assessment was the most pointed: "Iran is creating a strategic illusion where the tactical bombing campaign continues, but America's readiness for a larger, second-theater contingency quietly bleeds out with every high-end munition fired." Professor Steve Tsang of SOAS raised the scenario explicitly: "Even if the PLA is not fully ready, could Xi attack Taiwan while US precision munitions are potentially depleted? That is possible."

Undersecretary Elbridge Colby pushed back in Senate testimony: "Nobody should get the wrong impression — we're ahead of the problem." But the production data does not support this reassurance. The framework agreements signed in January–February 2026 are directionally correct but temporally irrelevant to the current conflict. Tripling PAC-3 production and quadrupling THAAD output are multi-year endeavors. The interceptors needed for Pacific deterrence in 2026 either exist today or they don't.

## Weeks of high-end weapons, months of gravity bombs, years of rebuilding

The answer to whether munitions constraints force an end to the campaign is nuanced. **The offensive air campaign can continue for months** using the "nearly unlimited" stockpile of JDAMs, laser-guided bombs, and small diameter bombs — weapons that cost $25,000–$100,000 per unit and exist in inventories of tens of thousands. With air supremacy achieved over Iran, this is a viable operational approach.

What cannot continue for months is the **defensive interceptor campaign** protecting US and allied bases across the Gulf. At initial expenditure rates, FPRI projected Arrow interceptors would be fully depleted by approximately March 12, Gulf partner THAAD stocks by March 16, and Patriot supplies under severe stress shortly after. CNN confirmed that at least one Gulf ally was already running low on interceptors by Day 4. Iran's declining attack tempo has provided some relief, but whether this reflects destroyed capacity or strategic conservation remains genuinely uncertain.

The campaign has already adapted. Three key adaptations are extending sustainability: the shift from standoff weapons to gravity bombs (reducing per-strike cost by 95%+); deployment of A-10 Thunderbolts and Apache gunships using gun and rocket systems for the Strait of Hormuz campaign; and the introduction of low-cost LUCAS attack drones as an offensive tool. Trump's March 21 comments suggesting the US is "getting very close to meeting our objectives" may reflect political calculation, operational assessment, or munitions reality — or all three simultaneously.

The rebuilding timeline is measured in years regardless of outcome. Replacing the interceptors consumed in the first four days alone will take **3–8 years at pre-surge production rates**, and the surge targets in the January–February framework agreements won't fully materialize until **2028–2030**. The $200 billion supplemental request signals the administration understands the scale of the problem. But the constraints are not primarily financial — they are physical: sole-source facilities, Chinese-controlled minerals, 24-month manufacturing lead times, and a workforce that "cannot be surged by presidential decree."

## Conclusion

Operation Epic Fury has exposed a structural vulnerability in American military power that decades of procurement choices created. The US built an exquisite, precision-strike military optimized for short, sharp conflicts against technologically inferior adversaries — then entered a sustained campaign against a regional power pursuing a deliberate strategy of attrition. The defense industrial base was designed for peacetime efficiency, not wartime resilience, and the gap between consumption rates and production capacity is not closable during this conflict.

Three findings stand out. First, the campaign is not at immediate risk of collapse — gravity bombs provide months of offensive capability — but the high-end interceptor inventory that enables the entire operation by protecting forward bases is the true limiting factor, and it is critically stressed. Second, Iran's asymmetric cost-exchange strategy is working as designed: cheap drones and missiles are forcing the expenditure of interceptors that cost 100–450 times more and take years to replace. Third, the most dangerous consequence may be invisible: the steady erosion of Pacific deterrence as interceptors, THAAD batteries, and scarce munitions flow from the Indo-Pacific to the Middle East. The question is not whether the US can keep bombing Iran. It is whether the US can afford to keep defending everything else while it does.
