Houthi (Ansar Allah) Autonomous Military Capability and the Persistent Red Sea Threat

Executive Summary

Ansar Allah (the Houthis) represents the most autonomous surviving arm of Iran's Axis of Resistance — but that autonomy is partial, not absolute. Their indigenous weapons capacity covers drone assembly and short-to-medium-range ballistic missiles, while advanced anti-ship cruise missiles, precision guidance electronics, turbojet engines, and radar seekers remain Iranian-sourced. Financially, the Houthis have built a multi-billion-dollar domestic revenue apparatus that could sustain basic operations through Yemen's taxation, port fees, and oil smuggling networks — but the continued flow of advanced weapons technology from Iran is existential for their high-end anti-ship capabilities. The evidence as of March 22, 2026 strongly supports the "strategic reserve" theory: the Houthis have deliberately withheld their Red Sea capability since the Iran war began on February 28, maintaining it as an unspent escalation card. If activated against both Bab el-Mandeb and the already-disrupted Strait of Hormuz simultaneously, the dual-chokepoint scenario threatens roughly one-third of global seaborne crude trade — a disruption the insurance markets are already partially pricing in, with Gulf war risk premiums surging over 1,000%.

1. Indigenous Weapons Production: What the Houthis Can and Cannot Build

Anti-Ship Ballistic Missiles (ASBMs)

The Houthis' most publicized indigenous ASBM is the Muhit, which Iran's IRGC-affiliated Tasnim News Agency confirmed in May 2024 is "patterned after" and "inspired by" Iran's Ghadr intermediate-range ballistic missile. Reuters confirmed that Iran's semi-official media explicitly stated the Ghadr was "made available" to Houthi fighters, and the Muhit is described as a direct derivative fitted with an optical seeker for maritime targeting. The U.S. Army Recognition assessment confirms this transfer, tracing the technology lineage from Iran's Tondar-69 (Chinese Silkworm origin) through the Qadr project, to the Houthi Muhit.[1][2][3][4]

The critical question — whether the Houthis can produce ASBMs truly domestically versus assembling from Iranian-supplied kits — remains contested. The most rigorous open-source assessment, from analyst Hinz and summarized by Yemen Today, concludes that despite "incremental advances in domestic arms production," the Houthis remain "dependent on Iran for key technologies and advanced capabilities". Critically, they lack the capacity to manufacture the small turbojet engines required for cruise missile systems and the active radar-homing seekers used in anti-ship variants. The ASBM program thus appears to be technology transfer and licensed production from Iranian templates rather than independent design.[5]

Drones (UAVs/UCAVs)

The Houthi drone fleet operates on a tiered model of indigenous assembly and dependency:

The UN Panel of Experts' 2020 report — the authoritative baseline — confirmed that "uncrewed aerial vehicles belonging to the Qasef and [Sammad] families are manufactured inside Houthi-held territory using a mix of locally available materials (e.g., fibreglass for the fuselage and wings) and high-value components sourced from abroad". By 2025, Conflict Armament Research discovered that Houthi drones had incorporated Chinese-made hydrogen fuel cells arriving mislabeled as oxygen cylinders, tripling the drones' range while reducing their heat signature — the first use of hydrogen propulsion in unmanned systems by any non-state armed actor globally.[6][7]

Production rates are not publicly confirmed. U.S. and British strikes (Operation Rough Rider, March–May 2025, and a joint UK-US strike targeting drone-manufacturing buildings near Sana'a in April 2025) specifically targeted drone manufacturing facilities. In October 2025, Yemeni government-aligned forces seized 2,500 tons of drone manufacturing equipment at the Port of Aden. Despite these setbacks, the Houthis were assessed to have "retained a large stockpile of drones" as of early March 2026, per their own officials.[8][9][10][11]

Naval Mines

Mine-laying capability is confirmed and operationally proven. The Saudi-led coalition had found and destroyed at least 171 sea mines as of June 2021, including Iranian-made Sadaf mines planted near Bab el-Mandeb and around Hanish Island in the Red Sea. The Stimson Center's 2024 analysis explicitly warned that if the conflict continued, the Houthis might "resort to laying mines to try to close the Bab el-Mandeb". A former cotton processing plant in Zabid, Al Hudaydah, was struck by the U.S. in March 2025 after being identified as having been converted into a mines and explosives factory.[12][13][14]

The mine threat is asymmetrically powerful: the U.S. Navy operates an aging, limited fleet of minesweepers, and the coalition lacks robust mine countermeasure capability in the Red Sea.[12]

Anti-Ship Cruise Missiles (ASCMs)

Anti-ship cruise missiles are assessed to be entirely Iranian-supplied. The U.S. Defense Intelligence Agency confirmed in 2024 that the missile used to attack the Norwegian tanker Strinda in December 2023 was an Iranian Noor ASCM — a reverse-engineered version of China's C-802 using Iran's Tolu-4 turbojet engine — with debris directly matching components displayed by Iran at the 2017 Moscow International Air and Space Salon. The Houthis demonstrably cannot produce the critical turbojet engines or radar-homing seekers necessary for cruise missiles.[15][5]

Conventional Ballistic Missiles (Saudi Arabia / UAE)

The Houthis have long-range ballistic missiles with claimed ranges up to 2,000 km. The Qaher-2 and Qaher-2M variants were converted from Soviet SA-2 (SAM-2) air defense missiles captured from Yemen's pre-war arsenal. Newer systems like the Zulfiqar and Borkan series are assessed to be either direct Iranian transfers or assembled from Iranian-supplied components and technology.[16][4]

2. Supply Chain Independence: The Critical Dependencies

The 90% Problem

The most authoritative single-source estimate comes from the Mokha Center's March 2026 analysis: the Houthis' advanced military capabilities are "nearly 90 percent" dependent on Iranian-supplied weapons, technology, and expertise. This does not mean the Houthis are 90% non-functional without Iran — their ground forces, conventional artillery, and politically consolidated governance structure are largely indigenous. But their anti-ship and precision strike capabilities — the capabilities that make them a global maritime threat — are the segment most exposed to Iranian supply disruption.[17]

Alternative Patrons: Russia and China

Russia has been documented providing satellite intelligence to Houthis via IRGC intermediaries, per the Wall Street Journal's October 2024 reporting. Arms dealer Viktor Bout reportedly arranged a $10 million small arms sale (AK-74s, potentially Kornet anti-tank missiles) in August 2024. In exchange, U.S. Treasury sanctions confirmed that Houthi leader Mohamed Ali Al-Houthi "communicated with officials from Russia and the People's Republic of China to ensure that Houthi militants do not strike Russian or PRC vessels" — a strategic quid pro quo that grants Houthis operational funding while protecting specific vessels.[7]

China has become the more significant non-Iranian technical supplier. Chinese components — sensors, voltage converters, semiconductors — have been found in Iranian drones transferred to Houthis. The hydrogen fuel cell innovation is specifically Chinese technology diverted via obfuscated customs channels. The U.S. Treasury designated Chinese firms in 2025 for supplying machining tools and manufacturing equipment to the Houthis, and an Oman-based logistics company was sanctioned for transferring cruise missile components into Yemen. Beijing and Tehran's 25-year Comprehensive Strategic Partnership, signed in 2021, creates structural channels that can sustain dual-use technology flows even as direct Iranian supply is disrupted.[18][9][7]

What Cannot Be Sourced Elsewhere

Three categories of components remain the critical bottleneck that cannot currently be replicated domestically or sourced from non-Iranian markets without significant effort:

Small turbojet engines for cruise missiles (the Tolu-4 class)

Active radar-homing seekers for anti-ship cruise missiles

Inertial and terminal guidance systems for precision ballistic missiles

The Beiruter's March 2026 analysis puts it directly: "The critical bottleneck is not finished weapons but the components needed to build them: seekers, guidance electronics, and engines. Each requires an import, and every import requires smuggling."[9]

3. Red Sea Interdiction Capability

Operational Record and Attack Intensity

Since November 2023, the Houthis have conducted more than 520 attacks targeting at least 176 ships and carried out 155 strikes on Israeli territory, per ACLED's June 2025 comprehensive report. The U.S. Department of Transportation's maritime safety communication confirmed at least 113 separate attacks on commercial vessels by February 2025, killing 4 mariners and seizing one vessel across 60 affected nations. JINSA tracked at least 683 projectiles fired against maritime targets as of March 2025. At least 4 ships were sunk and 9 mariners killed.[19][20][21][22][23]

Critically, ACLED data reveals that some Houthi attack surges were independent of Gaza ceasefire dynamics: the February 2024 spike coincided with their U.S. terrorist designation; the late May 2024 surge (record weekly high) followed expanded Israeli operations in Rafah. The pattern shows operational decision-making that is responsive to tactical opportunity, not exclusively driven by Tehran's instructions.[19]

Current Threat Assessment (March 2026)

As of March 22, 2026 — three weeks into the Iran war — the Houthis have not conducted significant anti-shipping attacks. Major shipping firms had briefly resumed Suez Canal transits during the October 2025–February 2026 pause. However, Houthi political bureau member Mohammed al-Bukhaiti announced on March 19, 2026 that the group was "weighing the possibility of blocking the Bab al-Mandab Strait to vessels from nations it accuses of aggression". Houthi leader Abdul-Malik al-Houthi stated on the same date that "all options at the military level are possible" and confirmed readiness to act.[24][25][26][27]

The current operational status is best described as a structured pause under tension, with extensive rhetoric, coastal force repositioning, and visible preparation, but not yet resumed attacks.[25]

Can the Houthis Close Bab el-Mandeb?

Analysts are nearly unanimous that the Houthis can functionally deny access to Bab el-Mandeb — but cannot hold it the way Iran physically controls Hormuz through territorial proximity.

The distinction matters: Iran controls the land adjacent to the Strait of Hormuz's 34-km-wide narrows and operates naval forces from those shores. The Houthis, by contrast, must rely on stand-off weapons (missiles, drones, mines, armed speedboats) and do not control the strait's coastline on both sides. Italian security expert Nevola summarizes the operational logic: "Houthi drones, missiles, sea mines, and fast boats could render Bab al-Mandab impassable without them needing to hold territory, much as their 2023–2025 Red Sea campaign forced upwards of 90 percent of ships to seek diversions".[28]

The Houthis' 2023–2025 campaign demonstrated this de facto closure capability: at peak intensity, approximately 90% of commercial traffic that previously transited Bab el-Mandeb rerouted to the Cape of Good Hope. Four ships were sunk; Suez Canal revenue fell approximately $6 billion in 2024. Port of Eilat experienced an 80% revenue collapse before ceasing commercial operations in July 2025.[29][30][23][28]

However, a retired Egyptian army brigadier and strategic analyst Samir Ragheb assessed that a full closure would require the Houthis to breach their existing ceasefire agreement with Washington (brokered in May 2025), "risking devastating retaliation". The Houthis therefore have a range of options below full closure that remain highly disruptive: selective harassment, mine-laying, insurance-market manipulation through threats, and targeted sinkings.[24]

4. The "Strategic Reserve" Theory: Evidence and Assessment

The Evidence For

The circumstantial and direct evidence in favor of deliberate strategic restraint is substantial:

Timeline evidence: In 2025, ACLED recorded 84% fewer Houthi attacks in the Red Sea compared to 2024 — just 7 verified attacks on commercial vessels versus 150 in 2024. This drop preceded the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire and correlates with Operation Rough Rider's degradation of stockpiles. Crucially, the Houthis simultaneously increased strikes on Israeli territory by 120% (125 strikes in the first 11 months of 2025), demonstrating continued operational will while reducing the most escalation-prone behavior.[31]

Explicit statements: Houthi army chief of staff General Yusuf Hassan al-Madani's November 2025 letter to Hamas's Qassam Brigades stated that the group was "closely monitoring developments" and would "reinstate the ban on Israeli navigation in the Red and Arabian Seas" if conditions changed. This language frames maritime interdiction explicitly as a conditional, held-in-reserve option.[22][32]

2026 Iran war behavior: Three weeks into the Iran war, Houthis have not resumed attacks despite announcing their intention to do so on February 28. Stimson Center's March 4, 2026 analysis frames this as "deliberate, conditional restraint — setting the stage for potential military re-engagement". A National News source with knowledge of the discussions offered an explicit framing: "The Iranians are holding off Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping in reserve, as part of strategic patience. It's a potentially major escalation that could be used by Iran at some point in the future".[33][34][24]

Force buildup signals: ICG Yemen analyst Ahmed Nagi reported in March 2026 that the Houthis "appear to be building up their forces by recruiting more fighters, relying on local weapons production and sending reinforcements to Yemen's western coastline on the Red Sea". This preparation posture is inconsistent with a group that has concluded not to act.[35]

The Evidence Against (Complicating Factors)

Stockpile depletion is real: Operation Rough Rider struck more than 1,000 targets from March–May 2025, at a cost exceeding $1 billion. The Pentagon privately admitted to Congress that the Houthi stockpile achieved only "modest" degradation due to hardened, primarily underground storage. However, Houthi officials themselves conceded to the Associated Press that the group's weapons stockpile was "running low after its attacks during the Israel-Hamas war" and that the Iran war had "further impeded the flow of weapons". The Beiruter's March 2026 assessment notes the pipeline that "built the Houthis' arsenal is not functioning" under current Iranian bombardment.[36][37][8][9]

The strategic logic is dual: The Houthis may simultaneously be (a) preserving stockpiles because they are genuinely depleted and (b) awaiting a signal from Tehran or a specific trigger condition. These are not mutually exclusive. The Stimson Center's analysis identifies three factors driving Houthi restraint: IRGC's own calibrated response, back-channel diplomacy, and "a shared desire between the Houthis and the IRGC to conserve arsenal stockpiles for more critical times".[33]

Governance calculus: The Mokha Center's analysis emphasizes that the Houthis face genuine domestic constraints — potential for Operation Rough Rider-style devastation to their infrastructure, Saudi and UAE recalibration against them, and a "threshold of endurance" within their social base already strained by Yemen's economic crisis.[17]

Assessment

The weight of evidence supports the strategic reserve theory as the primary explanation for Houthi restraint since the Iran war began, with stockpile depletion as a secondary constraint. The Houthis are making a strategic calculation: their maritime capability is more valuable unspent than spent — each day of continued global uncertainty about whether Bab el-Mandeb will be closed exerts economic pressure on shipping, insurance markets, and energy prices without the Houthis expending a single missile. Their activation threshold appears tied to either (a) an explicit Iranian signal, (b) perceived existential threat to Iran requiring Houthi intervention, or (c) collapse of the Washington ceasefire arrangement.

5. Financial Independence

Revenue Architecture

The Houthis have constructed one of the most sophisticated war-economy financial architectures of any non-state actor. Multiple converging revenue streams have made them substantially independent from direct Iranian cash transfers for operational expenses — though Iranian oil subsidies remain important:

Oil and commodities: The U.S. Treasury confirmed in January 2026 that the Houthis generate over $2 billion annually through illicit oil sales, with the Iranian government both selling oil to the Houthis and providing a free monthly oil shipment via UAE-based front companies. The UN Panel of Experts documented approximately $4 billion in customs duties on fuel imports collected by Houthis between 2022 and 2024.[38][39][40]

Safe-transit fees (contested): The UN Panel's October 2024 report — the most scrutinized financial finding — reported that the Houthis collected an estimated $180 million per month in illegal safe-transit fees from shipping companies via hawala networks, potentially reaching $2.2 billion annually. Multiple maritime security experts have expressed skepticism about the scale, noting the math requires significant traffic still transiting Houthi-threatened waters. The Houthis flatly denied the charges, calling them "without basis in reality". The UN itself acknowledged it could not independently verify the figure.[41][42][43]

Taxation and seizure: The Houthis levy tariffs of up to 100% on goods imported through ports outside their control, plus a general tax on all imports. They have frozen significant financial assets, including $120 million belonging to Yemenia Airways in August 2024. The latest UN Panel report documents aggressive asset seizure across banking, financial, and commercial sectors.[44][45]

Network diversification: GNET's 2025 analysis characterized the Houthis as running a "multibillion-dollar financial network" through coordinated port fees, smuggling, and hawala systems. The Houthi network includes operations in Yemen, UAE, Oman, China, and the Marshall Islands, per Treasury designations.[46][44]

Financial Sustainability Without Iran

The picture is nuanced. On operational funding for ground forces, governance, and basic weapon purchases, the Houthis are substantially self-funded and could likely sustain these functions without Iranian cash transfers. The Sana'a Center's Q4 2025 economy review confirmed continued revenue extraction through taxation and smuggling despite FTO designation. However, two specific financial dependencies on Iran remain:[45]

Subsidized oil: The free monthly oil shipment and below-market Iranian oil sales reduce the cost of governing northern Yemen and providing subsidized fuel — removing this would increase domestic economic stress.

Advanced weapons costs: The most expensive and precision-capable systems — ASBMs, advanced ASCMs, precision guidance kits — require Iranian supply chains and would need replacement funding beyond what the Houthi tax base can currently provide.

6. Post-Iran Scenarios: Do the Houthis Become More or Less Dangerous?

The Counterintuitive Case for "More Dangerous"

Three dynamics could make the Houthis more unpredictable and independent if Iran is severely weakened:

Structural autonomy increases: The Houthis have always been the most geographically and ideologically independent element of the Axis of Resistance. Unlike Hezbollah, which was created by the IRGC, the Houthi movement predates the relationship with Tehran by decades, rooted in Zaydi Shiite identity and Yemeni political grievance. The Mokha Center notes that the killing of Khamenei created an immediate vacuum in "strategic coordination" formerly managed by Tehran, "pushing the movement toward a form of strategic autonomy". Without centralized Iranian direction, Houthi decision-making becomes less predictable — potentially "more erratic," as the analysis warns.[47][17]

Regional leadership ambitions: A faction within the Houthi leadership has explicitly articulated that "if the Iranian system were to collapse, the Axis of Resistance would still require a new leader — and that Yemen could potentially assume leadership of a reconfigured axis". This suggests not passive adaptation but active aspirational repositioning.[17]

Horizontal network expansion: Houthi operatives have been documented in Iraq and Lebanon, and the UN Panel's 2025 report confirmed unprecedented cooperation with Al-Shabaab in Somalia — including direct communications, weapons smuggling, and training of approximately 400 Somali fighters in Houthi camps. This lateral network building suggests the Houthis are structuring redundant coordination channels that do not rely on IRGC-QF central direction.[48]

The Case for "Less Capable" (Though Still Dangerous)

Advanced capability degradation: With the critical bottleneck in seekers, guidance electronics, and engines (the components that cannot currently be produced domestically or easily sourced from alternative suppliers), sustained loss of Iranian resupply would gradually degrade the Houthis' precision anti-ship and long-range ballistic missile capabilities. The 52-day Operation Rough Rider campaign, which struck 1,000+ targets, achieved "some degradation" but found the group capable of "easily reconstituting" from hardened underground stockpiles. Without restocking, that reconstitution capacity degrades over time.[49][9]

Patron-seeking pressure: AGSI's Gregory Johnsen assessed in March 2026 that Houthis unable to demonstrate loyalty to Iran in its "hour of need" may find themselves "even more isolated in the future: weak and without a patron". This suggests the Houthis face a strategic dilemma where both action and inaction carry existential risks, a scenario that incentivizes risky behavior.[50]

Russia and China as partial replacements: The existing arrangements — Russia providing intelligence and small arms, China providing dual-use components and vessel protection deals — suggest a potential partial patron replacement. Both states benefit from having a proxy capable of disrupting global maritime commerce without bearing direct attribution costs. However, neither Russia nor China can replicate the depth of Iranian weapons technology transfer, at least on a near-term basis.

The Persistent Threat Assessment

Stimson's February 2026 analysis captures the essential point: "Rebuilding Iran's proxy network is going to be challenging given the structural shifts that have eroded its foundations". But the same analysis notes the Houthis emerged from the Gaza war as "the only faction perceived as relatively successful" — the last proxy standing with operational capability and territorial control. This gives them unique leverage in post-Iran proxy network reconstitution.[51][52]

The Houthis are most accurately assessed as a persistent, degradable but not eliminable maritime threat on the following basis:

Ground control of Yemen's northern population centers is durable and not dependent on Iran

Financial base is substantially autonomous for operational costs

Drone assembly capability is indigenous and expandable with Chinese components

Advanced cruise missile capability degrades without resupply, but slowly given underground stockpiles

Strategic geography (adjacency to Bab el-Mandeb) is permanent and cannot be degraded by military strikes

7. The Dual-Chokepoint Scenario: Feasibility and Market Implications

The Arithmetic of Global Disruption

The numbers establishing the severity of the dual-chokepoint scenario are well-established and constitute the single most consequential supply shock in global energy markets since at least the 1973 oil embargo:

Strait of Hormuz: 20–21 million barrels per day (bpd) of crude oil and petroleum products — approximately 20% of world consumption and roughly one-third of global seaborne oil trade[53]

Bab el-Mandeb: 6–7 million bpd of crude and refined products, plus significant LNG volumes moving from the Gulf to Europe[53]

Combined disruption: Approximately one-third of global seaborne crude trade simultaneously compromised[54]

Saudi bypass constraint: Saudi Aramco has activated its East-West pipeline to Yanbu to circumvent Hormuz, but those shipments then transit Bab el-Mandeb — a dual-chokepoint that converts the bypass route into a second risk vector. Analysts estimate 7 million bpd of Saudi exports via Yanbu could be at risk if Houthis target that terminal[55][56]

JP Morgan assessed that disruptions could remove up to 12 million bpd from global markets. Oxford Economics flagged potential Brent crude prices of $150–200+ as the level that "breaks parts of the economy" in severe scenarios.[57][53]

Thomson Reuters' March 2026 analysis directly characterized the current situation: "With the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut and the Houthis resuming attacks on the Suez/Bab el-Mandeb corridor, roughly one-third of global seaborne crude trade is compromised simultaneously. All five major container lines have suspended Hormuz transits".[54]

Insurance and Shipping Market Implications

Insurance market pricing captures the trajectory clearly:

Hormuz corridor (current war): War risk premiums for Gulf shipping surged over 1,000% following the February 28 Iran war commencement. With tankers valued at $200–300 million, a 3% war risk rate implies $7.5 million per voyage for hull coverage alone. Cargo war risk rates have moved in parallel.[58]

Red Sea (2025 reactivation): In July 2025, when Houthi attacks resumed after a ceasefire period, war risk premiums for Red Sea transits spiked to approximately 1% of hull value — meaning a $100 million vessel incurs $1 million in additional insurance costs per seven-day transit. By late 2025/early 2026 the rate reached 0.7%–1.0%.[59][60]

Route economics: The insurance premium differential makes Cape of Good Hope routing economically rational: Asia-Europe via Suez incurred ~$85,000 in war risk premiums (467% year-over-year increase) versus ~$12,000 via Cape — but add 10–14 days transit time and approximately $1 million in extra fuel costs, creating roughly $1 million in additional costs regardless of route. When both chokepoints are active, the Cape route loses its insurance advantage but retains the fuel cost penalty, stranding the global shipping market between equivalently expensive options.[60]

Market structure implications: Full normalization is expected to take 18–24 months after security stabilization — meaning even a rapid resolution of the Iran war and Houthi restraint would leave elevated baseline premiums for at least 2027–2028. Industry-wide hull and machinery premiums have already risen 15–25% regardless of route.[60]

Is Full Dual-Closure Realistic?

The dual-chokepoint nightmare scenario is credible but not currently actualized. As of March 22, 2026:

The Strait of Hormuz is effectively closed through a combination of Iran's declared interdiction, insurance withdrawal, and risk perception — operating more through commercial self-deterrence than physical blockade[54]

Bab el-Mandeb is threatened but not yet interdicted, with Houthi rhetoric and visible military preparation but no confirmed major attacks since the Iran war began[25][24]

The strategic expert consensus (New Arab March 2026) is that the Houthis "retain capacities to close Bab al-Mandab" through functional access denial, but lack the geographic territorial control that Iran exercises over Hormuz. A complete, sustained physical closure of Bab el-Mandeb would require higher operational tempo than the 2023–2025 campaign and is likely to trigger devastating military response under current circumstances.[28]

The more likely near-term scenario is partial Bab el-Mandeb interdiction: selective targeting of tankers linked to designated countries, elevated mine threat, and insurance market manipulation — sufficient to keep premium costs elevated and traffic rerouted, without triggering the full-scale military response that a genuine closure would invite.

Confirmed vs. Projected Capabilities: An Analytical Scorecard

Conclusion: Persistent Threat, Conditional Capability

The Houthis represent a persistent but conditionally capable maritime threat to Red Sea commerce — one that does not require Iranian survival to remain dangerous but does require Iranian supply to remain at peak effectiveness. The specific conclusion for each element of the user's core research question:

Post-Iran viability: The Houthis will survive Iranian regime collapse with their governance, ground forces, and basic drone production intact. Their precision anti-ship capabilities — particularly cruise missiles and advanced ASBMs — will degrade progressively over months to years without resupply. Russian intelligence support and Chinese dual-use component channels provide partial (not full) substitution.

Strategic reserve: The evidence strongly supports deliberate withholding of the Red Sea escalation option as of March 22, 2026. The Houthis have publicly announced readiness, repositioned coastal forces, and are clearly awaiting a trigger condition. The most plausible triggers are: (a) Iranian request as their war position becomes more desperate, (b) Israeli or U.S. strike on Houthi leadership directly, or (c) collapse of the October 2025 Washington ceasefire arrangement.

Second chokepoint permanence: Even under an Iran ceasefire or regime transition, the Houthis retain the geographic, military, and financial architecture to threaten Bab el-Mandeb indefinitely. They have demonstrated the capability, built the financial independence, and — critically — learned that the strategic leverage from threatening closure is itself economically powerful, regardless of whether attacks actually occur. The threat alone has demonstrated the ability to redirect 90% of Red Sea traffic.

The asymmetric cost equation: As one analyst noted, their drones and missiles are cheap while Western interceptions and naval deployments are far more expensive. Operation Rough Rider cost the U.S. over $1 billion and expended irreplaceable Tomahawk stockpiles, while achieving only "some degradation" of Houthi capability. This cost asymmetry is the structural foundation of the Houthi maritime threat — and it does not require Iranian support to persist.[61][62][63][37][49]
