# Precision Munitions in the 2026 Iran War: Burn Rates and Constraints  

The US-Israel “Operation Epic Fury” (Feb 28, 2026–present) has seen unprecedented PGM and interceptor use. In the first 36–96 hours, coalition forces expended thousands of weapons. For example, US/Israeli forces fired roughly **5,197 munitions in 96 hours** (~$10–16 B worth)【74†L1-L4】.  This included on the order of 200–300 JDAM‐guided bombs, ~375 Tomahawk cruise missiles, and hundreds of interceptors in the first four days【1†L1-L1】【81†L311-L319】. Specifically:  
- **JDAMs:** US forces dropped ~210 JDAM kit-guided bombs in the first 36 h (~140/day)【1†L1-L1】.  (US inventory is tens of thousands of tailkits; Boeing can produce on the order of 35–40k kits/year if needed【61†L110-L118】.)  
- **Tomahawk missiles:** The US launched roughly 375 Tomahawks in 96 h (~94/day)【81†L311-L319】. (At 85/yr production, replacing 375 missiles takes ~53 months【81†L311-L319】.)  
- **THAAD interceptors:** US and Gulf batteries fired dozens of THAAD rounds (the FPRI analysis implies Gulf THAAD would run out in ~12 days at that rate)【78†L293-L300】. (Current THAAD production is only ~96 per year【65†L126-L131】.)  
- **Arrow (Israel):** Israel reportedly launched on the order of 40 Arrow interceptors in the first 36 h (~27/day)【1†L1-L1】. FPRI estimates Israel’s Arrow stock was over half expended in 4 days, with only ~8 days of current burn rate remaining【78†L293-L300】. Replenishment will take ~2–3 years at current production (IAI/Boeing)【16†L0-L0】【78†L293-L300】.  
- **Iron Dome (Tamir):** Israel fired many Tamir interceptors (about 70 in 36 h【1†L1-L1】, ~47/day); its inventories are reportedly ~50,000 units, enough for ~3 months at that pace【15†L231-L239】. (Production by Rafael is steady but limited.)  
- **Patriot PAC-3:** US/Gulf forces combined launched ~943 Patriots in 96 h【81†L311-L319】 (≈236/day), roughly 18 months’ worth of production at 620/yr【81†L311-L319】.  Gulf partners alone fired almost twice as many Patriots as the US【81†L326-L334】, straining a single Lockheed/Boeing line. Under FMS rules, Gulf reload orders will lag US orders by ~6–12 months【81†L326-L334】.  
- **Small Diameter Bomb (GBU-39 SDB):** Usage has been light so far. FPRI shows ~30,000 SDBs in US stock (enough for ~750 days at current burn【15†L231-L239】), and production was ~7,000/yr pre-war【61†L117-L125】.  
- **JASSM/JASSM-ER (AGM-158):** Roughly 600 are built per year【15†L231-L239】. FPRI projects current usage would deplete stocks in ~104 days at high-intensity rates【15†L231-L239】, but daily firing rates have been far lower than launch stock.  
- **Other PGMs:** Israel used hundreds of its own PGMs: e.g. ~280 Spice stand-in bombs and 70‑100 Rampage/Delilah missiles in day 1【1†L1-L1】. US forces also used dozens of Harpoon, HARM, and cruise variants, but these remain “healthy” stocks by FPRI analysis【15†L231-L239】.  

## Production Capacity and Bottlenecks  
Key munitions are produced by major defense firms, but annual outputs are limited:  

- **JDAM kits (Boeing):** Boeing builds JDAM guidance kits in Missouri.  In 2019–20 the USAF bought ~30–37k kits/yr【61†L110-L118】, and Boeing has said it can ramp to ~36,500 kits/yr (~150 kits/day)【72†L1-L7】.  Production is constrained by component supply (GPS guidance, actuators) but no single rare material bottleneck is known.  
- **Tomahawk (Lockheed Martin/RTX):** Lockheed Martin (after 2019) produces the Tomahawk TLAM Block IV/V.  Under a new RTX multi-year deal, annual Tomahawk production will grow to **>1,000 per year**【69†L86-L94】 (roughly 2–4× current rate).  A critical bottleneck is the Williams F107 turbofan engine, sole-source for Tomahawk, JASSM, and LRASM【81†L321-L324】; engine output cannot be easily sped up.  
- **THAAD (Lockheed Martin):** Lockheed builds THAAD interceptors at roughly **96/year** globally【65†L126-L131】.  A recent Pentagon contract aims to raise this to ~400/yr by late 2020s【65†L126-L131】.  THAAD production is limited by the solid-fuel rocket motor (AMR), for which the Ampac facility is the sole US source of ammonium perchlorate propellant【81†L407-L414】.  
- **Arrow (IAI/Boeing):** Israel Aerospace Industries co-produces Arrow interceptors.  Annual production figures are not public, but pre-war stocks were small; Arrow 2/3 inventories were reportedly cut by ~50% in June 2025.  Israel has **ramped Arrow output** (new contracts in 2025)【63†L91-L100】, but replacing lost Arrow missiles still takes many months.  Russian rare-earth bans (e.g. on dysprosium) threaten seeker production.  
- **Iron Dome (Rafael Defense Systems):** Rafael produces Tamir interceptors at a steady but limited rate. Israel’s stock of Tamirs (~50,000) was amassed over years; the recent US focus on counter-drones has accelerated Rafael’s production and plans for the new Iron Beam laser. No open figures on annual Tamir production, but FPRI estimates ~89 days of supply at high burn【15†L231-L239】.  
- **Patriot PAC-3 (Lockheed/Boeing):** Lockheed/Boeing produce Patriot PAC-3 MSE interceptors at ~620/yr【81†L311-L319】.  In early 2026, a 7-year USAF multiyear deal was announced to ramp PAC-3 to ~2,000/yr by 2030【65†L126-L131】.  The main bottleneck is Boeing’s seeker assembly line【81†L311-L319】.  
- **Small Diameter Bomb (Boeing):** Boeing also makes the GBU-39 SDB (250‑lb) with wings, about 7,000 units/yr as of 2020【61†L117-L125】.  Its production was previously scaled for counter-ISIS wars; in budget plans SDB buys were cut back. Currently, no US supply crunch is reported for SDBs.  
- **AGM-158 JASSM/ER (Lockheed Martin):** Lockheed’s Troy, AL plant produces ~600 JASSMs per year【15†L231-L239】.  The US/NATO line (originally USAF and Poland) meets current demand; Lockheed is investing in additional lines (e.g. a second Troy line)【61†L165-L169】.  The F107 engine is the same bottleneck as Tomahawk.  
- **Other interceptors:**  US Navy Aegis interceptors (SM-2/3/6) are built at modest rates (SM-3IIA ~60/yr).  These will be reprioritized but cannot surge quickly.  Similarly, Ukraine/US legacy missiles (HARM, Hellfire, etc.) had ample inventory by early 2026【15†L231-L239】.  

Critical production bottlenecks include rare minerals and single facilities.  For example, **gallium, neodymium, dysprosium** (in missile seekers and electronics) are >90% produced in China【81†L378-L387】.  High-explosive filling (RDX/HMX) flows through the sole Holston Army plant【81†L416-L422】.  Solid propellant oxidizer (ammonium perchlorate) is made only at Ampac–Utah【81†L407-L414】; ~600 tons (6.7% of US annual output) would be needed to replace 96h of war rockets【81†L399-L404】【81†L407-L414】. 

## Stockpiles and Usage to Date  
**Pre-war stocks:** Exact inventories are classified, but open sources and FPRI provide estimates.  The US Air Force had ~115,000 JDAM kits, ~30,000 SDBs, ~20,000 Hellfires, etc.【15†L231-L239】.  The Navy had ~2,500 Tomahawks (implied by FPRI: 375 is ~12% of stock【16†L25-L30】).  US THAAD inventories were ~400 interceptors (48 per battery × ~9 batteries)【28†L220-L228】. Israel’s stocks included ~10,000 Spice/GBU-39 equivalents, ~600 Arrow interceptors, ~50,000 Iron Dome Tamirs【15†L231-L239】.  

**Used in prior conflicts:** In the June 2025 “Twelve-Day War,” the US used ~150 THAAD missiles (~25% of US THAAD stock)【28†L220-L228】 and over 200 Tomahawks.  Israel expended thousands of its own bombs/drones. These burns were already straining inventories.  

**Expended so far (Feb–Mar 2026):**  By March 2026, FPRI estimates ~53% of Israel’s Arrow interceptors and ~32% of partner THAADs have been fired【16†L0-L0】.  The Gulf partners have used ~15% of their PAC-3s, the US ~13%【16†L25-L30】.  About 11–12% of Tomahawks and Spice bombs have been shot【16†L25-L30】.  JDAM usage was lower relative to stock (tens of thousands unused).  Overall, FPRI’s “days-to-exhaustion” analysis suggests Arrow/ATACMS/GBU-57 are critically low (days to run out), while JDAM, Hellfire, SDB remain plentiful【15†L231-L239】【78†L255-L263】.  

## Cost Asymmetry: Interceptors vs Drones  
Iran’s cheap drones force a heavy cost burden on US defenses. A Shahed-136 kamikaze drone costs only **$20,000–$50,000**【58†L45-L53】.  In contrast, intercepting them with high-end missiles is hugely expensive: a Patriot PAC-3 interceptor costs ~$4 million【31†L221-L229】, and a THAAD interceptor ~$12–15 million【31†L232-L236】. 【77†embed_image】In effect, each US missile (~$4–$13M) must shoot down a drone worth ~$20–50k【58†L45-L53】【31†L232-L236】. For example, Iran launched ~1,000 such drones in the first week (an order of $20–50M worth of drones)【58†L45-L53】, which was countered by <$40M worth of Patriots.  This ~100× cost ratio means even sustaining **100 drone attacks per day** could burn $400M–$1.3B/day in interceptors, far outpacing Tehran’s expenditures (even if Tehran budgets only ~$3–5M/day on drones).  Such asymmetric attrition accelerates US stock depletion.  

## Surge Production and Allied Aid  
**US ramp-up:**  The Pentagon has activated multiyear contracts to expand capacity.  In Jan 2026 the US signed a 7-year framework with Lockheed to raise Patriot output from 600→2,000/yr and THAAD from 96→400/yr by decade’s end【65†L126-L131】.  In Feb 2026 Raytheon/RTX agreed to boost AMRAAM, Tomahawk, and Standard Missile production 2–4× (raising Tomahawks to >1,000/yr, AMRAAMs to ~1,900/yr, SM-6 to >500/yr)【69†L86-L94】.  The US also invested $1 B in L3Harris’s solid rocket motor (AMPAC) plant to secure propellant【65†L142-L147】.  These actions will pay off over **months to years**, but cannot instantly refill stocks.  The workforce is already a limiting factor (shortage of welders and assemblers)【81†L332-L340】.  

**Allied contributions:** European and Gulf allies are providing material support.  Germany’s Rheinmetall is **accelerating missile factories** (new Unterlüß and Spain plants due Q1 2027) in response to this war【33†L213-L221】.  France has dispatched SAM/PAC-3 kits and other air-defense equipment to Gulf partners【39†L98-L100】.  The UK sent Typhoon jets and an Aegis destroyer, Italy is sending SAMP/T batteries to Cyprus and Gulf【40†L158-L162】.  Qatar and UAE are transferring their US-supplied interceptors and missiles to the front.  Even so, allied stocks are limited, and some (e.g. 8 Aegis SM-3s) were already depleted by Iran’s earlier missile attacks【28†L220-L228】.  

## If Stocks Run Low: Shifts and Consequences  
**Weapon mix shift:**  Pentagon leaders acknowledge switching tactics.  Gen. Caine and Sec. Hegseth noted US strikes will shift from high-end standoff weapons to “less exquisite” GPS-guided bombs (JDAMs) once Iranian air defenses are largely gone【65†L71-L79】.  In practice, this means more unsighted gravity bombs (and smaller guided munitions like the LUCAS drones at ~$35k each【24†L83-L92】) instead of million-dollar missiles.  Indeed, the Air Force has deployed large numbers of **LUCAS** loitering munitions (US-made kamikaze drones) as a lower-cost alternative【24†L83-L92】.  Such a shift increases the risk of civilian casualties, as unguided/area-weapons are used more.  

**Operational impact:**  Exhausting precision stocks would slow operations.  Without interceptors, bases and ships face greater risk from Iranian missiles.  Without Tomahawks/JASSMs, striking deep Iranian targets requires manned bombers (or riskier tactics).  The strain is already evident: after initial high-intensity strikes, Iranian missile/drone launches have fallen ~90%【80†L171-L180】, reflecting a sort of temporary pause by Iran.  But Coalition leaders stress they can “take all the time needed”【53†L99-L104】.  In reality, if PGM stocks dip below usable levels, political pressure will mount for a ceasefire.  Defense Sec. Hegseth *claims* “no shortage” of munitions【65†L71-L79】, but acknowledged he’s using cheaper “stand-in” weapons now【65†L71-L79】.  (In fact, staff reported Hegseth said the US is switching to smaller bombs due to inventory limits【65†L71-L79】.) Historical precedent suggests that once precision stocks dwindle, operations either slow or rely on less discriminate means, increasing collateral damage and political cost.  

## Supply-Chain and Resource Vulnerabilities  
Modern weapons rely on complex supply chains and scarce inputs.  The FPRI analysis emphasizes: **You cannot just “print” missiles** – you need rare minerals. Replacing the 5,197 munitions used in 96 h requires dozens of tons of copper, neodymium, tantalum, etc.【81†L399-L404】.  Crucially, **ammonium perchlorate** (solid propellant oxidizer) is a single-point-of-failure: 600 tons would be needed to refuel all rockets, but only one US plant (AMPAC, Utah) makes it【81†L407-L414】.  Similarly, all US RDX/HMX explosives come from one WWII-era plant (Holston, TN)【81†L416-L422】.  Many electronics use Chinese-controlled elements: China provides ~98% of global gallium, 90% of neodymium, 99% of dysprosium production【81†L378-L387】, so Western missile seeker makers lack substitute sources.  These bottlenecks mean even with unlimited funding, munition output can only scale as fast as these inputs.  

**Summary:**  In sum, current reporting indicates that if the Iran campaign remains at peak intensity, several key munitions (Arrow, THAAD, ATACMS, GBU-57) could run out in **weeks** without resupply【15†L231-L239】【78†L255-L263】.  Less critical inventories (JDAM, Hellfire, SDB) would last months or years【15†L231-L239】.  The Pentagon claims stocks suffice “as long as needed”【43†L270-L276】, but independent analyses warn that *industrial* constraints (production rates, minerals, workforce) will force either a lull or a shift to simpler weapons within months.  Allied surge efforts and emergency contracts will help, but new production plants take ~1–2 years to start.  Therefore, absent a negotiated pause or massive, sustained ramp-up (via DPA orders, emergency funding, allied manufacturing), the campaign will likely be unsustainable at full intensity beyond a few months.
