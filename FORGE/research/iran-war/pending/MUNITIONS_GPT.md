Munitions Exhaustion in the 2026 Iran War: Physical Constraints on Campaign Duration

Analysis current as of March 22, 2026 | Sources: CSIS, JINSA, War on the Rocks, Defense News, Breaking Defense, RTX/Raytheon, Lockheed Martin, U.S. DoW budget documents

Executive Summary

Operation Epic Fury (begun February 28, 2026) has consumed military stockpiles at rates that structurally exceed current production capacity for the most critical weapons categories. The campaign's opening days expended more than 3,000 precision-guided munitions and interceptors in the first 36 hours alone — approximately $5.6 billion worth of weaponry — forcing a publicly acknowledged tactical pivot by Day 4 from expensive standoff missiles (Tomahawks, JASSMs) to cheaper GPS-guided gravity bombs (JDAMs). By Day 12, CSIS estimated total war costs at $16.5 billion.[1][2][3][4][5]

The hard constraint is not uniform across all weapons categories. The honest answer to "how long can this last" depends entirely on which munitions bin you are examining:

JDAM-guided gravity bombs: Effectively unlimited. Hegseth's "nearly unlimited" claim has genuine analytical basis — stockpiles number in the hundreds of thousands and production can surge rapidly.[6][3]

Tomahawks, JASSMs, SM-3s: Meaningfully constrained. 319 Tomahawks fired represents ~10% of total US inventory; replacements take two years to produce at rates of 90/year.[7][8]

THAAD interceptors: The most acute single vulnerability. The US entered this war already depleted from June 2025, has had no new deliveries since August 2023, and won't receive new units until April 2027.[9][3]

Campaign sustainability conclusion (CSIS): The US does not face immediate inventory crisis for the current war as fought (with gravity bombs), but reduced inventories create severe risks to other theaters — Ukraine and the Western Pacific — and to the US ability to enter future conflicts.[3]

1. Consumption Data: What Has Been Fired

Opening Salvo (First 36–100 Hours)

The campaign opened with the most resource-intensive phase. In the first 36 hours, more than 3,000 precision-guided munitions and interceptors were consumed across the US-Israeli coalition. During the first 100 hours, U.S. forces fired an estimated 168 Tomahawk cruise missiles — a figure CSIS described as "a massive expenditure" that would leave the Navy "feeling this expenditure for several years." Within the first six days, 319 Tomahawks had been launched against Iranian targets, representing approximately 10% of the total US Tomahawk inventory.[10][1][7]

Iran launched more than 2,000 one-way attack drones and more than 500 ballistic missiles in the opening days of the conflict, requiring a corresponding massive defensive expenditure of interceptors by US and allied forces. Admiral Brad Cooper confirmed on March 3 that Iran had launched over 2,000 drones and 500 ballistic missiles in fewer than 100 hours. By Day 5, Iranian ballistic missile launches had declined approximately 90% and drone attacks by 83%, the result of coalition destruction of launchers and production infrastructure.[11][3]

The Munitions Transition: Day 4–5

By the fourth day of the campaign, Defense Secretary Hegseth and CJCS General Caine publicly announced what CSIS calls the "point of munitions transition" — a shift from expensive standoff weapons (Tomahawks at ~$3.5M each, JASSMs) to GPS/laser-guided gravity bombs (JDAM kits at ~$34,500–$80,000 each). By Day 14, only 1% of munitions being used were standoff weapons.[12][3]

The Pentagon framed this transition as a product of tactical success — air dominance established, Iranian air defenses destroyed — making it safe to fly bombers closer to targets. The Washington Post reported simultaneously that the US was "rapidly running through" its precision munitions stocks. Both narratives are probably partially true: the tactical rationale is genuine, and the cost constraint reinforced the decision. This is the "smaller bombs" shift referenced in your query.[5][11]

2. Production Rates and Manufacturers

Tomahawk (RTX/Raytheon)

The Tomahawk is the single most consequential constraint in terms of strategic signaling. The missile's current production rate hovers at approximately 90 per year — the "minimum sustainment rate" at which Raytheon can keep the production line open. FY2025 Navy procurement was only 72 missiles; FY2026 dropped to 57 in base budget requests, though CSIS notes 190 are slated for delivery in FY2026 when including other service branches. At 90/year production against 319 fired in 6 days, the consumption rate exceeds production by roughly 200:1. RTX signed a new framework agreement in February 2026 — three weeks before the war started — to scale annual production to over 1,000 missiles/year. However, Tomahawks take up to two years to produce from order to delivery, meaning no new missiles are forthcoming until at minimum early 2028.[14][8][15][16][3]

THAAD (Lockheed Martin)

THAAD is the most acute structural vulnerability in the US inventory. Key production facts:

Current production rate: 96 interceptors/year[17]

Contracted surge target: 400/year over seven years (framework agreement January 2026)[18][17]

Time to achieve 400/year: Seven years (by ~2033)[17]

Unit cost: ~$15 million per interceptor[3]

Last US delivery: August 2023[3]

Next scheduled US delivery: April 2027[3]

Pipeline: 100 interceptors procured but not yet delivered; production has been directed to Saudi Arabia and UAE foreign orders[19]

Lockheed has broken ground on a new Munitions Acceleration Center in Camden, Arkansas to support the ramp-up, but this facility is "only just broken ground" as of March 2026.[20]

Patriot PAC-3 MSE (Lockheed Martin / Boeing seeker / L3Harris motor)

PAC-3 is the healthiest interceptor category by relative measure. Key production facts:

2025 production: 620 units (a 20% increase from 2024; 60% increase over two years)[21][22]

Contracted surge target: 2,000/year by ~2030 (seven-year framework agreement, January 2026)[23]

FY2026 deliveries: 172 expected to US forces[3]

Pre-war US inventory: ~2,000 total (1,600 PAC-3 MSE, remainder older variants)[3]

Largest ever Patriot order: $9.8 billion contract (September 2025) for 1,970 missiles over FY2024-FY2026[24]

Production capacity: Lockheed has sufficient floor space in Camden for final assembly; expansion relies on workforce and automated tooling, not new factories[24]

SM-3 (Raytheon/RTX)

Cumulative delivered to US by end 2025: ~506 interceptors[9]

FY2026 deliveries: 66 expected[9]

June 2025 expenditure: ~80 interceptors (~20% of full inventory)[9]

Cost escalation: Unit cost rose from ~$9M (FY2021) to ~$24M (FY2024 supplemental), reflecting supply chain disruption[9]

RTX agreement: Production increase for SM-3 IIA included in February 2026 framework deal[16]

JASSM / JASSM-ER (Lockheed Martin)

US inventory: ~3,500 missiles[3]

Current procurement: 550/year at maximum rate; USAF plans to scale to 810/year[25]

Production expansion: Second Lockheed production facility opening to double JASSM/LRASM output[25]

Lead time: ~2 years order-to-delivery[26]

Usage note: Used heavily in opening standoff phase; second-largest cost driver in early war after Tomahawks

JDAM (Boeing)

Unit cost: ~$34,500–$80,000 per kit depending on quantity[27]

FY2026 US procurement: 2,300 kits at $187.5M[6]

Surge capacity: Boeing's St. Louis facility can produce up to 36,500 kits/year (150/day at peak)[28]

Peak production: 30,872 units in 2019; capacity is not the binding constraint[29]

Strategic assessment: JDAMs are genuinely nearly unlimited in availability. The guidance kit can be attached to any unguided bomb in the US stockpile, which numbers in the hundreds of thousands.

Arrow-3 (Israel Aerospace Industries / Boeing)

June 2025 expenditure: ~34 Arrow-3 interceptors[30]

Unit cost: ~$2–3 million[31]

Production time: "A few months" (exact figure classified)[31]

December 2025 expansion deal: Israel signed a "billions of shekels" contract with IAI to expand Arrow-3 production[31]

IDF assessment (March 14, 2026): No interceptor shortage; prepared for prolonged conflict[32]

Israeli emergency procurement: NIS 2.6 billion (~$826M) approved March 14 for urgent defense procurement[32]

Iron Dome / Tamir (Rafael / R2S joint venture)

Unit cost: ~$40,000–$50,000 per Tamir interceptor[33]

New US production facility: R2S (Raytheon-Rafael joint venture) opened Camden, Arkansas facility November 2025[34]

New production contract: $1.25 billion Tamir supply contract awarded November 2025[35]

Target customer: Israel and US Marine Corps (SkyHunter variant)[35]

Role in current conflict: Used against Iranian short-range rockets and Hezbollah attacks; less relevant for Gulf defense than Patriot

GBU-57 MOP Bunker Buster (Boeing)

Inventory (classified): Fewer than 6 estimated remaining after June 2025 Midnight Hammer operation consumed 14 of an estimated ~20 in inventory[36]

Production rate: ~24/year (2/month); Oklahoma facility was expanded in July 2024[37]

Restocking contract: $100M contract signed February 2026 for replacement units; deliveries projected to start January 2028[38]

Constraint: Only B-2 bombers can carry this weapon; sole-source contract with Boeing[38]

3. Stockpile Estimates: Pre-War, Twelve-Day War Depletion, and Current Status

The Two-Conflict Depletion Problem

The 2026 Iran war occurs against a backdrop of already-depleted THAAD and SM-3 inventories from the June 2025 Twelve-Day War. This "compound depletion" is the core structural problem:

June 2025 Twelve-Day War expenditure:

THAAD: More than 150 interceptors (WSJ/CNN) or ~14% of stockpile (JINSA)[39][40][13]

SM-3: ~80 interceptors fired from Navy ships[39]

Patriot: ~30 at Al Udeid Air Base[41]

Arrow-3: ~34 (nearly double any prior use)[30]

THAAD inventory trajectory:

Pre-June 2025: 534 interceptors[19][9]

Post-June 2025: Approximately 384–434 (depending on whether 100 or 150 were fired)

Since June 2025: ~50 additional produced in 8 months at current 96/year rate[20]

March 2026 estimate: Approximately 430–490 interceptors available to US forces[20]

Complicating factor: 100-interceptor delivery backlog is contractually owed and production from late 2023–present has been directed to Saudi Arabia's foreign sale of 360 interceptors[19]

CSIS notes that if 150–250 THAAD interceptors are fired in the opening days of Epic Fury, the US could be left with insufficient interceptors to fully arm all of its eight THAAD batteries. A January 2026 Heritage Foundation report warned that high-end interceptors including THAAD would "likely be exhausted within days of sustained combat" in a major power scenario.[42][9]

4. The Asymmetric Attrition Problem: Cost Exchange Ratios

Iran's strategic use of Shahed-136 drones against Gulf targets represents a deliberate "asymmetric war of attrition focused on exhausting U.S., Israeli, and allied defensive resources," as the Soufan Center characterized it. The economics are stark:[1]

Shahed-136 specifications:

Cost: $20,000–$50,000 per drone (common estimate: ~$35,000)[44][45]

Range: ~1,000–1,200 miles[44]

Speed: ~120 mph; audible, trackable

Warhead: 50–90 kg

Production: Iran has dispersed manufacturing; RUSI expert notes underground production facilities built before the war; Houthi precedent suggests production under bombardment is feasible[46]

The Stimson Center calculated that for every $1 Iran spends manufacturing a Shahed drone, the UAE spends approximately $20–$28 to intercept it. The NYT cited a drone industry executive estimating the cost ratio at "10:1 to 60–70:1 in favor of Iran."[50][48]

Iran's production capacity: Bloomberg estimated that Iran had already launched over 2,100 Shaheds since the conflict began as of March 10. Despite US/Israeli strikes on Iranian drone factories, RUSI analysts note Iran likely pre-positioned underground manufacturing infrastructure. Iran's defense ministry explicitly stated on March 3 that it "has not yet deployed all its advanced weapons."[51][46]

The adaptation response: By March 2026, US forces began deploying the LUCAS drone (a reverse-engineered Shahed clone at ~$35,000 per unit) in combat for the first time, and using aircraft with Sidewinder missiles (~$4/shot against a drone) as a cost-effective alternative to Patriot intercepts. The UK is deploying Martlet Lightweight Multirole Missiles (~$20,000 range) from Wildcat helicopters for a similar cost-exchange improvement.[52][53][47]

Why Announced Rates Are Not Operational Rates

A critical analytical distinction: every announced "surge" is a framework agreement requiring congressional appropriations, sub-supplier buildout, and multi-year facility construction. War on the Rocks' January 2026 analysis — published one month before the war — made the key structural argument: "The primes aren't the real bottleneck in U.S. weapons production." The actual constraints lie in sub-tier suppliers that serve multiple programs simultaneously, including:[54]

Solid Rocket Motors (SRMs): The single most acute systemic bottleneck. The DoW's December 2025 investment acknowledgment was blunt: "The surge in demand for propellant-based weaponry, coupled with a narrow supplier base, has created a bottleneck in SRM production." Both domestic SRM suppliers — Northrop and L3Harris — are "tethered to a handful of shared suppliers for essential components." A disruption at any of these shared companies "would simultaneously cripple the production capacity of the entire solid rocket motor enterprise." The reconciliation bill included $200M for the SRM industrial base and $400M for emerging SRM makers, but new supplier capacity takes years to build. The DoW signed a $1B equity partnership with L3Harris's Missile Solutions division in January 2026 for SRM expansion.[55][56][57]

Microelectronics and Guidance Systems: Long-lead-time integrated circuits, navigation chips, and sensors are often sole-sourced or procured from a single approved supplier. JDAM upgrades require GPS receivers; Tomahawks require TERCOM guidance systems and turbofan engines that are, as 19FortyFive noted, "semiautonomous aircraft" more than simple bombs.[7][54]

Critical Minerals: China controls 95% of global heavy rare earth output, and the US imports almost 100% of what it uses, with 90% of that sourced from China. China's December 2024 ban on exports of gallium, germanium, and antimony to the US — minerals critical for semiconductors in guidance systems, night-vision optics, and radar components — was suspended in November 2025 following the Trump-Xi meeting in South Korea, but the suspension expires November 2026 and military-end-use exports remain banned. A Govini analysis found China's export ban affects more than 1,000 US weapons production systems and over 20,000 individual parts.[58][59][60][61]

Workforce: Defense analysts cite persistent workforce shortages and cost inflation as structural constraints, particularly for specialized manufacturing roles in missile production that require security clearances and years of skill development.[62]

The Realistic Surge Timeline

Given confirmed sub-tier bottlenecks, the realistic production surge timeline — not the announcement timeline — is as follows:

JDAM kits: 6–18 months to surge production materially (most achievable)

Patriot PAC-3 MSE: 2–3 years to meaningfully increase (Lockheed has floor space; needs workforce + tooling)

Tomahawk, JASSM: 3–5 years to reach surge production rates (SRM and guidance system bottlenecks)

THAAD: 7–10 years to reach 400/year (facility construction + supplier development + workforce)

SM-3: 3–5 years (capacity constrained; unit cost already escalating under uncertainty)

GBU-57 MOP: New units arrive January 2028 at earliest[38]

6. What Happens When Stocks Run Low: The Campaign's Trajectory

The Pivot Already Happened

The "smaller bombs" transition documented by Hegseth on March 4 is not a future scenario — it is the present operational reality. By Day 14, only 1% of US munitions were standoff weapons. The shift has both military and supply-chain dimensions:[3]

Military logic (official narrative): With Iran's air defense destroyed, US bombers can operate closer to targets, making expensive standoff capability unnecessary. JDAM-equipped 500-, 1,000-, and 2,000-pound bombs are accurate to roughly the same CEP when delivered from medium altitude against non-hardened targets.[6][3]

Supply logic (analyst assessment): The CSIS analysis notes that Hegseth "approached a 'point of munitions transition' by the fourth day" and this "dramatically reduced war costs and munitions strain." A single Tomahawk ($3.5M) and a BLU-110 JDAM ($100,000) both deliver 1,000-pound warheads with similar accuracy, but the JDAM is 35 times cheaper and available in far larger quantities.[3]

CSIS Inventory Threshold Assessment

CSIS's analysis of the Day 6 cost report concluded: the DoD "does not appear to face immediate inventory concerns in conducting the current conflict." However, the same analysis identifies critical caveats:[3]

Other theaters degraded: High-demand munitions are being diverted from Western Pacific and Ukraine war plans

Production won't cover usage: FY2026 production will not fully cover Epic Fury usage to date, limiting hoped-for stockpile expansion

Interceptor math is different from strike munitions: For interceptors (THAAD, SM-3), the constraint is structural, not just current-rate

No THAAD deliveries until April 2027: If Iran resumes high-intensity ballistic missile launches, the US has limited reserve capacity to replenish batteries under fire

The "Weeks" Assessment Contextualized

The American Prospect's characterization of "a few weeks of bombing left before running out of the precision munitions typically used in modern warfare" and NPR's Seth Jones assessment of "sooner rather than later" pain on stockpiles are both technically correct and potentially misleading, depending on framing:[2][63]

Accurate for: THAAD interceptors, SM-3s, Tomahawks, MOP bunker busters — these are genuinely constrained within the current conflict's demand curve

Misleading for: The overall air campaign's capacity to continue, given that JDAMs, gravity bombs, and tactical air power are not similarly constrained

Key distinction: The US can continue to bomb essentially indefinitely with gravity bombs. It cannot sustain standoff precision strikes or tier-1 ballistic missile defense indefinitely.

The Financial Times reported an internal Pentagon assessment that the US could "easily" expend "a year's worth" of certain munitions in just a few days. That assessment likely referred specifically to Tomahawks, THAAD interceptors, and SM-3s — the exquisite, hard-to-replace categories.[64]

Implications for Civilian Casualties and Political Pressure

The shift from standoff to gravity bombs introduces important targeting limitations:

Standoff weapons (Tomahawk, JASSM) are used against defended airspace and deeply hardened or long-range targets. They allow strikes without aircraft overflight.

Gravity bombs (JDAM) require aircraft overflight, limiting use to airspace where air dominance has been established. Against hardened buried targets, they lack the penetration capability of weapons like the GBU-57 MOP.

The GBU-57 inventory is effectively exhausted (fewer than 6 remaining post-Operation Midnight Hammer), with replacements not arriving until 2028. This means any remaining hardened, deeply buried Iranian facilities cannot be effectively targeted with the weapon designed specifically for that purpose.[36][38]

The shift to shorter-range weapons increases aircraft exposure and limits the target set, particularly for remaining missile production facilities Iran has dispersed underground.

Historical precedent (Gaza 2023) suggests that munitions transitions under operational pressure can increase proportional civilian casualties when precision capability is reduced — though JDAM-guided bombs remain precision weapons by any historical standard.[65]

7. Critical Mineral Dependencies and Supply Chain Vulnerabilities

The Mineral Problem

Modern precision munitions are not just metal and explosive — they are miniaturized information systems requiring sophisticated materials. Key dependencies:

Gallium (China produces 98.8% globally): Used in semiconductors critical for radar systems, guidance electronics, and advanced communications components found in virtually every modern PGM.[66]

Germanium (China produces 59.2% globally): Used in infrared optics (critical for targeting systems and seekers), fiber optic cables, and semiconductor devices. The Patriot system's thermal imaging seeker relies on germanium-based IR detectors.[66]

Antimony (China produces ~48% globally): Used in ammunition, flame retardants, batteries, and nuclear weapons components. China's September 2024 antimony export restrictions saw prices surge as US importers scrambled for alternatives.[66]

Rare Earth Elements for permanent magnets in guidance systems: China controls 95% of heavy rare earth output; the US imports ~100% of its needs, 90% from China.[59]

Current Status: China suspended its December 2024 ban on gallium, germanium, and antimony exports to the US in November 2025 following the Trump-Xi meeting — but the suspension expires November 27, 2026, and military-end-user exports remain permanently banned. This creates a ticking clock: if the war is still ongoing when the suspension expires, production ramp-ups reliant on Chinese minerals face a structural disruption.[61]

The DoW's official position (Senate Armed Services Committee testimony, February 2026): "For decades, we have outsourced and offshored mining and processing, creating a strategic vulnerability of the highest order. Today, our primary strategic competitor, China, controls the global supply chain for numerous critical minerals." The DoW's four-pillar mitigation strategy — domestic production, allied partners, demand substitution, strategic stockpile modernization — is multi-year in execution.[59]

The Sub-Tier Problem in Detail

Govini's December 2024 analysis found that China's mineral export restrictions affect more than 1,000 US weapons production systems and over 20,000 individual parts across all service branches. The scope is broader than any single weapons category — it is systemic across the precision munitions industrial base.[60]

For solid rocket motors specifically, both of the two primary US domestic SRM producers (Northrop Grumman and L3Harris) share common sub-tier suppliers for nozzles, case insulation, and propellant chemicals. A supply disruption at any shared node affects multiple missile programs simultaneously. The Senate explicitly cited sub-tier capacity as a limiting factor in legislation directing DoD to assess and strengthen the SRM industrial base.[56][54]

8. Allied Contributions and Stockpile Transfers

As of March 22, 2026, the allied contribution to the US-Israeli campaign's munitions burden has been limited:

UK: Operating in a defensive capacity. Royal Navy and Army assets deploying Martlet Lightweight Multirole Missiles from Wildcat helicopters to intercept Iranian drones — cost-effective given the Martlet's sub-$100,000 price point. A British military installation in Cyprus was struck by a suspected drone, killing UK servicemembers. UK has not transferred precision munitions stockpiles to the US campaign as of available reporting.[53][67]

Gulf states (UAE, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Bahrain): All facing interceptor depletion. CNN reported four days into the war that "at least one of the United States' Gulf allies is already running low on crucial interceptor munitions." Qatar has "enough interceptors for a long period" and is in contact with CENTCOM for contingency resupply. Saudi Arabia received its first operational THAAD battery in July 2025, with 360 interceptors — but these were part of an FMS sale now competing with US replenishment needs.[68][9]

No confirmed large-scale stockpile transfers from NATO allies (UK, France, Germany) to the active offensive campaign as of available public reporting. European allies are monitoring closely — BAE, Hensoldt, and Leonardo saw stock surges — but direct munitions transfers require export approvals and political decisions that have not been reported.[69]

Domestic emergency manufacturing response: Following a March 2026 White House meeting with defense CEOs, major primes (RTX, Lockheed, Northrop, Boeing, BAE, L3Harris, Honeywell) pledged to "quadruple production" of advanced munitions. This pledge faces all the sub-tier constraints described above, but the demand signal is now unambiguous and long-term contract structures are being established.[70][71]

9. Assessment: Campaign Sustainability by Munitions Category

What Can Be Sustained (Essentially Indefinitely)

JDAM-guided gravity bombs for strikes against non-defended airspace targets

Tactical air power (F-35, F-22, B-2, B-1 sorties) against degraded or absent Iranian air defense

LUCAS/counter-drone operations using reverse-engineered Shahed equivalents

HIMARS/ATACMS for medium-range precision strikes (inventory ~1,000; limited but not immediately exhausted)

What Is Under Stress (Weeks to Months of Sustained High-Tempo Operations)

Tomahawk: 319 of ~3,100–4,150 fired; remaining ~2,700–2,800. At 40-50/day high-tempo rate, exhausted in 55–70 days. At reduced current tempo, much longer. But no replacements arrive until ~2028.

JASSM/JASSM-ER: ~3,500 pre-war inventory with limited consumption in Epic Fury. Substantial reserve, but non-replenishable quickly.

Patriot PAC-3 MSE: ~2,000 pre-war; 172 FY2026 deliveries. At high intercept rates against resumed Iranian salvos, meaningful depletion within 4–6 weeks.

What Is Critically Constrained (Already Thin)

THAAD: The most acute vulnerability. The US entered this war with a depleted inventory from June 2025, no new deliveries since August 2023, and no new deliveries until April 2027. Current inventory likely in the 430–490 range across eight batteries. A resumed high-intensity Iranian ballistic missile campaign of the June 2025 scale could exhaust remaining reserve capacity within days.

SM-3: ~426 post-June 2025; meaningful use in Epic Fury ongoing. FY2026 deliveries of only 66 expected.

GBU-57 MOP: Effectively exhausted (<6 remaining). Cannot strike deeply buried targets until 2028 deliveries at earliest.

The Duration Verdict

The campaign can continue as currently constituted — gravity bomb strikes against targets in degraded Iranian airspace — for months or potentially longer. The constraint is not bombs. The constraint is:

If Iran resumes high-intensity ballistic missile launches: THAAD and SM-3 reserves could be exhausted within 1–3 weeks of sustained high-tempo intercept operations.

If Iran escalates to harder targets or if standoff capability is needed: Tomahawk and JASSM reserves are finite; no replacement Tomahawks for ~2 years.

If the conflict spreads to defended airspace: Iran's claim that it "has not yet used its most advanced weapons" matters. If Iran deploys CM-302 supersonic anti-ship missiles, the cost and nature of the campaign changes materially.

The Pacific and Ukraine risk: Every THAAD interceptor and SM-3 fired over Iran is not available for deterrence of China or support for Ukraine. This was explicitly warned by Joint Chiefs Chairman Caine before the war began. CSIS's conclusion is precise: the US faces "risks in other theaters."[68][3]

The American Prospect's "few weeks" assessment applies specifically to high-end standoff precision munitions and interceptors for tier-1 ballistic missile defense — and that assessment was accurate. The shift to gravity bombs, which Hegseth acknowledged, is the adaptation forced by that constraint. The open question is whether Iran can exploit the reduced precision standoff capability and THAAD thinness in a way that changes the campaign's character before production surges become operationally meaningful.
