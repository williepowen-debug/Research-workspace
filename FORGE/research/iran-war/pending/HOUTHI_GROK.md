# 1. Indigenous Weapons Production  
- **Anti‐ship ballistic missiles (ASBMs):**  Houthis use ASBMs based on Iranian designs (e.g. Mohit/Fateh, Asef/Fateh-313, Tankil/Ra’ad) and have little evidence of fully homegrown ASBMs.  Analysts report that the Houthis’ ASBMs “arrived … directly from Iran”【34†L52-L60】, and only one claimed “indigenous” ASBM (the *Falaq*) exists – which experts doubt【34†L131-L134】.  The IISS notes that some short-range ballistic or cruise‐derived missiles (≤200 km) – including Falaq, Mayun and Bahr al-Ahmar – may be assembled domestically, but key components (guidance, warhead tech) remain Iranian-sourced【47†L148-L157】【47†L171-L179】.  In practice the Houthis mostly refurbish or slightly modify imported missiles.  

- **Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs/UCAVs):**  Houthis deploy a mix of drones, from small commercial quadcopters to larger attack UAVs.  Smaller surveillance drones like “Rased” or hexacopter designs (e.g. *Rujum*) are essentially civilian systems modified locally【6†L149-L160】.  Larger UAVs (Shahed-131/136, Samad-2/3, Yaffa, Qasef, etc.) incorporate foreign parts.  For example, recovered Samad-3 drones use a Chinese DLE-170 engine even as they contain some Iranian avionics【47†L181-L189】.  The UN Panel notes *all known Houthi UAVs so far rely on piston engines*【24†L137-L139】, reflecting commercial engine use; the capture of a turbojet suggests they seek higher performance【24†L137-L144】.  Houthi leaders claim to design drones themselves, but experts find their designs nearly identical to Iranian models (e.g. the Houthi Qasef-I drone is “nearly identical” to Iran’s Ababil-II, even sharing serial numbers)【49†L164-L170】.  In short, Houthi drones are built from imported kits and commercial parts; advanced drones (long-endurance kamikazes) still rely heavily on Iranian engines/guidance【47†L181-L189】【49†L164-L170】.  (Public sources do not provide reliable production-rate figures, but the frequency of attacks implies dozens of new drones monthly.)  

- **Naval mines:**  Ansar Allah **does** produce and lay sea mines.  Since 2017 they “have sown hundreds of mines” along Yemen’s Red Sea coast【42†L383-L392】.  Lacking specialized minelayers, Houthis use armed boats and even fishing vessels to deploy moored and drifting mines【42†L387-L392】.  Most Houthi mines are thought to be locally improvised or manufactured (using welding, floating mines from gas cylinders, contact/magnetic detonators, etc.), though some advanced variants resemble Iranian designs【42†L387-L398】.  For example, the Stimson Center reports “most [Houthi mines] are believed to be indigenously manufactured by the Houthis”【42†L387-L395】.  Thus, yes – the Houthis have **mine-laying capability** using ad-hoc methods, and have deployed mines to defend coastal approaches.  (They have so far used mines sparingly against international shipping, focusing on targeted strikes, but analysts warn they could seed Bab al-Mandeb in a broader campaign【42†L421-L430】.)

- **Anti‐ship cruise missiles (ASCMs):**  Houthis use Chinese-derived ASCMs (e.g. the “Al-Mandeb 2”, a Yemeni name for a copy of China’s C-802/Noor) and possibly others, but *no evidence of full Yemeni manufacture* of such missiles exists.  The missiles they fire – such as C-802 clones or Iranian Noor variants – were supplied by Iran (or via Iran)【43†L85-L92】.  The Houthis claim they can build many ASCMs domestically, but U.S. assessments flatly dispute this: key parts like small turbojet engines and radar seekers for ASCMs are “not assessed to have the capability” in Yemen【47†L171-L179】.  In practice, captured ASCMs (e.g. disassembled Noor/C-802 systems) suggest Yemeni facilities only *assemble* imported kits【47†L177-L185】.  

- **Ballistic missiles (to Saudi/UAE):**  The Houthis inherited (and locally rebuilt) a variety of short- and medium-range ballistic rockets (e.g. Badr, Burkan series).  Some shorter-range ballistic “cruise-type” missiles (ranges ≲200 km) appear to be made in Yemen, albeit with foreign guidance.  For instance, a U.N. analysis finds a handful of close-range ballistic/anti-ship missiles (like Faleq, Mayun) are “probably manufactured domestically”【47†L152-L159】, though Iranians supplied the expertise (the *Mouhit* ASBM was a retrofitted Soviet SA-2 with Iranian guidance【47†L152-L159】).  By contrast, longer-range SRBMs (300–800 km) and MRBMs targeting Saudi/UAE or Israel are “almost certainly supplied directly by Iran”【47†L161-L169】.  In short, Yemen’s own factories can churn out simpler rocket bodies, but rely on Iranian propellant, electronics and design for any larger missile.  

# 2. Supply-Chain Independence  
Most experts agree the Houthis **remain heavily dependent on Iran for advanced weapons**.  The IISS notes the group’s arsenal “continues to be reliant on support from Iran”【47†L126-L129】: Tehran supplies critical components (guidance kits, seeker heads, engines, avionics) and complete systems for the most sophisticated arms【47†L148-L157】【47†L171-L179】.  For example, Houthi-made rocket/missile bodies may be local, but inertial/nav systems and optics come from Iran【47†L148-L157】.  The UN Panel and analysts alike emphasize that without Iranian missile-technology transfers (documented by seized fragments), Houthi missile capability would collapse【47†L148-L157】【47†L171-L179】.

However, the Houthis have developed **alternative supply channels**.  US sanctions and intelligence note they operate extensive smuggling networks via Oman, UAE and Red Sea ports【58†L691-L699】【60†L109-L118】.  China is increasingly implicated: US authorities sanctioned a Chinese satellite company for giving imagery to Houthis and even found Chinese “drone kits” on a ship destined for Houthi areas【51†L412-L417】.  Moscow is also engaging: reports indicate Russia has given Houthis targeting data and even plans to send some weapons (AK rifles, Yakhont ASCMs)【53†L343-L352】.  In addition, Yemen-based financiers (some in East Africa/Asia) ferry Iranian oil and weapons sales back to the Houthis【60†L120-L129】. 

Still, key technologies appear *irreplaceable* by other patrons.  The Houthis **cannot domestically produce rocket engines or radar seekers** – Iran is the only known source for those.  If Iranian support were cut, they would struggle to replenish ASBMs/ASCMs or advanced drones.  In short, a large fraction of the Houthis’ sophisticated arsenal depends on Tehran – estimates range in the **tens of percent** up to nearly all advanced components【47†L171-L179】【51†L412-L417】.  They offset this by maximizing locally available parts (3D-printed components, imported commercial gear) and by cultivating non-Iranian partnerships, but Iran remains the linchpin of their supply chain.

# 3. Red Sea Interdiction Capability  
- **Threat to shipping:**  The Houthis pose a **significant and active hazard** to vessels transiting Bab al-Mandeb and the southern Red Sea.  Since November 2023 they have launched *hundreds* of weapons at ships, forcing many to reroute.  US authorities reported **88 attacks on merchant vessels since Nov 1, 2023**【17†L231-L239】.  Industry sources count *“more than 100 merchant vessels”* targeted by early 2026【18†L133-L136】 (with dozens of drones, missiles and small boats fired).  The campaign has sunk or damaged ships (4 sunk, 1 captured, 8 crew killed by mid-2026) and driven up war-risk premiums dramatically【18†L133-L136】【18†L116-L124】.  The current ceasefire pause is tenuous; Houthis have declared they are prepared to resume strikes “immediately” if war pressures return【18†L62-L65】.  In sum, the ongoing threat level remains **high –** virtually all ships in the corridor must now consider Houthi capabilities when routing or insuring a voyage.  

- **Attack count & inventory:**  Available data indicate dozens of anti-ship weapons were fired in the Red Sea campaign.  Israel-based researchers documented *21 ASBMs* launched by Jan. 2024【34†L52-L60】 (and presumably more since).  Meanwhile, at least **25 ballistic or cruise missile strikes** were publicly confirmed between Nov 2023 and Jan 2024【43†L68-L73】.  The Houthis also used 4 cruise missiles and ~95 armed drones in that period【34†L52-L60】.  Given these tallies, analysts infer the Houthis likely have on the order of a few dozen ASBMs and a similar number of anti-ship cruise missiles (plus hundreds of drones) in reserve.  They also reportedly maintain *hundreds of naval mines*【42†L383-L392】 and have coastal radar/c2 emplacements to cue strikes.  No official “inventory” count is published, but the sustained high-tempo attacks imply a large stockpile of missiles and UAVs remains.  

- **Can they close Bab al-Mandeb?**  Completely sealing the strait would be extremely difficult.  The Bab al-Mandeb is a wider, more complex choke point than Hormuz, and there are few prepared defensive mines or A2/AD systems in Houthi hands.  So far, Houthis have targeted selectively (largely Israeli, US/UK interests) rather than indiscriminately mining international lanes【42†L421-L430】.  They have achieved **some** hits (mainly on civilian ships) but overall success has been limited【36†L159-L162】.  For example, despite launching many ASBMs, “the number of hits recorded so far remains limited”【36†L159-L162】.  Thus their capability today is best described as **persistent disruption and harassment**, not a guaranteed blockade.  However, they have caused serious diversion costs: rerouting through the Cape adds 10–14 days and millions in costs per ship【18†L126-L131】, and insurers charge hundreds of thousands extra per transit【18†L116-L124】.  If the Houthis and Iran were to coordinate fully (closing Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb together), roughly one-quarter of the world’s seaborne oil would be affected (Bab al-Mandeb alone carries ~6% of world oil shipments【32†L171-L174】).  Such a dual choke would trigger a major energy crisis; but as of now, the Houthis appear limited to harassment (though their doctrine explicitly keeps a mining option “in reserve”【42†L421-L430】).

# 4. “Strategic Reserve” Theory  
Analysts have observed that Houthis largely held fire during portions of the 2024–25 conflict, acting as a **brake on escalation**.  For instance, after a sharp peak of missile/drone strikes in late 2023, **attacks fell off dramatically by late 2025**.  One study notes *“from approximately mid-November 2025 until late February 2026, there were essentially no sustained missile/drone attacks”* on Red Sea shipping【18†L68-L72】.  Only when Israel-U.S. operations against Iran resumed in early March 2026 did Houthis announce they would **restart** attacks immediately【18†L62-L65】.  This pause coincided with regional ceasefires (e.g. Gaza truce) and seems deliberate.  

Evidence for a holdback policy includes public statements. Houthi leaders say only that their *“fingers are on the trigger”* and that they will act “at any moment” if warranted【32†L194-L202】 – suggesting they are waiting for signals rather than firing unilaterally.  The head of Houthi supreme council, Mahdi al-Mashat, has warned foreign navies against involvement, but otherwise the group speaks mostly of rights to retaliate.  Notably, an International Crisis Group analysis quotes a senior Houthi official who explicitly frames them as an “important card” being **kept in reserve** by Iran【32†L269-L277】.  Economist Gregory Brew (Eurasia Group) likewise notes that U.S.-Israeli strikes have weakened the Houthis and “discourage them from engaging in large-scale hostilities” until the timing is right【32†L259-L262】.

Strategic logic for holding back includes preserving one’s best capabilities.  By not expending all ASBMs and cruise missiles now, the Houthis (and their Iranian patrons) ensure these long-range weapons remain as an escalation option if needed.  This “reserve” could be used later if the war drags on or if Western/carrier forces intervene.  It also constrains Israel and allies: as long as Hamas is under heavy pressure, Iran won’t unleash the Houthis, but if Hamas is defeated, the Houthis could be unleashed suddenly.  In sum, while concrete evidence is limited, multiple observers (ICG, US officials) suggest the reduction in Houthi strikes during early 2024–26 was intentional, making Houthis a latent threat that can escalate on cue【32†L269-L277】【18†L68-L72】.  

# 5. Financial Independence  
The Houthis fund themselves largely from Yemeni revenues, supplemented by Iranian support.  Controlling much of northwest Yemen, they collect customs, taxes and fees on commerce and fuel.  Key sources include: 

  - **Oil/procurement smuggling:**  US Treasury finds the Houthis generate over **$2 billion per year** from illicit petroleum sales【58†L691-L699】.  Iran itself provides free monthly oil shipments and credit via UAE front companies【58†L691-L699】, which the Houthis then sell at market or higher prices.  Houthi networks in East Africa and Asia also resell Iranian fuel (often via small “teapot” refineries in China) and remit profits back【60†L120-L129】.  

  - **Port and trade taxes:**  Yemen depends on imports for 90% of essentials, so Houthi-controlled ports (Hodeida, Saleef, Ras Isa) generate huge fees.  The group reportedly collects up to 100% tariff on goods coming via non-Houthi ports【60†L93-L101】 and charges high prices on fuel to domestic buyers【58†L712-L719】.  (They likewise tax commercial transactions in Sanaa’s markets.)  

  - **Maritime fees:**  Uniquely, the Houthis extort payments from ships.  A UN report cited in Maritime Executive finds Houthi checkpoints on Bab al-Mandeb net them roughly **$180 million per month** in “passage fees” (ransoms) from commercial vessels【60†L109-L118】.  Although some question that figure, even a fraction of it provides a major revenue stream.  

  - **Other sources:**  They also harvest agricultural zones and local enterprises, and likely receive covert Iranian cash.  Before sanctions escalated, Iran transferred funds monthly; Tehran can still finance key projects (e.g. rebuilding missile stockpiles).  

In summary, the Houthis now have diversified income.  Analysts agree they are **not purely dependent on Iranian subsidies**.  Domestic revenues alone appear enough to keep a substantial armed force afloat: Treasury and research reports highlight that local funds (oil/tariffs/fees) vastly exceed any documented Iranian budget transfers【58†L691-L699】【60†L109-L118】.  Of course, Iranian wealth still flows into them, especially for weapons.  But even if Tehran’s funding dried up, the Houthis likely could sustain at least baseline operations with what they extract from Yemeni commerce and ports.  

# 6. Post-Iran Scenarios  
If Iran’s regime were to collapse or be sidelined, the Houthis’ position would shift in complex ways.  On one hand, losing Iran’s patron would **deprive them of advanced arms and political backing**.  IISS and US analysts emphasize that without Iranian missiles/tech, Houthi capabilities would shrink【47†L148-L157】【47†L171-L179】.  Economically, Iranian funding and free oil would vanish, though we have seen their local revenues are substantial.  Some officials (e.g. Brew) argue the group is already under strain (“struggling to pay fighters”【32†L259-L262】), so Iran’s fall could indeed weaken them tactically.  

On the other hand, the Houthis have long cultivated autonomy.  RFE/RL notes they “retain considerable autonomy” from Iran and are “an intractable force” in Yemen【32†L247-L252】.  In a post-Iran vacuum, the Houthis would almost certainly **seek new patrons**.  They already have warming ties with Russia and China: reports cite Russian arms transfers and combat advisors【53†L343-L352】, and Chinese firms providing tech and protecting Chinese shipping【51†L340-L349】【51†L412-L417】.  Both Beijing and Moscow have strategic reasons to court the Houthis if it counters Western interests.  It is plausible that a “fully independent” Houthi could emerge, trading with whoever keeps the oil flowing through Hodeida and allows their missiles to strike at sea.  

Could they become a permanent anti-shipping menace?  Possibly – but likely as a lower tier of threat.  Without Iran’s high-end missiles and drones, Houthis would revert to indigenous UAVs, rockets and mines (all of which they can sustain).  This would still endanger shipping, but lack of Iranian precision kits would limit their reach.  However, if Russia or China filled the gap (for instance, selling ASCMs or technology), the Houthis might maintain a strong maritime strike force.  The experts are mixed: some (ICG’s Nagi) suggest the Houthis are being held in reserve by Iran *now* as a lever, implying they would certainly remain potent if unleashed【32†L269-L277】. Others caution that their true backbone is Iranian support【47†L148-L157】.  Realistically, the Houthis would survive as a powerful Yemeni faction, but the scale of their maritime campaign would depend on who steps into Iran’s shoes.  

# 7. Dual-Chokepoint Implications  
Closing both Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb simultaneously would indeed be catastrophic: together they account for roughly 25–30% of global seaborne oil exports (Iran’s Gulf ports via Hormuz are ~20%, Bab al-Mandeb ~6%【32†L171-L174】; other estimates include partial flows through Suez).  If both were blocked, about one-quarter to one-third of oil shipments would halt, sending prices spiking.  Such a scenario is not fanciful during this regional war, but it would be **extremely severe**.  In practice, even the threat of dual closure has already rocked markets – oil prices spiked when Iran threatened Hormuz, and analysts warn Bab al-Mandeb attacks could double the impact.

For shipping and insurance, the effect has been real.  During the 2023–2025 Red Sea campaign, war-risk premiums on each Suez transit surged by hundreds of thousands of dollars【18†L116-L124】.  Underwriters refused insurance to vessels associated with Western firms【18†L116-L124】.  If Bab el-Mandeb were fully mined or shot-blocked while Hormuz was shut, insurers would either halt coverage for Red Sea voyages or demand prohibitive rates.  Carriers would reroute nearly all traffic around Africa (adding ~10–14 days and ~30% more fuel cost per trip【18†L126-L131】).  Countries dependent on Gulf oil (Europe, India, China) would face shortages.  While powerful navies (U.S., international coalition) would likely intervene to reopen lanes, the interim chaos would be unprecedented.  In summary, although dual closure is a logical possibility in this conflict, it would provoke an enormous global response; currently the threat remains that of **major disruption** rather than a guaranteed permanent shutdown【32†L171-L174】【18†L116-L124】.
