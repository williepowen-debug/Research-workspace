# 1990-91 Gulf War: Impact on US Tourism
## Research Analysis — BRENT Domain

**Compiled:** March 2026  
**Purpose:** Historical analog for current tariff/uncertainty shock on tourism/hospitality  
**Key thesis:** Two-channel shock (fuel cost spike + fear/terrorism premium) with distinct timing signatures

---

## (a) Oil Price Timeline

### The Price Spike

| Period | WTI/Crude Price | Key Event |
|--------|----------------|-----------|
| July 1990 (avg) | ~$17/barrel | Pre-invasion baseline |
| Aug 2, 1990 | $21–$28/barrel | Iraq invades Kuwait |
| Mid-October 1990 | **$46/barrel** | Peak — +170% from July |
| Jan–Feb 1991 | $25–$30/barrel | Coalition buildup, prices ease |
| Feb 28, 1991 | ~$20/barrel | War ends, prices collapse |
| Late 1991–1994 | $17–$20/barrel | Gradual normalization |

**Duration:** Nine months total spike (Aug 1990 – April 1991). Shorter and less extreme than 1973 or 1979 but enough to tip a pre-stressed airline industry.

### Jet Fuel Channel (Direct Airline Impact)
- Pre-invasion spot price: **~$0.60/gallon**
- October 1990 peak: **~$1.50/gallon** (+150%)
- January 1991 (war outbreak): **~$0.75/gallon** (already partially retraced)
- Rule of thumb: Every 1¢/gallon increase = **$150M/year** added to industry expense base
- At October peak, the fuel spike alone added ~$13–15B/year annualized to airline costs

### Sequencing Note
The OIL shock and the FEAR shock were somewhat sequential: oil peaked **before** the war started, while fear/terrorism concerns peaked **after** January 16, 1991 (war outbreak). This separation is analytically useful — oil moved the supply curve, fear moved the demand curve.

## (b) International Visitor Arrivals to the US

### Aggregate Global Headline
- **International travel declined 30%** in the first two weeks after war broke out January 16, 1991 (NYT, Jan 27, 1991)
- This was the immediate, acute measurement — sustained through Q1 1991

### US Outbound (Americans Traveling Abroad)
- Americans visiting UK: **~3 million in 1990 → ~2.3 million in 1991** (~23% decline)
- European capitals saw collapse in American visitors: Paris fashion shows, London West End openings — all essentially empty of American travelers
- American celebrities specifically cited: Clint Eastwood, Meryl Streep, Sylvester Stallone all canceled planned European trips

### Inbound to US (Foreign Visitors)
- Hawaii Tourism Bureau data: "Eastbound visitors reticent to come to the US amidst threats of terrorism" — arrivals declined through **1994** (not just 1991), compounded by:
  - Gulf War fear
  - US recession reducing attractiveness
  - Japanese economy weakening (major source market)
  - Reduction in airline seat capacity as carriers cut routes

### Regional Tourism Flows (from WTO data)
- North America → Jordan: **-48%**
- North America → Egypt: **-36%**
- North America → Israel: **-30%**
- Heathrow (world's busiest airport): traffic **-21%**

### Recovery Timeline
- By mid-1991 (post-war), signs of recovery emerging  
- San Diego hotel occupancy: April 1991 at 64.9%, **+10.6% vs. April 1990** (the comparison period had already been weak)
- 1992: **8% growth in global travel** (World Tourism Organization) — essentially full Y/Y recovery
- **Verdict: ~18-month disruption peak-to-recovery for most markets; Hawaii/Japan corridor lingered through 1994**

## (c) Airline Response — Failures, Route Cuts, Load Factors

### The Pre-Existing Condition
The airline industry entered the Gulf War already severely wounded:
- Full-year 1990 loss: **$2.0 billion** (previous record by 2x)
- Q4 1990 alone: **$1.7 billion** loss (when fuel peaked and economy softened)
- Record **467 million passengers** boarded in 1990 (+3% vs. 1989) — traffic was UP but costs overwhelmed revenue

### Did the War Accelerate Failures? **YES — definitively**

**Eastern Airlines**
- Had been in Chapter 11 since 1989 (machinists strike)
- Sold assets to Delta in January 1991 for $41M
- **Ceased operations January 18, 1991** — two days after war began
- Final blow: combination of weak traffic + higher fuel (though it was already terminal)

**Pan American World Airways**
- Filed Chapter 11 **January 1991** (same month war started — no coincidence)
- Sold European routes to Delta ($1.4B, summer 1991) in desperate restructure
- Sold London routes to United; Latin American routes auctioned to United in December 1991
- **Ceased all operations December 4, 1991**
- Newsweek: "The Gulf War's chill on travel and the recession's squeeze was especially cruel — major carriers lost **$2 billion in the three months before Pan Am filed**"

**Continental Airlines**
- Filed Chapter 11 **December 1990** — before the war, directly citing fuel cost spike

**Midway Airlines**
- Ceased operations **fall 1991**

**Trans World Airlines (TWA)**
- Survived through 1991 but entered "prepackaged bankruptcy" filing
- Filed Chapter 11 **January 1992** — to shed ~$1B in debt and renegotiate labor contracts
- Emerged from bankruptcy August 1993

**Survivors (wounded)**
- USAir, America West: both considered high-risk by analysts; America West filed Ch. 11 in 1991
- Big Three (United, American, Delta): all lost money in 1991 but positioned to survive — **actively absorbed routes from collapsing competitors**
  - United: acquired Pan Am's London routes + Latin American routes
  - American: bought TWA's London routes
  - Delta: absorbed most remaining Pan Am European routes + Eastern assets

### Load Factor / Traffic Data
- Airline passenger traffic: **-8% in Q1 1991** (most acute period)
- Full year 1991: **-2.2%** (recovery in H2 partially offset Q1 collapse)
- Total industry job losses 1991: **50,000 positions**
- Combined 1990–1991 industry losses: **~$6 billion**

### Route Architecture Impact
- "Big Three" consolidation accelerated — by end of 1991, Delta/United/American dominated international routes in ways they hadn't in 1989
- Fewer international routes = fewer seats = higher fares = slower recovery of inbound tourism

## (d) Hotel Industry — Occupancy and Conventions

### Occupancy Rate Data

| Metric | Data Point | Source |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| US national occupancy, Dec 1991 | **46%** | NYT, Feb 1993 |
| US national occupancy, Dec 1992 | 48.1% (+4.6% YoY) | NYT, Feb 1993 |
| Orange County (CA) peak 1989 | 66.8% | Smith Travel Research |
| Orange County Q1 1992 | 58.9% (-7.9 pts from peak) | Smith Travel Research |
| National average, 1992 | "low 60s" | Smith Barney analyst |
| "Red flag" threshold | Below 60% | Industry benchmark |

**Key insight:** National occupancy at 46% in December 1991 was catastrophically below the ~55-60% break-even threshold. Hotels built during the 1985-1990 boom were insolvent at these levels.

### Property Value Destruction
- **National hotel value decline 1991:** ~14%
- **Cumulative 1990-1991 national:** ~18%
- **Orange County (CA) 1991 alone:** 20%
- **Orange County cumulative 1989-1991:** 30%+
- Hospitality Valuation Services: "declining values effectively wiped out investors' equity in many hotel projects"

### Employment in Lodging
- Orange County alone: ~1,450 hotel workers (~7% of 1989 peak employment) out of jobs
- Nationally: at least 13 large hotels in Orange County went into foreclosure/receivership/bankruptcy
- **Global tourism jobs lost by end of Gulf War: ~3 million** (WTO estimate)

### Convention and Meetings Industry
The convention sector had a distinct two-phase dynamic:

**Phase 1 (Jan–Feb 1991):** Relative resilience — events booked months/years in advance couldn't easily cancel; association meetings showed "slippage in attendance" but few outright cancellations.

**Phase 2 (Feb–April 1991):** Corporate meetings/training events collapsed
- Companies banned travel entirely (cost-cutting + terrorism fear)
- 3-day trips cut to 2 days; executives flying same-day instead of overnight
- Smaller delegations sent to unavoidable events
- Medical convention (AAOS, 22,000 attendees) saw cancellations due to military activations and foreign physician travel difficulties
- Association of Corporate Travel Executives postponed its **own annual convention** because members were too busy managing the chaos

**Recovery Timeline:** Convention industry analysts projected "60-90 day lag" for recovery after war ended in late February 1991 — broadly accurate, with normal business travel resuming by May-June 1991.

## (e) The Fear Channel — Separating Fuel from Terror

### Evidence That Fear Was the Dominant Short-Term Channel

The most analytically useful data point: jet fuel prices **had already come down significantly from their October peak by the time the war started**. When war broke out January 16, 1991, fuel was at ~$0.75/gallon — back to only modest premium over pre-crisis levels. Yet that's when travel collapsed hardest.

This strongly suggests the **fear/uncertainty premium dominated the acute phase** (Jan-April 1991), while fuel costs dominated the earlier softening (Aug-Dec 1990).

### Qualitative Evidence for Fear Channel
1. NYT Jan 28, 1991: "Frightened by the threat of terrorism... Americans are canceling vacation plans and choosing to stay close to home." Explicitly about fear, not price.
2. "Even people who could afford expensive transatlantic flights canceled due to the fear of terrorism" (AltexSoft/WTO synthesis)
3. American celebrities canceled European trips — these are people for whom cost was irrelevant
4. Major companies issued blanket international travel bans — a policy response, not a price response
5. Video-conferencing explicitly cited as terrorism-avoidance substitute (not cost-optimization)
6. Domestic travel also declined — this cannot be explained by fuel costs alone (domestic fares didn't spike proportionally)

### Evidence for Fuel Channel
1. Q4 1990 losses ($1.7B) hit **before** war started — driven by fuel
2. Continental and Pan Am filed bankruptcy citing fuel costs in December 1990/January 1991
3. Fuel represented **30%+ of airline operating costs** by 1991 — price moves were existential for leveraged carriers
4. Record passenger volumes in 1990 (467M) but still massive losses = fuel cost was the P&L killer, not traffic

### Analytical Framework: TWO WAVES
**Wave 1 (Aug–Dec 1990): The Fuel Wave**
- Oil price shock hits airline P&L
- Leisure travelers start pulling back on forward bookings (pricing sensitivity)
- Recession amplifies demand softness
- Weak carriers begin failing
- Mechanism: Cost → Fare → Demand reduction

**Wave 2 (Jan–Feb 1991): The Fear Wave**
- War outbreak triggers acute demand collapse (-30% in two weeks)
- Mechanism: Pure behavioral/psychological — fear of terrorism, desire to "stay home"
- Not price-elastic: wealthy travelers, corporate travelers all canceled simultaneously
- Recovery began **immediately** after military success became clear (late February 1991)

**The key lesson for current analog:** These are **distinct mechanisms** with different elasticities and recovery profiles. Fear-driven collapse recovers faster (once uncertainty resolves) but can be more acute. Fuel/cost-driven pressure persists as long as the cost structure remains elevated.

## (f) State-Level Data — Florida, Nevada, Hawaii

### Hawaii
- **Most severely impacted US state** — high dependence on air travel (no driving alternative), large Japanese visitor base
- Hawaii State Legislature declared tourism emergency in **March 1991** and allocated $6 million in emergency marketing funds for mainland US TV commercials
- Hawaii Visitors Bureau deployed full emergency campaign
- Japanese visitor arrivals compounded by subsequent Japan economic deterioration
- **Extended recovery: arrivals and airline seats decreased through 1994** (4-year disruption vs. ~18-month for continental US)
- Hawaii Tourism Authority historical data confirms the early-90s trough as one of the deepest in the state's modern tourism history

### Nevada / Las Vegas
- Las Vegas was in active expansion phase (MGM Grand, Mirage, Excalibur all 1989-1993 openings)
- Convention business hit hard — LVCVA historical data shows meeting attendance dropped
- Convention authority cited company travel bans as primary driver of business meeting cancellations
- Gaming revenue relatively resilient vs. lodging — local/regional drive market provided partial buffer
- Las Vegas occupancy dropped but less dramatically than coastal international markets due to high drive-market proportion
- Full historical LVCVA annual data from 1991 not accessible in current search, but regional pattern consistent: air-dependent visitors dropped sharply, drive-in visitors provided partial cushion

### Florida / Orlando
- Walt Disney World attendance: **-7%** in 1991 (cited by trade publications)
- Disneyland (California): **-10%** in 1991
- Sun-Sentinel (Orlando): "Orlando's theme parks have seen a drop in attendance with every major economic downturn, such as the 1991 recession and Persian Gulf War"
- Foreign visitors to Florida (particularly Europeans and Latin Americans) dropped sharply
- Miami/international airport hub traffic impacted by route consolidations (Delta absorbing Pan Am Caribbean routes added uncertainty)
- Florida's dual exposure: domestic recession + international travel fear + airline route disruptions
- Recovery faster than Hawaii due to large domestic drive market; European visitors returned by 1992

### Comparative State Recovery Speed
| State | Recovery Timeframe | Key Constraint |
|-------|-------------------|----------------|
| Hawaii | 1991–1994 | Air-only access + Japanese economy |
| Florida | 1991–1993 | International exposure offset by domestic |
| Nevada | 1991–1992 | Drive market buffer, convention lag |

## (g) Employment Sequencing — Hospitality Claims vs. Oil Peak

### Timeline of Labor Market Events

**August 1990:** Oil spikes. Airline industry begins Q4 earnings collapse. Hotel forward booking cancellations start accumulating.

**October 1990:** Oil at $46 peak. Airline Q4 losses accelerating. First hospitality layoffs begin in heavily air-dependent markets. Continental files bankruptcy December 1990.

**January 1991:** War begins. Acute demand collapse. Pan Am files bankruptcy. Eastern shuts down. Airlines begin emergency route cuts. Hotel check-ins drop sharply.

**January–February 1991:** Hospitality employment claims spike. The fear wave triggers immediate layoffs in:
- Airport concessions
- Downtown/urban hotels (business travel)
- Theme parks (forward booking cancellations)
- Airlines themselves (50,000 jobs lost in 1991)

**March–April 1991:** Convention industry layoffs lag by 60-90 days. Resort/vacation hotel segment follows as spring break and summer bookings prove weaker than expected.

**Full-Year 1991 Hospitality Employment:** Net job losses. Orange County lodging alone: ~1,450 workers (~7% of sector). Nationally, hotel values fell 14% with widespread foreclosures.

**Key Sequencing Observation:**
- Oil peak: **October 1990**
- War outbreak/fear peak: **January 1991** (+3 months)
- Acute hospitality claims spike: **January–March 1991** (coincides with fear, not fuel)
- Employment trough: approximately **Q1–Q2 1991**
- Recovery in employment: **Q3 1991** for leisure/recreation; lodging lagged into 1992

**Important nuance:** The BLS USLAH series (Leisure and Hospitality total employment) only goes back to January 1990 in readily accessible form, but the trajectory is clear: employment was tracking up through mid-1990, peaked approximately July 1990, then declined through early 1991. The decline was **not coincident with the oil price spike** — it lagged by ~2-3 months, consistent with the demand destruction working through forward bookings before hitting payrolls.

## Synthesis: The Two-Channel Model for Current Analog

### 1990-91 Template

```
CHANNEL 1: FUEL COST                    CHANNEL 2: FEAR/UNCERTAINTY
─────────────────────────────────────   ────────────────────────────────────
Trigger: Iraq invades Kuwait (Aug 2)    Trigger: War begins (Jan 16)
Peak: Oct 1990 ($46/bbl)               Peak: Jan–Feb 1991
Mechanism: Cost → Fare → Margin        Mechanism: Behavior → Demand
Who hurt first: Airlines (Q4 1990)     Who hurt first: International tourism
Elasticity: Price-sensitive leisure    Elasticity: Inelastic (even wealthy cancel)
Recovery: 9 months (prices normalize)  Recovery: 3-6 months after uncertainty resolves
```

### 1990-91 Analog Scorecard for Today

| Dimension | 1990-91 | Today's Tariff Shock |
|-----------|---------|---------------------|
| Fuel/cost channel | Jet fuel spike (+150%) | Price uncertainty + supply chain cost |
| Fear/uncertainty channel | Terrorism + war fear | Anti-US sentiment + travel advisories |
| Pre-existing weakness | Pan Am, Eastern already sick | Which carriers/hotels are pre-stressed? |
| Duration of uncertainty | ~9 months (clear endpoint: war ends) | Open-ended — no clear resolution date |
| Recovery speed | Fast once war ended | Uncertain |
| International inbound | -30% acute, recovered 1992 | Monitor Canadian/European cancellations |
| State differentials | Hawaii worst (air-dependent + single source market) | Similar exposure pattern likely |

### Critical Differences vs. 1990-91
1. **No clear endpoint:** In 1991, the war ended February 28 and fear resolved quickly. Current trade/political uncertainty has no scheduled resolution — this argues for a **longer recovery tail**
2. **Fuel not the driver (yet):** Current shock is demand/sentiment-driven (anti-US sentiment, travel advisories) rather than supply-cost driven — closer to the "fear wave" than the "fuel wave"
3. **Recession context:** 1990-91 recession was mild (GDP -1.5%, 8 months). Current macro uncertain
4. **Airline sector:** 2025 carriers are better capitalized than 1991; no Eastern/Pan Am equivalent obviously waiting to fail — though Spirit bankruptcy (2024) is a recent precedent
