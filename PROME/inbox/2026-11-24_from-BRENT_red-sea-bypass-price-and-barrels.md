## For Will, in plain words

**What oil did:** it shrugged. Brent rose 2.3% in Tuesday's day session (January settled about $91.40, secondhand, matching my own read), then gave back about $1.25 in the evening, and most of that fall came *before* the Hodeidah news. When the Reuters headline hit around 10:26 PM ET, US crude ticked up 20 cents for about 15 minutes and then slipped back. At 11:30 PM ET Brent January was about $90.15 and US crude January about $80.76, both down roughly 1.5% from Tuesday's settle. The shape of the futures curve did not change: the gap between this month's and next month's Brent stayed at about $2.6. A market that feared losing Saudi barrels soon would bid the nearest month up against later ones. It didn't. Diesel margins kept falling too ($71.6 a barrel at Tuesday's settle against $79.7 on 11/19), even though diesel is what Saudi Arabia's Red Sea refineries export most.

**How many barrels are at risk, and how sure we are:** the Red Sea side carries about 2.5 to 3 million barrels a day of Saudi crude out of Yanbu, roughly 40% of Saudi crude exports and about 2.5% of world supply. Up to about 1 million barrels a day of fuel from the west-coast refineries also leaves there, but that figure is from 2025 and summer 2026. A shutdown of the Red Sea outlet lasting days would put about 3 to 4 million barrels a day at risk. The tracking firms disagree with each other by up to 1.5 million barrels a day, so treat these as ranges. Jeddah, the port the Houthis named, is mainly a container, food and fuel-import port. A strike there would hurt Saudi imports and civilian supply, not crude exports. Yanbu is the oil port that matters.

**What is established tonight and what is not.** Established: the Saudi-led coalition says it hit Hodeidah, so the Houthis' condition for attacking Jeddah and the other Saudi Red Sea ports is now met. A drone hit a Riyadh office tower on Tuesday and wounded 11 people. Three ships were hit in Hormuz, but the maritime authority (UKMTO, warning 219-26) dates that attack to Sunday evening 11/22, so Monday's and Tuesday's prices already included it. **Not established: any hit on Saudi oil export facilities, and any halt in loadings at Yanbu.** After the first round of threats on 11/20, nothing was confirmed hit either. The ADNOC notice bars outside tankers from waiting or transferring cargo in Abu Dhabi's western waters for 90 days. It does not stop the UAE's own exports. It removes waiting and ship-to-ship space inside the Gulf; no volume is stated.

**What it means:** the danger lies in the combination, not in tonight's facts. The East-West pipeline exists so that Saudi crude can avoid Hormuz. If Yanbu is hit while ships are still being struck in Hormuz, the backup route and the main route are both under fire at the same moment, and there is almost no spare capacity anywhere else. EIA's November outlook shows OPEC spare capacity at about zero, because the spare sits behind Hormuz. The only real cushion is stockpiles: EIA has world inventories building about 1.5 million barrels a day this quarter. The market is pricing the threat as noise because every Red Sea threat this year has ended with interceptions and no lost barrels. That record is real, but it measures how good Saudi air defence has been. It does not show that the risk is small.

**What I expect next:** a quiet holiday week with Brent January (which expires Monday 11/30) mostly between $87 and $93. Over the next three days, a Houthi attempt at a Saudi Red Sea port is more likely than not. A *confirmed* hit that halts Yanbu loadings for a day or more is about a 5% chance in 72 hours and about 8 to 10% over two weeks (my view, unscored). If that happens, I expect Brent up $6 to $12 within a session or two, with the front of the curve jumping. The things to watch are an Aramco or Saudi ministry statement, Maersk or MSC suspending Jeddah calls, and Yanbu war-risk insurance quotes rising from about 3% of hull value. OPEC+ meets Sunday 11/29; with the spare stranded, any quota increase adds paper, not barrels.

**One finding for you, no action taken:** the energy stand-down (energy arm retired 8/7, the WQ-192 stand-down) still fits tonight's evidence. No barrels were lost and the market priced nothing. The bypass-plus-Hormuz scenario is exactly what the surviving frame-breaker (BG-02) was written for: a Saudi statement naming 0.7 million barrels a day or more offline. That route still exists and needs your word at every step. Tonight did not meet it.
