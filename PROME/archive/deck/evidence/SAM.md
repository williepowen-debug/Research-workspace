### #1 — Japan Is Selling America's Debt, and Nobody's Talking About It

**The Finding:**
Japan — the single largest foreign owner of US government bonds — is actively selling those bonds to bring money home, and the numbers are getting bigger every month.

**Why It Matters:**
The US government borrows trillions every year. Japan has been one of the most reliable buyers of that debt for decades, helping keep US interest rates lower than they'd otherwise be. When Japan sells instead of buys, someone else has to absorb that supply — and if they won't, interest rates rise. Higher US interest rates mean your mortgage gets more expensive, your company's borrowing costs go up, and stock valuations fall. Japan isn't just a foreign country doing foreign things; it's one of the pillars holding US borrowing costs down.

### #2 — The Carry Trade: A 30-Year Bet Is Coming Undone

**The Finding:**
For three decades, global investors borrowed money in Japan at near-zero interest rates, invested it in higher-yielding US and European assets, and pocketed the difference. Now that Japan is raising rates, that bet is reversing — simultaneously, globally, and fast.

**Why It Matters:**
Imagine millions of investors all doing the same trade: borrow cheap in Japan, invest somewhere else. That trade worked as long as Japanese rates stayed near zero. The moment Japanese rates rise — even slightly — the math breaks. The borrowed yen becomes more expensive to carry. Investors sell their US stocks and bonds to pay back their yen loans. This doesn't happen slowly and individually; it happens in a rush, all at once, because everyone is in the same trade. The August 2024 preview showed what this looks like: the yen moved 3%, and the VIX (Wall Street's fear gauge) hit 65 within hours. Nikkei fell 12% in a single day. Your 401k felt it.

**The Source:**
BOJ policy record; August 2024 market precedent; SAM carry unwind probability models

**Strength:** 🔴 Smoking Gun

**Quote-ready slide sentence:**
> "The carry trade is the world's biggest crowded trade. When it unwinds, it doesn't walk out the door — it runs. We saw it in August 2024. The conditions today are more extreme."

### #3 — USD/JPY at 159.50: Japan's Government Drew a Line in the Sand Here Before

**The Finding:**
In January 2026, Japan's Ministry of Finance conducted "rate checks" — the formal precursor to currency intervention — at exactly USD/JPY 159.50. On March 13, 2026, the exchange rate hit that exact level again.

**Why It Matters:**
When the yen gets too weak, Japan's government intervenes by selling US Treasuries from its reserves and buying yen. This is called FX intervention. It matters for two reasons: First, intervention means Japan is selling US Treasuries into an already-stressed market, accelerating the "demand hole" for US debt. Second, a sudden sharp yen strengthening — which intervention causes — can trigger the carry trade unwind immediately, because all those investors who borrowed in yen suddenly have a much more expensive loan. The 159.50 level is the lit fuse.

**The Source:**
MoF rate check records (January 2026); USD/JPY market data (March 13, 2026); Finance Minister Katayama verbal statements

**Strength:** 🔴 Smoking Gun

### #6 — Regulatory Change Is Forcing Japan's Insurers to Sell Foreign Bonds Permanently

**The Finding:**
Japan changed its insurance solvency rules in April 2025 (J-ICS), requiring life insurers to value their assets and liabilities at market prices. This makes foreign bonds — especially unhedged US Treasuries — structurally unattractive to hold, regardless of what markets are doing.

**Why It Matters:**
This is the most important "this isn't going to stop" piece of evidence. Life insurer selling isn't tactical; it's regulatory. Japan's big four life insurers (Nippon Life, Dai-ichi, Sumitomo Life, Meiji Yasuda) collectively hold roughly $450 billion in US Treasuries. They are now structurally incentivized to shrink that position every year. The J-ICS rules force duration matching: your liabilities (life insurance payouts) are in yen and long-dated, so your assets should be too. A US Treasury bond in a Japanese insurer's portfolio creates a regulatory drag. The selling is not a choice; it's math.

**The Source:**

## GLOSSARY

**Carry Trade**
A carry trade is when you borrow money in a country with low interest rates, convert it to a currency with higher interest rates, and invest it there — pocketing the difference. For 30 years, Japan's interest rates were near zero, making the yen the world's favorite borrowing currency. Investors borrowed trillions in yen, bought US stocks, Australian bonds, and emerging market assets, and earned the spread. The problem: when Japan raises rates, the cost of that borrowed yen goes up, and everyone tries to exit the trade at the same time. When everyone runs for the exit simultaneously, markets crash.

**Intervention**
Currency intervention is when a government buys or sells its own currency to influence the exchange rate. Japan's Ministry of Finance intervenes to prevent the yen from falling too much (which raises import costs) or rising too fast (which hurts exporters). To strengthen the yen, the MoF sells US dollars — which means selling US Treasuries from its reserves. This is relevant because intervention simultaneously creates a yen shock (triggering carry unwind) AND reduces demand for US Treasuries (raising US borrowing costs). A "rate check" is the warning shot before actual intervention: the MoF calls banks and asks what the current USD/JPY rate is, signaling that it's watching.

**Repatriation**
Repatriation is when money that was invested abroad comes home. Japanese insurance companies, pension funds, and banks have invested hundreds of billions of dollars overseas — mostly in US Treasuries and bonds — because foreign yields were higher than Japanese yields. Now that Japanese yields are rising and regulatory rules have changed, it makes more sense to hold domestic bonds. Repatriation means selling those foreign bonds, converting the proceeds back to yen, and reinvesting in Japan. The selling of foreign bonds is the mechanism that reduces demand for US Treasuries and puts upward pressure on US interest rates.

**BOJ (Bank of Japan)**
The Bank of Japan is Japan's central bank — the equivalent of the US Federal Reserve. It sets Japan's interest rates and manages monetary policy. For most of the past three decades, the BOJ kept rates at or below zero, making Japan the world's cheapest source of borrowed money. Starting in 2024, the BOJ began raising rates for the first time in 17 years. This shift — even though rates are still low by US standards (currently 0.75%) — has enormous global consequences because it changes the economics of the carry trade. Every BOJ meeting is now a potential catalyst.

**Fiscal Year-End**
Japan's fiscal year runs April 1 through March 31 — one month offset from the calendar year. Japanese companies, banks, and insurance firms close their books on March 31 and need to report their financial positions. In the weeks before March 31, institutions engage in heavy "window dressing" — selling losing positions, locking in gains, and rebalancing portfolios to show a clean balance sheet. This creates predictable seasonal selling pressure in foreign bonds, as institutions repatriate capital to hit domestic targets. The last two weeks of March are historically the most active repatriation window of the year.
