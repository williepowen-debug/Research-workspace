# The Stealth Exit
*Deep Dive | 18min | 2026-02-12*

---

China is selling roughly $100 billion per quarter of U.S. Treasury bonds.

And they're hiding it in Belgium.

This isn't conspiracy. This is mechanics. And it's the second major shock vector hitting the Treasury market — after Japan. Together, they're creating a **$300 billion annual demand hole** that no one is talking about.

Let me walk you through what's happening, why it matters, and why the system that absorbed similar selling in 2015-2016 no longer exists.

---

## The Belgium Puzzle

Here's the data that should make you pause.

Belgium — a country with a GDP of about $600 billion — currently holds **$481 billion** in U.S. Treasury securities. That's up **33% year-over-year**. In the last 12 months alone, Belgium's reported Treasury holdings increased by roughly $120 billion.

Does that make sense to you? Belgium's entire GDP is $600 billion, and they're holding $481 billion in U.S. Treasuries?

No. Because Belgium isn't the buyer.

Belgium is the **custodian**.

---

## How Custody Works

Here's what's actually happening.

**Euroclear** — one of the world's largest securities settlement systems — is based in Brussels, Belgium. Over 100 central banks use Euroclear to hold and settle their bond portfolios.

When a foreign government or central bank doesn't want to hold U.S. Treasuries directly in their own name — maybe for political reasons, maybe for operational flexibility — they can use a custodian like Euroclear.

Those holdings show up in the U.S. Treasury's TIC data — the Treasury International Capital report — as "Belgium."

So when you see Belgium's holdings surge by $120 billion in a year, you're not seeing Belgium buying. You're seeing someone **parking Treasuries in Brussels**.

The question is: who? And why?

---

## The China Connection

Let's look at another data point.

China's **official** U.S. Treasury holdings, according to TIC data, are currently $688 billion. That's down from a peak of $1.06 trillion in 2021. A decline of $372 billion.

But here's the thing: China's **true** exposure to U.S. Treasuries is much larger than $688 billion. Estimates put it between **$2.5 trillion and $2.8 trillion** when you include:
- Holdings via offshore custodians (like Belgium/Euroclear)
- U.S. agency bonds (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac)
- Chinese state bank portfolios
- Shadow banking system net foreign assets

So when China's official TIC number goes **down** by $372 billion, and Belgium's number goes **up** by $180 billion, what you're seeing is:
1. Actual selling (net outflow of $192 billion)
2. **Plus** custody migration (moving holdings offshore to obscure the true picture)

And here's the confirmation: On **February 9th, 2026**, Bloomberg reported that Chinese financial regulators issued **window guidance** to the Big Four state banks. The message: **Stop adding to U.S. Treasury positions. Gradually reduce where excessive.**

That's not rotation. That's **strategic withdrawal**.

---

## The $300 Billion Demand Hole

Now let's zoom out.

China isn't the only official-sector seller. There are three major sources of Treasury demand that are disappearing:

**1. Japan: ~$150 billion per year**

Japanese life insurance companies are selling U.S. Treasuries at a rate of $10-15 billion per month. Why? Because Japanese government bond yields have risen dramatically. The 30-year JGB now yields 3.57% — up from below 1% for most of the last decade.

For a Japanese insurer, buying a 30-year JGB at 3.6% and avoiding currency risk is more attractive than buying a U.S. Treasury at 4.5% and taking yen exposure — especially when the yen is at 156 to the dollar and intervention risk is high.

Total annual selling from Japan: roughly **$150 billion**.

**2. China: ~$100 billion per year**

We just covered this. Window guidance to banks. Custody migration to Belgium. Strategic withdrawal ahead of potential tariff escalation or secondary sanctions risk.

Estimated annual selling: **$100 billion**.

**3. Saudi Arabia: ~$50 billion per year**

Saudi Arabia used to be a reliable buyer of U.S. Treasuries. Petrodollars came in, Treasuries went out. But Vision 2030 — Saudi Arabia's economic diversification plan — is running deficits. They're spending down reserves, not accumulating them.

They're no longer a **marginal buyer**. Estimated reduction in annual demand: **$50 billion**.

**Total: ~$300 billion per year** in official-sector demand that's disappearing.

---

## Why This Is Different from 2015-2016

You might be thinking: didn't China sell a bunch of Treasuries back in 2015-2016? And the market absorbed it fine.

Yes. China sold roughly **$500 billion** between 2015 and 2017. And you're right — the market absorbed it.

But here's what was different then: **the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan were doing massive quantitative easing**.

In 2015-2016, the ECB was buying €60-80 billion per month in European bonds. The BOJ was buying ¥80 trillion per year in Japanese bonds. That pushed European and Japanese investors into U.S. Treasuries searching for yield.

So when China sold $500 billion, European and Japanese private investors bought it. The selling was offset by QE-driven demand from other regions.

**In 2026, that safety net doesn't exist.**

The Federal Reserve is doing **quantitative tightening** — shrinking its balance sheet, not expanding it. The ECB has ended QE. The BOJ is trying to **normalize** policy, not ease further.

There is no offsetting demand from official-sector QE programs. The $300 billion demand hole has to be filled by **private buyers**.

And private buyers are **price-sensitive**. They require higher yields to step in.

---

## Term Premium: The Price Adjustment

Let me explain what "term premium" means, because this is the mechanism that's going to drive yields higher.

When you buy a 10-year Treasury bond, the yield you demand has two components:

1. **Expectations component:** What do you expect short-term interest rates to average over the next 10 years?
2. **Term premium:** The extra yield you demand for taking the risk of locking up your money for 10 years instead of rolling over short-term bills.

For most of the last decade, term premium has been **negative** or near-zero. Why? Because foreign official buyers — central banks and sovereign wealth funds — didn't care about price. They were buying for reserves, for diversification, for political reasons. They were **price-insensitive**.

When you have $2-3 trillion in price-insensitive buying, it suppresses term premium. You don't need to pay investors extra to hold long-term bonds, because central banks will buy them anyway.

But when that official-sector demand **disappears** — when Japan, China, and Saudi Arabia are no longer net buyers — you need to attract **private buyers**. And private buyers are price-sensitive.

Academic estimates suggest that a **$200 billion reduction** in foreign official demand requires term premium to rise by **30 to 100 basis points** to attract replacement buyers.

We're talking about a **$300 billion reduction**. That suggests term premium needs to rise by roughly **90 to 210 basis points**.

Currently, term premium (using the New York Fed's ACM model) is at **0.80%**. The historical average is **2.16%**. If term premium normalizes toward 1.5-2.0%, that's an additional **70 to 120 basis points** on top of wherever the Fed has rates.

That's the adjustment the market is going to have to make.

---

## Who Replaces the Foreign Official Buyers?

So if Japan, China, and Saudi Arabia aren't buying... who is?

**1. Hedge funds running the basis trade**

The basis trade is when a hedge fund buys Treasury bonds and shorts Treasury futures, capturing the price difference (the "basis"). It's a low-risk, low-return arbitrage — but you can juice returns by using massive leverage.

Current basis trade exposure: **$1.85 trillion**, funded primarily through money market repos at leverage ratios of **18-to-1**.

Here's the problem: this is **reflexive**. If Treasury yields spike, hedge funds get margin calls, they have to unwind positions (sell Treasuries), which pushes yields higher, which triggers more margin calls. It's a doom loop.

The basis trade works when the market is stable. It **amplifies** when the market is unstable.

**2. Domestic banks (with regulatory relief)**

If the Fed or regulators provide **Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) relief**, big banks could expand their balance sheets to buy more Treasuries without hitting capital constraints.

This would add roughly **$34.5 billion in capacity per G-SIB** (globally systemically important bank).

But this is **regulatory capture**. The government would be changing the rules to force banks to absorb the debt that foreign buyers don't want. And it's not enough — we're talking about $150-200 billion in total capacity, not $300 billion per year.

**3. Stablecoins and money market funds**

Pending legislation like the GENIUS Act could allow stablecoins to back their reserves with Treasuries. That creates new structural demand.

But this primarily targets **short-term** Treasuries (bills), not the long-term bonds that Japan and China are selling.

**Bottom line:** The replacement buyers are either **leveraged** (hedge funds) or **limited in scale** (banks, stablecoins). Neither is a stable substitute for $300 billion in price-insensitive official-sector demand.

## The April Catalyst

Why is this accelerating now?

Because there's a **catalyst** on the calendar: the Trump-Xi summit in Beijing, scheduled for **April 2026**.

Think about the logic from China's perspective:

If you're the People's Bank of China, and you think there's a risk of:
- 50%+ tariffs with no carve-outs
- Secondary sanctions (limiting access to SWIFT or dollar clearing)
- Asset freezes (Russia-style response to a Taiwan escalation)

...then you want to **reduce your exposure** before that risk materializes.

The window guidance issued on February 9th — two months before the summit — fits this timeline perfectly.

China isn't panicking. They're **pre-positioning**. Selling gradually, using Belgium as a custodial buffer to obscure the flow, and timing it ahead of a potential inflection point in U.S.-China relations.

April is the two-month countdown.

- **China official holdings <$650 billion** = Accelerating exit
- **Combined China + Belgium quarterly flow** = If it's running at -$100 billion per quarter or more, the stealth exit is confirmed

**Quarterly: BIS International Banking Statistics**

The Bank for International Settlements publishes cross-border banking claims data, including custodial holdings.

This will tell us: Is the Belgium surge **custody migration** (money just moved) or **actual exit** (money left the system)?

If Belgium TIC goes up but BIS custodial claims stay flat, that's **Scenario B** — actual exit, not just reshuffling.

**Weekly: Auction Results**

Every week, the Treasury auctions new bonds. We're watching two metrics:
1. **Bid-to-cover ratio** — How much demand vs. supply? Below 2.3x is stress.
2. **Indirect bidder percentage** — This is the foreign buyer share. If it falls below 62-65% sustained, foreign demand is softening.

The February 11th 10-year auction: **2.39x bid-to-cover** (down from 2.55x), **64.5% indirect** (down from 69.6%). Not crisis territory, but softening.

## The Domestic Plumbing Problem

Now let's connect this to the domestic side.

The U.S. funding market has **two missing safety systems** right now:

**1. The Reverse Repo Facility (RRP) is at zero**

The RRP peaked at $2.5 trillion in late 2022. It was the system's **shock absorber** — when funding stress hit, cash could flow out of the RRP into markets.

Today, the RRP is at **$1.3 billion**. Effectively zero.

That means when the Treasury auctions new debt, or when tax season drains reserves, there's no buffer. The stress transmits **1-to-1** into the banking system.

**2. Dealer balance sheets are clogged**

Big banks that act as Treasury dealers are holding roughly **$200 billion net long** in Treasuries. The Supplementary Leverage Ratio (SLR) prevents them from expanding their balance sheets further.

Even if spreads are profitable, dealers can't intermediate more supply. The market has **zero elasticity**.

So you have:
- **Foreign official buyers disappearing** ($300 billion per year)
- **No RRP buffer** to absorb shocks
- **Dealers stuffed** and unable to expand

And the replacement buyers are **leveraged hedge funds** running $1.85 trillion in basis trades at 18-to-1.

This is a **dual-fragility regime**: structural withdrawal on the demand side, zero cushion on the plumbing side.

## What Happens Next

Here's the most likely path:

**Phase 1 (Current): Gradual selling, rising term premium**

Japan and China sell $20-30 billion per month. Term premium rises slowly from 0.80% toward 1.2-1.5%. The 10-year Treasury yield drifts higher, but not catastrophically. Markets adjust.

**Phase 2 (If April summit fails): Acceleration**

If the Trump-Xi summit produces no tariff de-escalation, China accelerates. Belgium holdings breach $500 billion. Combined China+Belgium flow hits -$50 billion per quarter or more.

Term premium spikes toward 1.5-2.0%. The 10-year yield breaks above 5.0%. Regional banks with held-to-maturity Treasury portfolios face mark-to-market stress. Credit spreads widen.

**Phase 3 (Tail risk): Forced liquidation**

If there's a geopolitical shock — secondary sanctions, Taiwan escalation, asset freeze threats — China liquidates aggressively. $200-300 billion exits over 3-6 months.

Basis trade hedge funds get margin calls. Forced selling into a falling market. Treasury yields spike 100+ basis points in weeks. The Fed has to intervene with yield curve control or emergency QE.

Probability: 10-15%. But the consequences are severe enough that it's worth tracking.

## The Counter-Argument

Let me steelman the other side.

**Argument 1:** "China has been selling for years, and the market absorbed it fine."

Response: Yes, but **2015-2016 had ECB/BOJ QE**. That safety net is gone. And the scale is larger now — $300 billion annually vs. $150-200 billion then.

**Argument 2:** "Domestic buyers will step in if yields rise enough."

Response: Yes, **if yields rise enough**. That's the point. The question isn't whether the market clears. It's what **price** it clears at. If term premium has to rise 90-210 basis points to attract private buyers, that's a **structural repricing** of U.S. debt.

**Argument 3:** "China won't dump Treasuries because it would hurt their own holdings."

Response: True in a **panic scenario**. But in a **strategic withdrawal**, they sell gradually to minimize price impact. That's exactly what we're seeing — Belgium custody migration, verbal window guidance, pre-positioning ahead of April.

They're not dumping. They're exiting. There's a difference.

## The Bottom Line

China is selling roughly $100 billion per quarter of U.S. Treasury bonds, and they're hiding it in Belgium. Japan is selling $10-15 billion per month. Saudi Arabia has stopped being a marginal buyer.

Together, that's a **$300 billion annual demand hole**.

In 2015-2016, when China sold, the ECB and BOJ were doing QE. European and Japanese private buyers filled the gap.

In 2026, there's no safety net. The Fed is doing QT. The RRP buffer is gone. Dealer balance sheets are clogged. The replacement buyers are leveraged hedge funds at 18-to-1.

The system is transitioning from **official-sector stability** — where central banks bought regardless of price — to **private-sector fragility** — where hedge funds buy only if the price is right, and sell fast if it's not.

That adjustment happens through **term premium**. It's not a crisis. It's a repricing. But repricing from 0.80% to 1.5-2.0% means the 10-year Treasury yield rises by 70 to 120 basis points **on top of wherever the Fed has rates**.

And we have a countdown: April 2026. Two months.

The stealth exit is real. The question is whether it stays gradual, or accelerates.
